CHAPTER TWO

On that very day, Wang Youling arrived at North Tongzhou. Leaving Hangzhou, he had taken a black-canopied boat to Suzhou and then boarded a grain boat heading north. Unfortunately, his journey coincided with the breach north of Fengxian. He had to leave the water and travel by cart, yet he was unwilling to spend more than necessary. Along the way, he asked innkeepers to find carts or boats on which he could share a ride. This saved considerable expense but wasted so much time that nearly half a year passed before he reached North Tongzhou.

The town was a major junction for travel by land and water. The granary vice-minister was stationed there, and countless local people earned their living from the grain boats and warehouses. It was now the busy season, when southern fleets converged and their rice was brought into storage. The transport associations had all sorts of official and private dealings with the registered grain-handling operators, while the boatmen's private cargoes also had to be unloaded for sale. Teahouses, taverns, inns, and bathhouses were crowded everywhere. Wang hired a porter to carry his two bundles of luggage on a shoulder pole and tried several inns, but could find nowhere to stay. At last he came to the Xingfa Inn outside the west gate. There were relatively few people, carts, or horses loitering at its entrance. This one ought to do, he thought. Repeated refusals had taught him something: a polite request at the counter was all too easily turned down. Better to put on an official air and intimidate the tradesmen.

He straightened his riding jacket, took a large pair of dark smoky-quartz spectacles from his breast, put them on, and strode inside. An attendant hurried to meet him. Before the man could speak, Wang issued an imposing command: “Find me a quiet room.” Smiling respectfully, the attendant first asked, “May I have your honourable surname, sir?” “Wang.” “Ah. You have come from the south, I presume?” “Mm,” Wang replied. “I am going to the capital on business at the Board of Civil Appointments.” The attendant had seen plenty of officials awaiting appointment and had recognised the type at a glance. Now that Wang had confirmed it, he changed his form of address. “Your Honour Wang!” Then, hesitating, he added, “We do still have two rooms, but I am afraid I cannot let you have either.”

“Why not?” “The department magistrate's office has sent someone to reserve them. An imperial commissioner is expected in a day or two, with a large entourage. Every vacant room in the inns outside the west gate has been booked. I am very sorry, Your Honour, but perhaps you could try elsewhere.” He made another respectful salute, repeating, “Please forgive us, Your Honour.” The man's manner left Wang no grounds for an unreasonable demand. He could only return to persuasion. “I've already tried several places. You really must help me find a room. I'll stay just one night and leave first thing tomorrow.” A stay of one night was easier to accommodate. The attendant agreed to consult the proprietor.

The guests innkeepers dreaded most were the military officers from the grain boats. Their rank was low, but their airs were grand. They shouted abuse at the slightest provocation, struck attendants who answered back, and made trouble whenever the service fell short. As mere captains and lieutenants, they were not even senior enough to serve as personal attendants to a governor-general or governor. They dared behave so outrageously because their associations stood behind them. The grain-transport men also had a secret organisation, so closely guarded that outsiders could learn nothing of its inner workings. All anyone knew was that they were fiercely united. At a shout of “Fight!” every fist would rise, and they would smash the inn to pieces before anything else. If a lawsuit followed, so be it: men and money could be produced at a word. No inn could possibly stand against them. In such circumstances, invoking a government reservation was the simplest way to avoid endless trouble.

Wang was different. He too gave himself official airs, but he was courteous and cultivated, and did not look unreasonable. Nor did he appear to be one of those hardened travellers who knew every trick and were difficult to manage. The proprietor therefore agreed to take him in, subject to one condition. “Your Honour Wang,” the attendant said, “there is something we must make clear beforehand. We hear that the commissioner has already left the capital. We cannot be sure whether he will arrive tonight or tomorrow morning. If he comes tonight, we shall have to ask you to put up with some inconvenience. Of course, we won't leave you with nowhere to sleep. But... well, in that case, you would have to squeeze onto the big heated sleeping platform with the rest of us.” “Certainly, certainly!” Exhausted by his journey, Wang was entirely satisfied and readily agreed. “As long as there is somewhere to sleep, that will do.”

The attendant found him a single room in the western side courtyard. Wang paid the porter and had his luggage brought in. The attendant, whose name was Liu Si, supplied tea and water, then chatted with him about his journey as he unwrapped his bedding. Once Wang had washed and settled down with his tea, Liu brought an oil lamp and asked what he would like for supper. Reaching Tongzhou felt almost like reaching the capital itself, and Wang was in a relaxed mood. He ordered two dishes and a pot of clear grain liquor, which he drank slowly. Pleasantly tipsy, he was recalling his days with Hu Xueyan when the door curtain lifted and someone asked, “Would Your Honour care to hear a song?” The voice was clear and lively. Looking up, Wang saw a woman of about thirty, her face heavily powdered and her hair arranged in a high “magpie's tail,” jingling with silver pins and little gold bells. She wore a green jacket, black trousers, and embroidered red shoes scarcely larger than rice dumplings. On closer inspection, her complexion was rather dark, but every movement of her eyes seemed to overflow with charm. Half drunk and looking at her by lamplight, Wang thought her a perfect beauty.

He had encountered this kind of business several times on his journey north. Beckoning, he said, “Come here.” The woman smiled, waved to the older woman behind her, and entered alone. After a respectful greeting, she asked, “May I know your surname, sir?” “Wang. And yours?” “They call me Jincui.” “Jincui. Mm, yes.” He examined her once more from head to foot and nodded his approval. “Are you here alone, Your Honour?” “Yes, alone.” Wang added, “Go out for now. I'll ask Liu Si to call you later.” Jincui gave him another inviting glance. In a slightly cloying voice, she said, “Thank you, Your Honour. Do remember me. Be sure to have Liu Si call me.”

“I won't forget.” She lifted the curtain and left. Wang continued drinking, now pouring less and sipping more slowly than ever. He drank and waited, but Liu Si failed to appear. Instead, other wandering prostitutes came to solicit him. Having already made his choice, Wang scarcely looked at them and soon grew irritated. Finally, he stepped outside himself and called loudly, “Liu Si! Liu Si!” Liu was still in the front courtyard. At the sound of Wang's voice, he hurried over, assuming that his guest was impatient for food. He began by apologising: there were so many travellers that day that the kitchen could not keep up. He suggested another four liang of liquor. “Take your time and enjoy a drink, Your Honour,” he said with a conspiratorial smile. “I'll find you some entertainment later.” “What sort of entertainment?” Wang asked, though he knew perfectly well. “It's still early. There's no hurry. If nobody comes after the second watch, this room will be yours for the night. I'll bring someone you're certain to like.” Liu added, “The woman I have in mind is the very best in her trade. Her name is Jincui.”

Wang smiled. “Bring more liquor!” he ordered loudly. He drank until the second watch and ate two flatbreads. Liu cleared away the remains and brewed another pot of tea. Then the curtain hook rattled, and Jincui appeared without further summons. “Look after him well,” Liu told her before withdrawing and closing the door behind him. Jincui poured a bowl of tea, untied the pink handkerchief fastened to her jacket, and wiped a tea stain from the rim before presenting it to Wang with both hands. For a northern woman of her calling, her manner was gentle and quiet. Wang liked her all the more. Taking her hand, he asked, “How old are you?”

She smiled with a hint of embarrassment. “Why do you ask?” “Is it something I shouldn't ask?” “Not exactly,” she replied. “If I tell you the truth, I'm afraid you won't want me. But if I don't tell the truth, I'd be deceiving you, and I don't want to do that.” “Why should I object?” Wang said earnestly. “I don't mind.” Her lively eyes travelled over his face; then she lowered her head and let her eyelids fall, her long lashes trembling. This seemingly tender gesture was ample consolation for the hardships of his journey. Wang decided to stay another day.

After a night of intimacy, added to the fatigue of travelling, he slept deeply and comfortably. He woke once, drew a silver-cased watch from beneath his pillow, and saw that it was nearly noon. It was late, but since he intended to spend another evening with Jincui, there was no reason to hurry. He turned over, pulled the covers over his head, and went back to sleep. Before long, an argument outside the window woke him. He recognised Liu Si's voice, apologising humbly. Liu admitted that he had known the room was reserved and should not have let it to another guest. “But His Honour Wang had already tried several inns without success, and he looked unwell. Surely travellers ought to help one another. Please don't be angry, sir. Sit down for a moment and have some tea. I'll clear the room for you at once.” So he had occupied someone else's room. Wang could not let Liu Si get into trouble over that. He hurriedly sat up, put on his clothes, and got out of bed. As he lifted the latch, he called out, “Liu Si, there's no need to argue with the guest. I'll give up the room.”

When he opened the door, he saw a man of about fifty standing beside Liu in the courtyard. The newcomer wore a sheepskin robe with a brand-new grey cloth covering and a small cap, but on his feet were a pair of sturdy, close-fitting boots known as “ground-gripping tigers.” At first glance, Wang could not place him. “I'm very sorry, Your Honour!” Liu said, indicating the man. “This is Second Master Yang, who serves the imperial commissioner. Your room was assigned to him. I'll find you somewhere else. Please bear with us and pack your things.” “Ah!” Wang nodded to the man in greeting. “Then the room is rightfully yours. Come in and sit down.” “There's no hurry, no hurry,” Yang replied, now equally polite. “Take your time, Your Honour.”

He spoke with a Yunnan accent. Its strong guttural sounds gave an impression of straightforward sincerity. Wang had lived in Yunnan as a boy, so the accent sounded especially familiar and warm to him. Smiling, he asked, “Where's your home? Kunming?” He too spoke in the Yunnan dialect. His pronunciation was not entirely accurate, but he caught its rhythm. Yang was delighted, as though he had met an old acquaintance far from home. “Your Honour, are you from Yunnan too?” “I was born there. I suppose that makes us fellow provincials of a sort.” “That's wonderful!” Yang exclaimed. “Then don't trouble yourself, Your Honour. You can stay right here.” “I couldn't possibly impose on you. Come, do sit down.” “Thank you,” Yang answered sincerely. “To speak frankly, as one of our own people, I still have some business to attend to. I'll find another room while I'm about it. When I'm done, I'll come back and we can talk about home. It won't take long—less than a couple of hours.” “Very good. I'll wait for you.”

They exchanged repeated bows with clasped hands and promises to meet again. Wang went back inside and sat down. Once he had collected his thoughts, he felt that the encounter was a piece of excellent fortune. Apart from the warmth of human kindness on his travels, he saw another possibility. An imperial commissioner's attendant would naturally know his way around the capital. Wang had been worrying about how to manage his business at the Board of Civil Appointments, where he knew neither the procedures nor the people. Now someone had appeared who could guide him. What could be better? The thought revived his spirits. He had just risen to call for service when Liu Si arrived with a younger attendant, carrying hot water for washing and fresh tea. “Your Honour is certainly in luck,” Liu said cheerfully. “Everything is sure to go well on this visit to the capital.” “Very kind of you!” Wang was delighted. “Liu Si, Second Master Yang is coming back to see me, and I'd like him to stay for a meal. Arrange something for us—not too elaborate, but nothing shabby either.” “I understand. Leave it to me, sir. I guarantee it will be good to eat and easy on your purse.”

Before two hours had passed, Yang returned as promised, carrying a parcel. Wang caught sight of him through the window and was reminded of what courtesy required. He hurriedly opened his trunk, took out a few local specialities, and laid them on the table before lifting the curtain to welcome his visitor. “Have you finished your official business?” he asked. “Yes,” Yang replied. “I've left them to carry out the arrangements.” Once seated inside, they formally exchanged names. The visitor was Yang Chengfu. Wang addressed him as “Brother Yang,” which pleased him greatly. He then began opening the parcel he had brought. Wang quickly forestalled him by bringing out his own gifts: a boxed pair of folding fans with polished bamboo ribs, made by Hangzhou's celebrated Shulianji, and a large packet of cut tobacco from Mi Dachang, a firm known even to the women in the inner quarters of northern official households. “A modest token for our first meeting, Brother Yang,” Wang said with a smile. “Though I suppose fans are not quite the thing to give in winter.”

“My dear fellow!” Yang caught his hand before he could put the gifts down. “Listen to me for a moment. I want to speak frankly, as one brother to another, and you mustn't take offence.” “How could I? Say whatever you wish, Brother Yang.” “I know these local gifts of yours. They belong to what people call the ‘Four Hangzhou Specialities.’ But they would be wasted on me. I prepare water-pipe tobacco for my master, but I smoke an ordinary pipe myself. And when have you ever seen a man like me waving a folding fan and pretending to be a grand gentleman? Keep them to give to someone in the capital. They will earn you goodwill there. There's something else...” Yang seemed reluctant to say it, but after a pause he continued. “Once my master and I reach the south, there'll be no shortage of these things. My dear fellow, one has to think ahead. You're coming north, where southern goods make useful presents, yet you would give them to me when I'm heading south. What sort of reckoning is that?”

There was something of an elder brother's guidance in his words. Further ceremony would only make Wang seem distant. “Put that way,” he said, “you make my politeness sound rather empty!” “There's no need for ceremony between us.” As he spoke, Yang finished unwrapping his parcel. Although he would not accept Wang's presents, he had a persuasive case for offering his own. He had brought household medicines: Zijin tablets, Zhuge's Campaign Powder, and a golden preparation shaped like mouse droppings and named accordingly. These were no ordinary remedies. They were specially prepared in the Imperial Dispensary from costly ingredients and could not be bought on the open market. Eunuchs seeking gifts from his master had supplied them in considerable quantities. He had simply wrapped up a few to bring over: a kindness that cost him nothing, useful to have even if never needed. When Wang returned south, they would make excellent presents. The thoughtfulness and sincerity of this explanation moved Wang. When Liu brought four cold dishes and a hotpot, Yang readily accepted his hospitality. The new acquaintances drank together and exchanged accounts of their travels. Although Wang felt as if he had known his guest for years, they had only just met. To ask for help now would be to presume too much on a new acquaintance. He therefore said little about his family, confining himself to the fact that he had come north to purchase an upgraded qualification for a local magistracy.

On learning that Wang had purchased qualification as a salt-station superintendent—and was thus an official, however minor—Yang became conscious of his own inferior position. Slightly embarrassed, he said, “Then I have been far too familiar.” “How so?” “To tell you the truth, I'm only a servant. How can I address you as a brother?” “Nonsense!” Wang replied. “I have no patience with such worldly distinctions.”

Yang held his cup and pondered. He seemed unsure how to behave, though perhaps he was also turning over some other idea. After a long pause, he abruptly put the cup down. “In that case, let me suggest something. First, how much money have you brought?” The question was unexpected. Wang remembered the traveller's maxim: “Tell a stranger only a little; never lay your whole heart bare.” He hesitated, then reproached himself. The other man had been so sincere. Why should he respond with petty suspicion? He answered honestly, “Less than five hundred taels.” Yang nodded. “That should just about cover an upgraded qualification for a magistracy. But if you want a good appointment, you'll need another approach.” “I was hoping I might rely on your help, Brother Yang.” “You are too kind.” Yang returned to the subject. “There are endless intricacies in the purchase of official qualifications. How could an outsider understand them all? The clerks in the Board's Appointments Department would eat you alive and leave no bones. Have you made any arrangements?”

“Before leaving Hangzhou, I consulted people who knew the system. I thought I might also pay for the provision giving me priority within my category, so I could obtain a post sooner.” “That provision is expensive.” Indeed, many expectant magistrates waited on official summonses until they had eaten through their resources and pawned everything, growing white-haired without ever acting in a substantive post. Wang proposed to upgrade from a principal eighth-rank salt-station superintendent to a principal seventh-rank county magistrate and then claim first consideration for any vacancy upon reaching his assigned province. Such a convenient arrangement naturally carried a high price. Yang offered another calculation. “There's no need to do that. You must understand that the first thing in official life is to find a patron. Even if you pay every fee at the Board and secure priority within your category, once you reach the province, vacancies will still pass you by unless someone speaks for you.”

“Really?” Wang was surprised. “Can the provincial treasurer simply ignore the Board's regulations?” “Regulations are one thing; practice is another. The treasurer can always find a reason to exclude you. Suppose a county magistracy falls vacant. He can say that the county has a distinguished literary tradition and requires an examination graduate with exceptional learning. That rules out everyone who bought a qualification. He can just as easily argue the reverse: the county is strategically important and its affairs are complicated, so it needs an able administrator experienced in government business. The implication is that examination graduates tend to be bookworms. Think about it. Isn't that how it works?” Wang considered the argument carefully and suddenly understood. Raising his cup in a cheerful toast, he said, “A conversation with you is worth ten years of reading.”

“So I advise you not to pay for priority within your category. You can obtain a good post without it.” Could there really be such an opportunity? Wang rose and bowed deeply. “Brother Yang, my whole future rests with you. If I make the slightest progress, I shall never forget it.” “You are too kind, too kind!” Yang jumped up and caught his hand. “Please sit down and let me explain.” Yang's advice was that, instead of paying heavily for priority within his category, Wang should spend less and purchase the right to choose his province of assignment. Ordinarily, expectant local magistrates drew lots, leaving their destination to chance. A designated provincial assignment allowed them to seek favourable circumstances and avoid unfavourable ones. Yang wanted Wang to specify Jiangsu when making his application. “My master is Jiangsu's education commissioner. His standing is equal to that of the governor of Jiangsu and the garrison general at Jiangning; even the governor-general of Liangjiang must show him consideration. Once you're assigned to Jiangsu, I'll speak to him for you. A word from him to the governor or provincial treasurer, and I guarantee you'll have your appointment posted and be acting in a vacancy within three months. How good the post is will depend on your luck.”

It was an extraordinary opportunity, as though fallen from the sky. Wang could hardly stop smiling, yet did not know what to say. What was the master's name? He wanted to ask but felt awkward. After all this conversation, would it not be absurd to admit that he did not even know who Jiangsu's education commissioner was? Afraid that Wang might still doubt him, Yang added, “My master is especially willing to help people from his home province. You count as half a Yunnan man, and with me speaking for you, success is certain.” As the wine took effect, Yang grew more expansive. Although a servant, he was no common labourer. He was literate and capable of receiving his master's guests and handling contacts on official business. He therefore knew a great deal about developments in the capital's official circles. His friends, however, were rough men who could not converse with him on such subjects. Wang was different. Whether Yang discussed the examinations, official advancement, or the backgrounds and connections of high officials, Wang understood and listened with keen interest. This gave Yang immense satisfaction. Drinking with someone who truly understood him seemed one of life's rare pleasures.

“My master is liked by everyone. Among his examination classmates, he was young, talented, and handsome, so they were all willing to look after him. Within ten years of completing his Hanlin probation, he had become a vice-minister. If his father hadn't died four years ago, forcing him to spend more than two years out of office in mourning, he'd surely be a minister by now.” The reference to completing Hanlin probation told Wang that the man had belonged to the Hanlin Academy. “Which examination year was your master's?” he asked. “The yiwei year, the fifteenth year of Daoguang. That was a cohort of dragons and tigers. They're the most influential men around now.” Yang spoke with delight. “My master placed forty-ninth in the second class and was selected for the Hanlin Academy. Fiftieth was His Excellency Peng, now a grand councillor. He wasn't selected for the Hanlin, but his official luck has been the best of all. He's very much in favour now and runs everything in the Grand Council.”

That seemed difficult to believe. Wang knew that seniority mattered among the grand councillors. However remarkably Peng Yunzhang had risen, he was still a relatively recent member. How could he be running everything? “Then there's something I'd like to understand,” Wang said. “Aren't there several grand councillors?” “There are, certainly. But the ones above him aren't handling affairs now. The most senior was His Excellency Saishang'a. He was sent to Guangxi to fight the Long-Haired rebels, lost his battles, and was dismissed. Then there's His Excellency He Rulin, who's unwell and has taken sick leave. That leaves His Excellency Qi Junzao, a man of long standing, but his energy is failing too. And that sixth son of Prince Zheng's household, the imperial minister Sushun, is always opposing him. He's so discouraged that he's even less willing to take charge. That leaves His Excellency Peng. There are another two or three below him who passed their examinations earlier, but they joined the Council later. In its business, they have to defer to him and let him decide.”

“No wonder! With such a powerful patron, your master's promotion to minister can only be a matter of time.” Wang asked another question. “When he returned after mourning, did he resume his post as vice-minister of War?” “He was transferred—to Revenue, with additional responsibility for the Mint Office. A fine appointment! Without someone on the inside looking after him, how would it have come his way?” Yet what was the man's name? Wang grew increasingly curious, but the longer they talked, the more awkward it became to ask. After the meal they arranged to meet again. No sooner had Yang left than Wang went out into the street himself.

He wanted to buy a book. It was available in every major city: the Complete Directory of Official Ranks, printed by Rongzhuzhai in the capital's Liulichang district. Wang bought two issues, the summer and autumn editions for the current renzi year of the Xianfeng reign. Turning to the section on the Board of Revenue, he could scarcely believe his eyes. The names were unmistakable. The Chinese-held posts of president and vice-presidents of the Board of Revenue were occupied by Sun Ruizhen, Wang Qingyun, and He Guiqing. He Guiqing, courtesy name Genyun, was a native of Kunming, Yunnan. “How strange! Can it be the same He Guiqing?” Wang murmured. “His ancestral home wasn't in Yunnan, and I never heard of him using the name Genyun. Is it really him?”

Wang felt an excitement he could hardly describe, but his thoughts were in turmoil. He urgently needed a quiet place to think. Back at the inn, he closed his door and lay on the heated platform, trying to recall the past. The wine, combined with this wholly unexpected discovery, left his mind in confusion. Only after a long struggle did he find a thread to follow through the tangle. That thread began with his first arrival in Yunnan with his father. His father's personal name was Xie, his courtesy name Meilin. Poor but devoted to learning, Wang Xie was widely respected. In the twenty-third year of Jiaqing, he placed thirty-sixth in Fujian's provincial examination. He scraped together every available resource for travelling expenses and went to Beijing for the metropolitan examination. The examiner assigned to his section recommended his paper, but the chief examiner rejected it. A poor scholar who failed the examination faced miserable circumstances. Fortunately, Yan Jian, formerly governor of Fujian, had been promoted to governor-general of Zhili. He had always thought highly of Wang Xie and invited him onto his private staff. This was an excellent opportunity: the stipend would support Wang's family, while he could remain near the capital and await the next metropolitan examination. He would avoid another long journey and the despair of trying to raise its cost.

Unexpectedly, in the third year of Daoguang, Wang Xie's great-grandmother died and he had to return home for the funeral. The metropolitan examination was held only once every three years. Repeated interruptions brought him into middle age. Even if he passed and received an immediate appointment, he would become no more than a departmental official in one of the Six Boards or a local magistrate. Why not take the route of the Grand Selection instead? The Grand Selection had been devised for provincial degree-holders who were older, poor, and experienced in the world. Princes and senior officials appointed by the emperor selected candidates, judging first their bearing and appearance and then their fluency in speech. Wang Xie met both requirements. He also wrote well, and his service on a governor-general's private staff made his familiarity with official business unquestionable. He was therefore placed in the first class and assigned to Yunnan. He took his family to Yunnan and was promptly appointed acting deputy prefect of Qujing. After serving in various counties, he was eventually transferred to act as magistrate of Kunming, the county containing the provincial capital. One day, returning to his office from an outing, he heard someone reading aloud in the gatehouse as his chair passed through the entrance. The voice was exceptionally clear, its rise and fall bringing out the full meaning of the prose. He could not help admiring it.

When he reached his private quarters, he asked an attendant, “Who was the boy reading in the gatehouse?” “The son of Old He, the clerk who drafts documents at the gate.” “Ah! He reads well. Bring him to me.” Old He's son was summoned. Wang Xie saw a boy of fourteen or fifteen with fine features and a composed manner, looking for all the world like the son of a family distinguished in scholarship and office for generations. Studying him more closely, he found something exceptional in his features, the signs of a man destined to rise early. His astonishment grew.

“What is your name?” “He Guiqing, Your Honour. Gui as in the red osmanthus, and qing as in the phrase qingmi, ‘pure and secret.’” The answer almost seemed an omen of selection for the Hanlin Academy, whose hall was known as the Qingmi Hall. Wang Xie asked, “Have you begun composing essays?” The boy looked a little embarrassed. “I have no one to guide me,” he said. “I haven't yet found my way into it.” “Bring me your written exercises.” He had brought them already: two slim notebooks made of bamboo paper. He presented them with both hands. Opening them, Wang Xie found that the boy had not only begun writing essays but also attempted both classical poems and lyric verse. He had considerable learning, and his handwriting was good as well. Having risen through hard study himself, Wang Xie knew the hardships of a poor scholar. The sight of He Guiqing stirred his sympathy for neglected talent. “This is what we'll do,” he said. “From tomorrow, you shall study with my eldest son.”

That eldest son was Wang Youling. From then on, He Guiqing became both his study attendant and his fellow pupil. Was this the He Guiqing who employed Yang Chengfu? That was the question Wang now had to answer. He regretted not having asked where Yang was staying; he could not go and find him. Then it occurred to him that even if he knew the address, he could not rush over and ask outright. If it was the same He Guiqing, perhaps his family background was a secret. Why tear open an old wound and turn a former friend into an enemy? If it was someone else, Yang might think Wang suffered from some disorder of the mind. How could he then be expected to recommend him wholeheartedly to his master?

There was nothing for it, then, but to search his memory. He remembered He Guiqing as a proud young man, but also one of deep feeling, who often secretly helped him with his lessons. He liked to argue and held views unlike other people's—sometimes remarkably perceptive, sometimes absurd. Whatever the merits of his ideas, however, it had been a pleasure to lie in adjoining beds on rainy nights and listen to him range freely over past and present. Those days had not lasted long. Wang's mother died in Kunming, and he travelled the great distance home with her coffin. He never saw He Guiqing again. Nor did he remember his father speaking of him afterwards. In fact, after father and son parted in Yunnan, they themselves had few opportunities to meet. Wang recalled that He was only a year or two older than he was. How could he have been selected for the Hanlin Academy more than a decade earlier? Besides, he was not a Yunnan native and could not have taken the provincial examination there. It seemed likely that the vice-minister of Revenue appointed education commissioner in Jiangsu merely shared a name with Wang's old schoolmate.

But why should both men be connected with Yunnan? One coincidence was possible; could there really be another? Yang's description of a master who had risen young, who was talented and handsome, also fitted the He Guiqing Wang had known. His uncertainty deepened, and his desire to resolve it grew stronger. At last evening came, and Yang arrived as agreed. Once again, they sat opposite one another over four dishes and a hotpot, drinking together.

“I finally settled an important piece of business this afternoon,” Yang said. “I've hired all the boats.” “Oh?” This time Wang asked about He Guiqing by name rather than referring vaguely to “your master.” “When will His Excellency He arrive?” “Around noon tomorrow.” “Will he board the boat as soon as he arrives?” “Hardly! We'll be delayed at least three or four days. Think how many officials in Tongzhou want to cultivate him. Quite apart from anything else, he cannot decline the farewell dinners given by the Tongzhou–Yongping circuit intendant and the granary vice-minister. That's two days gone already.”

“Then...” Wang asked cautiously, “might I have an opportunity to meet him?” Yang considered it. “Let's do this. Come to the temporary residence early tomorrow morning. When my master arrives, join those waiting to receive him at the gate. I'll submit your visiting card immediately and see what he says.” “Excellent. I'll do as you suggest.” “One more thing. My master is young and handsome himself, so he pays attention to other people's appearance. Have you brought your formal robes?” That reminded Wang of a difficulty. He had left in the fifth month and had a set of summer robes made just before his departure. He had no winter set. When he admitted this, Yang said, “It's a good thing I asked. There's no time to have them made now, and we may not find any ready-made ones. Fortunately, you're of average build. I'll borrow a set for you.”

Yang was as helpful as could be. He personally borrowed a nearly new blue padded gown, a black satin outer jacket edged with fox fur, and a winter hat. Wang opened his trunk and took out the gold hat button signifying the eighth rank, together with his insignia badge embroidered with a small quail, and fitted everything in place. Looking at his boots, he saw that they had two holes, so he sent Liu Si to buy a new pair. Meanwhile, he had his head shaved and face scraped at the barber's stall outside the inn. Back in his room, he hastily trimmed the lamp and wrote his visiting card. Beneath his name, he carefully added in smaller characters: “Courtesy name Xuexuan; also known as Yingjiu.” If Yang's master really was He Guiqing, his former schoolmate and study attendant, there could then be no doubt who this Wang Youling was. Early the next morning, Wang dressed carefully and examined himself in the mirror. The proverb was right: “A Buddha needs gilding; a man needs fine clothes.” In the borrowed robes, he felt dignified and imposing. His pleasure lifted his spirits further. He asked Liu to hire a carriage and went straight to the temporary residence Yang had described: a secluded set of rooms in a Daoist temple near the west gate.

“You're early,” Yang said. “He won't be here until around noon. Sit down and have some tea.” Something now occurred to Wang. Once his card was presented, an astonishing reunion might follow. Yang, unaware of the care behind Wang's silence, would surely think, “What a sly fellow! I've treated him so well, yet he never breathed a word beforehand. That's no way to treat a friend.” But Wang could not tell him the whole story, and even a hint of its origins would be unwise. After turning it over in his mind, he decided to give a vague warning now and explain afterwards. He drew Yang aside and said quietly, “Brother Yang, if His Excellency He receives me, something may happen that you won't expect.” “What sort of thing?” Yang grew uneasy. “You're not planning to present some memorandum, are you?” “No, no!” Wang answered, clasping his hands reassuringly. “Have no fear. Whatever happens, it certainly won't mean trouble.” “Good. I didn't think you would get me into difficulties.” “How can you say that?” Wang was distressed. “You have shown me more kindness than I can repay. How could I cause you trouble?”

Yang nodded and was about to ask more when a man dressed as an official messenger galloped up to the gate. He appeared to be an advance rider from He's party, and Yang hurried out to meet him. It was indeed the news they awaited. He Guiqing had reached Tongzhou and was exchanging courtesies with the officials assembled at the reception hall. He would be at his temporary residence very shortly. Wang began to feel nervous. If this really was the He Guiqing he had known, their present positions were as far apart as heaven and earth. How should he address him? What could he say without impropriety? He had no idea. The tangle of fear and excitement reminded him of only one other moment in his life: the day he became a bridegroom. Fortunately, the great chair, carried by eight bearers and preceded by gongs and shouted commands to clear the way, went straight through the residence gates. Wang merely stood in attendance without announcing his name. The curtain of the chair remained closed, and its occupant had no idea that an expectant salt-station superintendent was there to receive him. Anyone else might have considered the effort wasted. Wang felt only relief. He breathed out and returned to his seat in the gatehouse.

He had scarcely sat down when calls were passed outward from within: “Invite His Honour Wang in!” “His Honour Wang!” His heart began pounding again. He stood, sat down, and stood once more, uncertain what to do. At that moment Yang came running faster than anyone. Reaching Wang, he pulled him aside to a quiet spot. Blinking repeatedly in astonishment, he asked, “Your Honour Wang, what exactly is your connection with my master?” “Brother Yang...” “Your Honour!” Yang interrupted loudly. He made a respectful salute, then straightened. “You must never address me like that. If my master hears of it, he'll be furious. He'll send me straight back to Yunnan.” “What should I call you, then? Old Yang?” “Yes. If you would rather not use my name, Old Yang will do.”

“Old Yang, first tell me: what did your master say when he saw my card?” “He was delighted. He said, ‘This is an old friend. Show him in at once!’” Now Wang was delighted too. “Yes,” he answered readily. “Our families are old friends. We haven't met for many years. I feared it might be a different man with the same name, so I didn't dare tell you sooner.”

“No wonder!” Yang's puzzle was solved. “Please come in at once.” He bent slightly, extended a hand to invite Wang forward, and led him to a small courtyard. The courtyard had formerly been the temple priest's retreat for quiet meditation. Half hidden by trees and flowers stood a row of three single-storey rooms. Above the central doorway hung a small plaque bearing the name Crane Pavilion. Before they reached it, an attendant called out, “His Honour Wang has arrived!” The padded curtain lifted, and a man in his thirties stepped outside. His face was as fair as jade. He wore a small black satin cap topped with a coral bead, a lightly padded robe covered in somewhat worn blue-grey satin, neatly pressed trousers gathered at the ankles, white cotton socks, and black satin shoes. Graceful and at ease, he looked from head to foot like a young gentleman from a family of distinguished refinement. Nothing in his appearance suggested a serving official of the second rank. At first Wang scarcely dared recognise him. It was He who spoke first. “Xuexuan! Twenty years apart, and who would have thought we'd meet again here?”

The voice was utterly familiar. The only difference was that the old address, “Young Master,” had become “Xuexuan.” That brought home to Wang how completely their relative positions had changed. He could no longer call him “Little Qing.” He could not even call him “Genyun,” a name he had discovered only in the official directory. Rank demanded its proper courtesies. He would have to address him as “Your Excellency He.” “Your Excellency He!” Wang said, making a formal salute. Now He grew a little embarrassed. “Please, there's no need!” He stepped forward to raise his old friend, then turned to Yang. “Has His Honour Wang brought an attendant?” The real question concerned a change of clothes. Had Wang brought a servant, the man would have known that the host would invite his guest to dispense with formal dress and would have brought a clothing bundle accordingly. Yang understood and answered quickly, “His Honour is travelling alone. He has brought no one.”

“Then help him change at once,” He said. “See whether that newly made fur robe of mine will fit.” “Yes, sir.” Yang turned to Wang. “Please come with me, Your Honour.” He led Wang into a guest room on the east side, let the curtain fall, and withdrew. Looking around, Wang saw that the paintings and calligraphy on every wall bore the signature Genyun. Although this was only a brief stop on a journey, the rooms had clearly been arranged for their occupant. He Guiqing certainly lived in style. Twenty years had transformed him completely. As Wang reflected on these changes, Yang returned with a new fur robe belonging to his master and a sleeveless outer garment that was almost new, and helped him change. In the space of a single day, the two had gone from strangers to close friends, and from friends to men whose positions were wholly unequal—almost master and servant. Even this small turn in human relations was enough to reveal the endless, unpredictable changes of the world and give a man much to ponder.

“Your Honour,” Yang said, “these clothes fit very well. You can wear them when you leave. I can return the borrowed official robes straight away, which will save another errand.” “I am truly indebted to you!” Wang grasped his hand. The warmth he felt in his heart exceeded even that supplied by the new sheepskin robe, whose fine curls of wool were known as “shredded radish.” “Old Yang, I really don't know how to thank you.” “You make too much of it. Everything in life comes down to a fortunate connection.” Yang brought him a mirror. “Look at yourself, Your Honour. What a difference a day makes!” Wang found that he looked different again from the man in official robes. His face glowed with pleasure and good fortune. With a little moustache turned down at the corners, he would have looked like a comfortably prosperous gentleman blessed with every advantage.

After studying himself for a while, he suddenly laughed. The laughter was cheerful but seemingly without cause, and Yang was puzzled. “Old Yang, you say life is a matter of connections. I say it's a play. Look!” He pointed to what he was wearing and then to the neatly folded official robes. “Aren't these all costumes? Then again, perhaps it's those connections that create the drama. Chance brings people together, and each of us depends on the opportunities it gives. There's no explaining it.” “Quite right, Your Honour. Now, please come along. My master is waiting. Make sure you play your part well!” “Very true.” Wang nodded deeply. Once he began to think of it as playing a part, he no longer felt awkward or at a loss. Walking with measured dignity, he followed Yang into He's room on the west side. He bowed on entering and said composedly, “Thank you, Your Excellency, for your generous gift. You have truly taken the robe from your own back to clothe me. I cannot express my gratitude.” He Guiqing had not expected such worldly assurance and self-possession from Wang. He was surprised, but also relieved. He had been afraid that Wang might expose his humble origins before the servants. Judging by this performance, he need have no such fear.

“Please, you are far too formal! What is mine is yours.” He Guiqing responded generously, making it clear from the outset that he had not forgotten their old affection. “Come and sit on the heated platform. You'll be more comfortable.” Eight raised dishes of fruit and refreshments stood on its small table. In front, coals crackled softly in a gleaming copper brazier. Wang felt that the setting was ideal for a long conversation about the past and gladly took the less honoured seat. He Guiqing tried to give him the place of honour, but Wang firmly refused, and the matter was dropped. When Yang brought lidded bowls of tea, the host instructed him, “Turn away every visitor. His Honour Wang and I have been like brothers since childhood. We haven't met for twenty years and have a great deal to discuss. Tell the others they needn't wait outside.” “Yes, sir. May I ask about this evening's dinner engagement...” “I know. We'll discuss it later.”

Once the servants had withdrawn and the two were alone, they found it difficult to know where to begin. He Guiqing still seemed somewhat embarrassed. Seeing this, Wang blurted out the first words that came to him. “Twenty years apart! I never imagined Your Excellency would rise so high. As the poem says, ‘The companions of my schooldays have become men of consequence.’ My warmest congratulations.” The words were not particularly tactful, but at least they opened the conversation. He Guiqing immediately waved a hand. “Xuexuan, we must change how we address each other. In public, the dignity of the court requires official titles. In private, call me Genyun.” “Very well.” Wang readily accepted the suggestion. “I didn't know you had taken that name. How did you come by it?” “I chose it myself. Genyun means ‘rooted in Yunnan’—a reminder never to forget where I began.” So that was it. Wang thought the explanation amounted to deliberately displaying a sign that said “Yunnan native.” Perhaps He had passed the provincial examination under a false claim of local registration. But that was none of his business. In any event, remembering one's beginnings was a good thing.

“I also heard that your father had passed away,” He Guiqing continued. “It happened just when I lost my own father. We were both in mourning, and etiquette did not permit an exchange of condolence visits.” The man he referred to as his late father was the person Wang had once called Old He. Now Wang naturally had to change his language too. “I was remiss as well. I didn't even know of your honoured father's passing. To tell the truth, I didn't know you had passed the provincial examination or entered the Hanlin Academy either. Otherwise...” Otherwise, he would long since have been in touch. Wang left the words unsaid, but He understood. Yang had already briefly explained that Wang was travelling to the capital to purchase a higher qualification, and his circumstances did not appear particularly good. He therefore asked, “Have you been in Zhejiang all these years?” “Yes,” Wang replied. “When I met my father in the capital that year, the journey back to Fujian for the provincial examination was so long that I purchased qualification as a salt-station superintendent instead. I was assigned to await a vacancy in Zhejiang and have lived in Hangzhou ever since.”

“How have things gone for you?” “Ah! It's a long story.” Wang hesitated. “We've been brothers since childhood. What is there you cannot tell me?” Young and proud, Wang had been ashamed to describe his poverty to his former study attendant. Encouraged now by He Guiqing, and remembering his own notion that life was a play, he found he could speak freely. “On this journey, I've had two extraordinary encounters. One was with you; the other was with a remarkably generous friend. To meet both an old friend and a new one in such unusual circumstances is one of the great pleasures of my life...” He then told the story of Hu Xueyan's gift. He Guiqing listened with keen interest and smiled warmly when it was over. “I too ought to thank this Mr Hu. Without his generous help, you would never have come north, and we could not have met again on our travels.”

“Exactly! It seems this is the year my bad luck ends and my good fortune begins.” Just then, Yang called from outside the window. “Your Excellency, the Tongzhou–Yongping circuit intendant has sent to invite you to the banquet.” “Very well, I know.” After a pause, He added, “Come in.” When Yang appeared, He told him to arrange a meal for Wang, settle his bill at the inn, and bring over his luggage. Wang said nothing and left the arrangements to his host. He then enjoyed the most comfortable meal he had eaten since leaving for the north. Yang, who only yesterday had sat at table with him, exchanged toasts, and called him brother, now stood at his side with the utmost deference. If anything troubled Wang, it was the sense of guilt and discomfort this change produced.

After the meal, Yang went to fetch the luggage, and Wang reclined on the heated platform for a nap. He woke as the clock struck three, just as He Guiqing returned. Over freshly brewed tea, they resumed their interrupted conversation. Returning to Wang's change of fortune, He Guiqing asked about his plans in detail. Wang frankly described Yang's proposed arrangements but made no request of his own. He thought it unnecessary: He would naturally make the appropriate provision for him. “You must certainly purchase the right to a designated province,” He Guiqing said. “But you needn't specify Jiangsu.”

“Which province, then?” After a moment's thought, He suddenly asked, “Have you heard about the major case in Zhejiang?” Almost immediately he corrected himself. “Of course you haven't. It happened only recently, and news doesn't travel that fast. We'll leave that aside for now. But you do know that Zhejiang had a change of governor half a year ago?” “Yes. Governor Huang.” “Huang Shouchen is an examination classmate of mine. He is enjoying great imperial favour just now. However...” He paused briefly. “You should return to Zhejiang.” The meaning was obscure, and Wang could not make it out. He steadied his thoughts. This was his opportunity, the crucial moment; he must not let it slip. Surely it would be better to remain near He Guiqing, on whom he could rely in any emergency, than to be sent to another province. Having decided this, he pressed his point indirectly. “If you would rather I did not go to Jiangsu, then I shall return to Zhejiang.” “You misunderstand me!” He answered at once. “Why should I not want you in Jiangsu? To be frank, I have no friends from my youth around me and sometimes feel very lonely. Nothing would please me more than to have you close by for a daily conversation. I am asking you to return to Zhejiang for your own sake.”

“Then I certainly misunderstood.” Wang smiled. “But how would it benefit me? Please explain.” “I don't know Governor Yang Wending of Jiangsu well. He also passed one examination cycle before me, which makes him my senior and leaves me less free to speak. Even if he obliged me, he would not give you a particularly good post. Zhejiang is different. To speak frankly, Huang Shouchen is a very harsh man. But with a letter from me, his treatment of you would be quite another matter.” “I see,” Wang replied, only half convinced. “I might as well explain plainly, so that you can stop worrying. But...” He glanced towards the window. “This is strictly confidential. You must not breathe a word of it.” “Of course, of course.”

“Huang owes his position to the support of us yiwei examination classmates. That is only part of it.” Leaning across the small table, He lowered his voice. “He is now involved in a case which the emperor has sent me to investigate secretly on my journey. Naturally, he knows that I carry an imperial commission and has no choice but to oblige me. Your luck is remarkable. Earlier would not have done, nor later. At this exact moment, a letter from me will make him do whatever I ask.” “Ah!” Wang felt relief and delight spread through him. He could not help recalling the saying about accumulating virtue for one's descendants. Had his late father not helped He Guiqing, how could this opportunity have come to him today?

That evening He had another engagement, this time with the granary vice-minister. On returning, he ordered wine and sat up drinking with Wang far into the night. Twenty years of joys, sorrows, separations, and reunions supplied an endless flow of conversation. Yet one great puzzle remained in Wang's mind, and he could never quite bring himself to ask about it. How had He Guiqing entered the Hanlin Academy? Wang assumed that he had purchased Imperial Academy status to qualify for the provincial examination, passed it to become a provincial graduate, then gone to the capital, succeeded in the metropolitan examinations, and been selected for the Hanlin. But he was not a Yunnan native. How had he been allowed to take the provincial examination there? False claims of local registration were not unheard of, but they required considerable influence. Who had helped him?

Wang was embarrassed to ask, and He was embarrassed to explain. Over their cups, they talked instead of affairs in the capital. He described the Daoguang emperor's frugality and the hidden story of the present emperor's succession. It had depended on the guidance of his tutor, Du Shoutian. While still a prince, the future Xianfeng emperor had displayed such benevolence and brotherly affection that his father chose him to inherit the throne. “Although His Majesty is young, he is discerning and decisive, and determined to accomplish something,” He said enthusiastically. “The tide is turning. He has seen through those muddle-headed banner grandees; they cannot handle important affairs. Han Chinese officials are gaining influence now, though even among them he values only men who are young and willing to take responsibility. The petty, unscrupulous senior officials who merely follow precedent, muddle through, and want the office without doing the work are retiring one after another. There's a new spirit at court. Xuexuan, we are fortunate to serve an enlightened ruler. Let us both make the most of it.”

“How can I compare with you? A vice-minister sent out as education commissioner—when your three-year term is over, you'll become either a minister or a governor. I could never catch up with you.” “There's no need to lose heart. In wartime, a local official who renders military service can rise very quickly too.” He added, “Harsh as Huang Shouchen is, and difficult as he may be to serve, he does believe in getting things done. If you're prepared to endure hard work under him, he'll certainly promote you.” “That would naturally be due to your influence too. But...” Seeing him hesitate, He asked with concern, “What troubles you? Tell me, and we can discuss it.” “You say Governor Huang is difficult to serve. My own temper isn't good either. I'm afraid we may not get on.” “You needn't worry. His treatment of people depends on who they are. With my friendship behind you, he will certainly not make things difficult for you.”

“I see.” After a moment's thought, Wang asked cautiously, “You said there was a case involving him and that the emperor had sent you to investigate it secretly. What sort of case is it?” At this inquiry into confidential business, He looked uncomfortable. He considered it for a while before replying. “You'll learn of it sooner or later, so I may as well tell you. But it must go no farther than from my lips to your ears.” He then described in detail how Huang Zonghan had driven Chunshou to his death and how the emperor had become suspicious. Wang listened with alarm. He now had a fair idea of the governor's character. “Can a case like that really be suppressed?” he asked. “Why not? ‘Without someone at court, do not seek office.’ With the right connections, anything can be managed.” “What about Chunshou's family? Surely they won't let the matter rest.” “What do you think? Of course they want to make trouble. But Huang is exceptionally good at handling such things. You needn't worry about him.” He added, “I hear that Chunshou's wife went several times to the governor's office to protest in tears. She even had a statement of grievance drawn up and sent someone to appeal in the capital. Now everything is quiet. That's Huang's ability for you. I don't know how he managed to settle it.” “Can such things really happen? I've never heard the like.” “There's no end to the sordid things in official life.” He brushed the subject aside. “There's no need for us to concern ourselves with other people's affairs.”

Leaving others' affairs alone meant returning to Wang's own interests. He explained that the forces of Hong Xiuquan and Yang Xiuqing had not been stopped in Guangxi and had now entered Hunan and Hubei, spreading like wildfire. The court urgently needed military funds, and opportunities to purchase official qualifications had been greatly expanded. Donations towards military supplies were generally referred to the Board for favourable recognition. This was therefore an excellent moment. He urged Wang to proceed to the capital promptly, register, purchase the upgraded qualification, and return to Zhejiang to await appointment as soon as possible. “A few days won't matter,” Wang said with a smile. “I can see you aboard your boat before I leave.” “There's no need. When I took leave of the emperor, he personally instructed me to examine the advantages and disadvantages of sea transport for the grain tax along my route and report back, since this was among my responsibilities at the Board of Revenue. I need to discuss it thoroughly with the granary vice-minister here, so I shall be delayed several more days. Jiangsu and Zhejiang are close enough; we shall have plenty of opportunities to meet. Tomorrow I'll send someone to take you to the capital. I'll write the letter to Huang now.” “It would be a great help to have someone accompany me. The clerks at the Board of Civil Appointments have so many tricks that a man really needs someone who knows them.”

“Exactly. Now let me ask you something else. Once you return to Zhejiang and receive a post, what will you do?” Wang was taken aback. After thinking it over, he understood: the question was still about the capital required to become an official. Once the provincial treasury posted his appointment, whether substantive or acting, he would immediately become a serving magistrate. He would need a residence, a sedan chair and horses, suitable clothes, servants—the whole outward establishment. Add the journey to his post, and the expense would be considerable. Nor could he begin devising ways to extract money the moment he arrived. The expenses of the first two or three months would have to be found elsewhere. Naturally, Wang had considered this. He nodded. “Once the appointment is posted, that can be managed.” “I know. As soon as an expectant magistrate receives a post, people will offer him loans. But, to put it bluntly, that money is like the advance a brothel keeper makes to a prostitute. The lender follows you to your post and holds you in his power in everything you do. You'll end up with your reputation in ruins.”

He rose, went into the inner bedroom, and returned with a bank draft in his hand. “I'm not very well supplied with money myself, so this is all I can do. If you're economical, it should be about enough.” The draft was for eight hundred taels—more than enough. Overjoyed, his eyes shining with tears, Wang replied, “Such great kindness is beyond thanks. But I truly don't know how I shall ever repay it.” “Why talk of repayment?” On He's face was the satisfaction and relief of a man who had freely given a fortune and settled an old debt of gratitude. “To tell the truth, this is my repayment for your honoured father's help. Without him, I could never have passed the provincial examination in Yunnan.”
