INSTALLMENT 003
A Swift Rise to Prominence, Volume I — Chapter Two, concluding part

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

“Even so, I cannot help feeling that I do not deserve it.” “You needn't think that way. But you must remember Mr Hu, who generously helped you when you had nowhere to turn. He was neither a relative nor an old family friend. He helped because he saw ability in you and believed you could accomplish something. You should keep that in mind.” Wang took the advice deeply to heart. He had never lacked ambition, and these successive strokes of fortune stirred it still further. Whenever he closed his eyes, he saw a future of dazzling promise. Though he had not yet obtained an appointment, he was already savouring the pleasures of office. When he rose the next morning, He Guiqing was waiting with a letter to Huang Zonghan. It was no ordinary note of introduction; indeed, it scarcely resembled a recommendation at all. Much of it concerned private matters between close friends. Only then did it mention Wang, describing him as “a friend since boyhood, as dear as a brother,” whom He had specifically advised to purchase assignment to Zhejiang so that Huang might nurture his abilities and advance his career. The letter was so earnest and substantial that little was lacking except He's presence in person, hands clasped in appeal.

After Wang had read it and it had been sealed, he asked He to provide someone to accompany him. He very much wanted Yang Chengfu, but this was a wish his friend could not grant. Yang was his most capable assistant. Many official matters and personal connections were fully understood by Yang alone; no one could replace him. “Why not send Gao Sheng with His Honour?” Yang suggested. “That would work very well.” Gao Sheng was honest and capable too, and willing to accompany Wang, so the arrangement was settled. Wang took leave of He Guiqing, thanked Yang, and, under Gao's care, reached the capital that same day. He had intended to stay at the provincial guildhall. But the current renzi year had brought a special examination, and the following guichou year would bring the regular one. With metropolitan examinations in two successive years, unsuccessful candidates and newly arrived provincial graduates packed the place. An empty room was almost impossible to find. Moreover, Wang was seeking to upgrade a purchased qualification on the basis of his Imperial Academy status. Beside provincial graduates who might win distinction in the fourth month, he felt that he belonged to a lesser world, and the comparison embarrassed him. He therefore decided to spend a little more and take a room at an inn in Liangheyan.

The weather was bitterly cold. Even with a stove burning, the room felt like an ice cellar. Gao went out to buy tough paper and flour, then cooked a basin of paste on the stove. Cutting the paper into strips two fingers wide, he sealed every crack in the windows and wooden partitions. Once the northwest wind was shut out, the stove could do its work. The room soon felt as warm as spring. After supper, Wang discussed their business with him. “Your Honour, I have an idea. See what you think of it,” Gao said. “Tomorrow is the eighth day of the last month. In little more than ten days, the government offices will seal up their official seals for the New Year.” “Ah!” Wang suddenly remembered. “Once the seals are put away, the offices will be closed for a month. If we cannot finish in these ten-odd days, we'll have to spend the New Year here waiting. That would mean a long delay.”

“Exactly. Whichever way you look at it, that would be a poor bargain. I was thinking that it might be better to spend a little extra and finish the business before the offices close, then set off south before the year ends.” “Before the year ends? Wouldn't that be rather hurried?” “I'm thinking of your interests, sir. If you have few friends in the capital, spending the New Year at an inn won't be much pleasure. Besides, from New Year's Day to the Lantern Festival, you have to spend freely wherever you go. It really isn't worth it. Better to spend the holiday on the road. There's another point.” Gao leaned closer. “It would be best to reach Zhejiang and see Governor Huang before His Excellency He arrives, or at about the same time. That's when his letter will carry weight.” Wang understood at once and recognised the wisdom of Gao's advice. Huang had a reputation for harshness. Once Chunshou's case was settled, he might well discard the man who had helped him and cease to oblige He Guiqing. While He was in Zhejiang investigating the case, Huang would still need his goodwill, and the situation would be different. In any event, arriving sooner rather than later could only be the right course.

“I'll take your advice. Let's do that. But do you know how to go about it?” “There will be a way. I'll start looking tomorrow,” Gao answered confidently. “I'll see that it is done well and at a reasonable cost.” Pleased, Wang opened his trunk and took out the Board's certificate for his original purchased qualification as a salt-station superintendent. He then ground ink, spread out paper, and prepared a statement of his ancestry for three generations and a detailed account of his background. Once the documents were ready, he explained everything to Gao and gave him twenty taels for the expenses of making the necessary contacts.

Gao began his rounds the next day. The first reports were discouraging: either completion in time could not be guaranteed, or the extra fees demanded were too high. Three or four days passed. Wang grew anxious, and even Gao began to lose heart. Just as they were abandoning hope and preparing to spend the New Year in the capital, matters unexpectedly changed. A clerk at the Board of Civil Appointments had suffered a fire at home in which a mother and child had died. With the year drawing to a close, his household was ruined. To make matters worse, he himself was in prison over a case. Desperate and with nowhere else to turn, he had to appeal to his colleagues. Helping meant contributing money if one had it, or effort if one could. Financial contributions amounted to no more than eight or ten taels apiece; practical assistance proved more substantial. Official business was especially heavy before the New Year. From departmental officials down to clerks, everyone knew that provincial messengers and men seeking appointments or purchasing qualifications wanted their affairs settled early. Spending the holiday in the capital meant wasted time and additional living costs; missing a deadline on official business might also bring punishment. It was therefore an ideal opportunity to obstruct proceedings and extract money from those who could pay. To assist their unfortunate colleague, the clerks privately agreed on a standard schedule of charges. Anyone wanting business completed within a fixed time would pay the usual unofficial fees, plus an extra sum according to the circumstances. The addition would go to the stricken clerk. For outsiders, this was much cheaper than trying their luck, seeking one intermediary after another, and being fleeced at every stage.

Gao heard of the arrangement at a brush-and-ink shop in Liulichang. He checked with an acquaintance, confirmed that it was real, and hurried back to tell Wang. Through this acquaintance they negotiated a total of 480 taels to upgrade Wang's qualification to an expectant local magistracy with assignment to Zhejiang. Two-thirds represented the official payment; one-third covered miscellaneous expenses. They obtained two bank drafts. Wang paid the official amount himself, while the intermediary delivered the other. In only five days, Wang had both a new certificate from the Board and the official receipt for his contribution. The main business was settled, but arranging the long journey south proved troublesome. With the year almost over, carters and boatmen were reluctant to undertake it. Wang consulted Gao again. Now that everything important was done, they could leave whenever they wished; a few days made little difference. Why not wait until after the fifth day of the New Year? Gao's only concern was his master's interests, and he accepted the decision. They booked two large carts, paid half the fare, and agreed to depart on the seventh day, an auspicious date for a long journey.

By then, every government office except the Grand Council had closed for the holiday. The streets were full of hurried figures: some wore anxious faces as they sought help to get through the New Year; others carried lanterns and pursued debts late into the night. Wang, however, was at ease and went sightseeing with Gao every day. He knew some people in the capital, but there were several reasons not to visit. Everyone was busy with the holiday, and he did not wish to disturb them. He had few local gifts left, and arriving empty-handed would be discourteous. He also felt that his present circumstances were not impressive enough. Better to wait until he had prospered; then recalling old times would bring the real pleasure of friendly hospitality. Apart from brief calls on one or two close relatives, he visited none of his fellow provincials or other friends. On New Year's Eve, the guildhall stewards invited him to join their celebration. After dinner, tables were set up in the hall for various forms of gambling, including the shaking of counters and games of pai gow. Wang had no taste for these pursuits and returned early to his inn at Xiheyan. He had already given Gao the evening off, so the servant was away. An inn attendant stirred up the stove and made fresh tea. Bored with sitting alone, Wang ordered some wine, but drinking by himself brought no cheer. He considered seeking a woman's company to enliven the festival, then remembered that he was now qualified to become a magistrate, a parent to the people. He feared Gao would find out and think less of him. The Eight Great Lanes were close by, but their fashionable singing houses were notorious places to squander money. After so many strange encounters that year, who knew whether another romance might await him? If he became entangled in some endless debt of passion, would he not betray the generosity and hopes of both Hu and He?

Amid firecrackers sounding throughout the streets, Wang quietly went to bed alone. Yet sleep would not come. Looking back over his life, he found sadness and joy, much to reflect on, and still more to hope for. By failing to take the regular examination route as He Guiqing had done, he had already fallen behind. But that was no reason to admit defeat. A man must develop his own strengths. He might be less learned than He, yet he had grown up beside his father, observing people's livelihoods, becoming familiar with administration, learning to endure hardship, and dealing with all sorts of people. In these things, scholars buried in their books and ignorant of the world could not compare with him. “A thorough understanding of human affairs is itself learning.” If he belittled himself and lost his ambition, his abilities would only wither. Now a splendid opportunity had come, at a time when the country faced grave difficulties and an enlightened ruler sought effective government. A practical official seasoned by the world might achieve as much as a scholar in the splendid halls of court. These reflections stirred a powerful determination to accomplish something. Yet if he expected great things of himself, he needed more knowledge—not poetry and elegant essays, but learning relevant to the country's administration and the people's welfare.

Thus, when he visited Liulichang on New Year's Day, while others bought food and amusements, Wang remained in the bookshops like a scholar with a passion for collecting books. His bearing and clothes were now very different from before. The proprietor was eager to please him: first New Year greetings, then a plate of fruit, and finally polite inquiries about his surname, home province, and examination achievements. “My surname is Wang. I'm from Fujian. I was fortunate enough to pass last autumn.” He spoke as though he were a newly qualified provincial graduate. Wang was such a common surname that he felt safe from exposure. “Ah, indeed! With good fortune shining in your face, Your Honour is certain to succeed again in the coming metropolitan examination. My congratulations in advance!” “Thank you. In the examination hall, literary merit is no guarantee. We must leave it to luck.” “Your Honour speaks excellent Mandarin. You must have spent many years accompanying your father away from home.” “Yes.” Further questions might expose him, Wang thought. He promptly changed the subject. “Have you any good books on practical affairs?”

“Certainly.” The proprietor considered, then took a new set of books from a shelf himself. “Does Your Honour already own this?” It was He Changling's Collected Essays on Statecraft under the Present Dynasty. Wang had long heard of it and replied with pleasure, “I'll take a set.” “An excellent work. Practical learning is much valued nowadays. Master this book, and your policy essay in the palace examination is bound to be distinguished.” “Have you any books on foreign affairs?” “On that subject, there is a work compiled by His Excellency Lin of your own province that you must read.” This was Lin Zexu's Account of the Four Continents, which Wang also bought. Understanding now what his customer wanted, the proprietor brought out one related work after another until a pile had accumulated. There was no time to inspect them all closely, but the prices were generally fair, so Wang accepted them and had them bundled up to take back.

From that day onward, he shut himself in his room to study. He repeatedly read the essays on salt administration, river management, and grain transport in the Collected Essays on Statecraft, unwilling to pass lightly over a single word. He had long admired the achievements of Tao Shu of Anhua, Hunan, posthumously honoured as Wenyi. Reading these memorials and proposals, and learning how the reforms of salt administration and grain transport had been carried out, deepened his admiration. He also reached a conclusion of his own: creating benefits was not the hardest task; removing abuses was. To open up new ground through hardship, effort was enough to produce results. Once those benefits existed, however, people appropriated and controlled them until abuses multiplied. Removing the abuses then meant infringing what those people regarded as their rights, and fierce resistance was inevitable. Tao's methods themselves were not extraordinary. He had sought truth in the facts and aimed at workable, fair arrangements. What deserved admiration was his determination and courage in abolishing abuses. This led to a further essential point: power. The saying that a capable man cannot go a day without authority was true, Wang thought, but its meaning had to be considered from the other side. A man who held power yet accomplished nothing, drifting along in mediocrity, might commit no direct wrongdoing. Still, his harm was no less than that of a villain, for it was through men like him that official abuses became deeply rooted and almost impossible to remove.

His wish to serve the country compelled him to follow current events. By chance, an official messenger from Hubei arrived at the inn and occupied the next room. Lonely away from home, they shared wine one evening and discussed Hunan and Hubei. Wang learned that, after failing to take Changsha, the forces of Hong and Yang had captured Ningxiang and Yiyang, acquired several thousand boats, emerged at Linzikou, crossed Lake Dongting, and occupied Yuezhou. Pressing eastward, they had taken Hanyang in the eleventh month and the provincial capital, Wuchang, in the twelfth. Governor Chang Dachun, the education commissioner, the provincial treasurer, the judicial commissioner, the provincial military commander, the brigade commanders, and the circuit intendants, prefects, magistrates, and deputy prefects—almost all the city's civil and military officials had died. At the most tragic passages of his account, the messenger's tears fell into his wine cup. Wang too set down his cup in sorrow. Less than a year had passed since Chang Dachun's transfer from Zhejiang to Hubei. Wang had seen him in Hangzhou: a scholar of gentle and kindly temperament. Because he had suppressed pirates while governing Zhejiang, the court had assumed that he understood warfare and sent him to Hubei to halt Hong and Yang's advance. He had died with the city. In a sense, his death was a grievous injustice.

Yet local officials were responsible for defending their territories. The court had already ordered senior officials living in retirement to organise local militias for self-defence. In a troubled age, who could insist that civil and military duties remain separate? To distinguish himself in office, a man needed the ability to mount a horse and fight, then dismount and write the announcement of victory. Recognising this, Wang returned to Liulichang and bought works such as Records of Imperial Military Achievements, dealing with campaign strategy, troop training, and military finance. He intended to read them carefully on his journey. On the appointed day, the seventh of the first month, he left the capital early by road, heading south through Changxindian. Apart from the sea route through Tianjin, the land and water routes out of the capital met at Dezhou on the Shandong border. The canal approached from the east of the capital, then turned southwest through Linqing and Dongchang. The road came from the west and turned southeast through Pingyuan, Yucheng, Tai'an, and Linyi into Jiangsu. At Qingjiangpu, the two routes met again.

For twenty days on the road, Wang kept a book in his hands despite the cart's constant jolting. At night, in the inns, he continued taking notes beneath the tiny flame of an oil lamp. In this way he finished the Collected Essays on Statecraft, Records of Imperial Military Achievements, and Account of the Four Continents. Sometimes, reflecting in the cart, he felt that he now had a general grasp of grain transport, salt administration, and military affairs at home, as well as conditions abroad and relations with foreign countries. He had already planned the next stage of his journey. The carts were hired as far as Wangjiayingzi. Across the Yellow River lay Qingjiangpu, where he would hire a boat to take him along the canal to Hangzhou. To test his reading against reality, he might spend a few days there while changing from cart to boat. This was ancient Huaiyin, famous as the home of Han Xin, and the foremost junction of land and water routes for travellers coming from the south. The governor-general of the southern river administration was stationed there. River management, grain transport, and the distribution of Huai salt all centred upon the town. A firsthand investigation would truly be worth ten years of reading.

But at Wangjiayingzi he heard that the Taiping advance was growing ever more alarming. Upon reaching Qingjiangpu, he immediately sensed panic in the air. An endless stream of carts and horses carried disorderly loads of furniture and household goods. Countless strangers in dishevelled clothes spoke in unfamiliar accents, every face anxious and distressed. Plainly, they were refugees fleeing from the south. “Your Honour,” Gao said quietly, “things look bad. I fear the inns will all be full. Dragging the luggage from one to another would be a burden. Why don't you sit in a teahouse and watch it while I find us a room? Once I've secured one, I'll come back for you.” “Very well.” Looking up, Wang saw a large teahouse on the south side of the street. “I'll wait here.”

Inside, they stacked their luggage to one side and paid the porter, then looked for somewhere to sit. The place was noisy and crowded. Only a long table set lengthwise by the entrance was empty. Gao went over, dusted a bench, and said, “Please sit here, sir.” He was a northerner who had never travelled the southern waterways and knew nothing of their customs. Wang knew a little. That was the “head table,” reserved for leaders of the grain-transport fraternity. He hastily caught Gao's arm. “You can't sit there!” “Oh!” Gao stopped in surprise. There was no time for an explanation, and a tea attendant had already come to find Wang a place at another table. Once his master was settled, Gao hurried off. Over his tea, Wang asked his companions for news.

The news was dreadful. Since the fall of Wuchang, Hong and Yang's army had acquired more than ten thousand vessels of all sizes. They loaded them with gold, silver, other valuables, weapons, and provisions, and drove eastward along both banks of the Yangtze with hundreds of thousands of civilians. At Wuxue, a town in Guangji county, they encountered Lu Jianying, governor-general of Liangjiang. Hubei lay outside his jurisdiction. He had entered the province as an imperial commissioner to meet the enemy. The Green Standard troops were exhausted in spirit and had long since lost their ability to fight, while the newly recruited forces were few. They could not withstand Hong and Yang's men pouring downstream like a mountain torrent and fell back at every engagement. Meanwhile, the rebels' fleet passed Jiujiang, Hukou, and Pengze and entered Anhui. The judicial commissioner of Jiangsu, defending Xiaogushan, abandoned his position and fled, leaving the approach to Anqing, the provincial capital, wide open. Governor Jiang Wenqing had only a little over two thousand men to defend the city. Lu Jianying passed through after his defeat but refused to remain and went straight back to Jiangning. Seeing little hope of holding out, Jiang transferred part of the treasury funds, provisions, and weapons to Luzhou, then stayed to defend the endangered city. By then the garrison had already dispersed. Hong and Yang's men took the city easily, and Jiang was killed at the western outer gate of the governor's compound. This had happened ten days earlier.

“Ten days ago?” Wang exclaimed in alarm. “Where are the Long-Haired rebels now, then?” “That I don't know.” His fellow customer shook his head, looking deeply troubled. “They must have reached Wuhu. Perhaps they're at Jiangning already.” Wang turned pale. Could the army have moved so fast? He found it difficult to believe. But when he steadied himself and thought, it was not surprising. Here his reading on the journey served him well. Since ancient times, Jingzhou had been the strategic stronghold of the upper Yangtze. Once the upper reaches were lost, an enemy could sweep downstream, and the lower reaches could scarcely be held. Thus, southern dynasties that made Jinling their capital always stationed able commanders in Jingzhou and Xiangyang to protect the upper river. If that region changed hands, Jinling was like meat on a chopping board. Hence the sleepless anxiety of the Eastern Jin court while Huan Wen controlled Jingzhou, and the eventual conquest of the Southern Tang by the founding Song emperor after its upper-river defences had been lost.

He immediately formed a judgement of the present situation. No one at court seemed to understand strategy. They had concentrated troops in Hunan, Hubei, and Henan to prevent the rebels from moving north, while overlooking the weakness of the lower Yangtze region. This had driven the enemy towards the southeast, the country's great source of revenue. It was a grave mistake. Jinling, it seemed, must fall sooner or later. Thinking of He Guiqing, Wang's heart suddenly sank. Then he remembered that He was not in Jinling. Wiping the sweat from his forehead, he breathed out and murmured, “Thank goodness! Thank goodness!”

His companions looked up at him with troubled eyes. Realising that he had behaved oddly, he offered an apologetic smile. “I was thinking of a good friend. He...” Suddenly he asked, “Isn't the education commissioner's office at Jiangyin?” “I'm not sure,” the man replied. “Jiangsu has so many high officials that I can never remember which office is where.” “How can you not know?” someone at the next table interjected. “Jiangsu certainly has the most high officials, but all their offices are in fine places.” He counted them on his fingers. “Start with Qingjiangpu. The southern river governor-general is here; the director-general of grain transport is at Huai'an. The governor-general of Liangjiang, the garrison general, and the Jiangning provincial treasurer are at Jiangning. The governor and provincial treasurer of Jiangsu are at Suzhou; the education commissioner is at Jiangyin; and the Lianghuai salt commissioner is at Yangzhou.” So Jiangyin it was. Wang began planning. Once he reached Yangzhou by canal, he could travel east along the river to visit He Guiqing, then return to Hangzhou by way of Wuxi, Suzhou, and Jiaxing. There would still be time.

He had just settled the plan when Gao returned, out of breath. After great difficulty, he had found a room. Though he had left a deposit, someone else might take it if they delayed. In the confusion of war there would be no one to whom they could appeal, so he urged his master to hurry. Wang paid for the tea, and they went outside. They stopped a porter to carry the heavy trunks and cases on a shoulder pole. Gao took the bedding roll on his back, while Wang himself carried the lighter things, including the hat case. They hurried to the inn. The room was tiny and stood beside the kitchen, where cooking smoke filled the air. It was hardly fit for a guest. Yet when Wang thought of the refugees outside, supporting the old and leading children, not knowing where they could shelter, this little room seemed positively heavenly. “What about you?” he asked Gao with concern. “You need somewhere to sleep too.” “I'll spread my bedding on the floor beside your bed. We'll leave as soon as we've hired a boat. It'll only be a day or so.”

“Gao Sheng, I'd like to make a detour to Jiangyin to see His Excellency He.” Wang explained his plan. “Well...” Gao hesitated. “I would advise going straight back to Hangzhou. For one thing, you should report your arrival as soon as possible. For another, changing boats twice more would be easy enough in ordinary times, but it could be very troublesome now. The news is bad, sir. What if the route is cut off?” Gao's judgement was sound. An expectant magistrate assigned to Zhejiang could wait for ever without obtaining a post if he became stranded in Jiangsu. Wang changed his mind at once. That evening they agreed to hire a boat and reach Zhejiang as quickly as possible.

By the next morning, many more refugees had arrived, along with definite news. Wuhu had fallen, the government fleet had suffered a crushing defeat, and the brigade commander of Fushan had been killed. Hong and Yang's forces were advancing on Jiangning in three columns by land and water. The people of the lower Yangtze region, who had known no warfare for a century or two, were panic-stricken. Hiring a boat was difficult. With war spreading through the south, boatmen feared both taking their passengers into danger and having their vessels requisitioned by the authorities. Either could mean disaster. After a day's searching, they finally found a solution. Some Zhejiang grain boats were returning empty and could take passengers, at twenty taels a head. This was more than ten times the usual fare. With no alternative in such urgency, Wang reluctantly agreed. Still, travelling on a grain boat brought advantages at checkpoints and dams. The wind was favourable, and they made rapid progress. At Yangzhou they heard that Jiangning was under siege. Outside its walls stood seven or eight hundred thousand Taiping soldiers with red cloths wrapped around their heads; inside were only four thousand banner troops and a thousand Green Standard soldiers. The walls built by the founding Ming emperor were famously strong, however, and would not be easily breached.

If the attackers really numbered seven or eight hundred thousand, Wang thought, whether they took Jiangning immediately was almost beside the point. They had enough men to divide their forces: one army could advance east and seize Suzhou and Changzhou, while another crossed north of the river to establish itself on the Central Plain. Jiangning would then become an isolated city and fall without a battle. The thought made him pessimistic about the wider situation. Lying awake at midnight, listening to the canal water, he turned over the question of how the lower Yangtze region might be saved from disaster.

In this troubled state of mind, he reached Hangzhou. His first thought on landing was not of home but of Hu Xueyan. Yet naturally he could not go searching the teahouses before even putting down his luggage. Once home, he found many things to arrange. Most urgent was a move to a larger house. Their existing quarters were cramped; quite apart from appearances, there was nowhere for Gao to stay. Wang therefore sat only long enough to drink a cup of tea before taking Gao out to find a property agent. In Hangzhou, property brokers were nicknamed “tile-shakers.” They gathered daily at a particular teahouse. Each trade had such a place where business contacts were made, known as its “tea meeting.” When Wang arrived, the brokers—men who supposedly made even roof tiles shake their heads—took one look at his clothes, bearing, and attendant and assumed that an important customer had appeared. They crowded round to ask whether he wished to buy a house or take possession of one under a mortgage arrangement.

“Neither. I want to rent a house, and quickly. Ideally, I should like to move in today.” “Where would you find that?” they laughed, somewhat disappointed. “I have one,” a man said. Wang turned to negotiate with him alone. The layout sounded exactly right, and the rent was reasonable. “Then let's go and look,” Wang said. “If it suits me, we'll make the agreement at once and the tenancy can begin today. I like things straightforward. I don't want a house with complications attached.” “From your accent, I'd say you're from Fujian but have lived in Hangzhou for some years. Surely you know that we stubborn Hangzhou men stand by our word?”

The house was in Qinghefang, in the district Hangzhou called the Upper City. Since the Southern Song, this had been the heart of the town. It was close to both the governor's office in Youshengguan Lane and the provincial treasury, making official visits convenient. That alone pleased Wang. The house itself had a main building five bays wide, two successive courtyards, side wings, a sedan-chair hall at the front, and a small pavilion in a bamboo grove behind. Neither luxurious nor shabby, it suited his present standing perfectly. Reading his expression, the broker said, “Your Honour's luck is certainly flourishing. The previous tenant, Mr Zhang, was promoted and transferred to Shanxi. He left only yesterday. A house like this won't stay empty even a day; it might be let tomorrow. And you happen to come today. What a fortunate coincidence!” “Yes, very fortunate!” Wang too felt that everything was going his way. Delighted, he said, “Find the landlord immediately. We'll sign the agreement and begin the tenancy now.” “When shall we move in, sir?” Gao asked. “No day is better than the day at hand. Today, if we can; otherwise tomorrow.” Moving that day proved impossible, but there was plenty to do. First, men had to be hired to clean the house; then furniture and household necessities had to be bought. Wishing to avoid the air of a man suddenly come into money, Wang deliberately chose used mahogany tables and chairs. Together with his existing set of marble tables and chairs brought from Yunnan, they made an impressive display.

Money did indeed make things easy. Within two days of the move, everything was arranged: a cook in the kitchen, maids and an older woman in the private rooms, four chair-bearers waiting in the gatehouse, and a brand-new blue-cloth sedan chair in the front hall. Gao had become his chief steward. It was time to look for Hu Xueyan. Though his position had changed, Wang felt he must not flaunt it before a friend who had stood by him in poverty. One morning he put on a worn padded robe, took no servant, and walked to the teahouse he had once visited every day. He met many familiar faces there, but not Hu.

“Where's Young Hu?” he asked the tea attendant. “He hasn't been here for a long time.” “Why?” Wang grew anxious. “What's happened? Where has he gone?” “No idea.” The attendant shook his head. “He comes and goes like a spirit. Nobody really knows his affairs.” “Well, then...” Wang asked for a piece of tea-wrapping paper, borrowed a brush, and wrote down his address. Handing it over, he said earnestly, “If you see him, please be sure to ask him to come to me.”

After leaving, he remained uneasy. Afraid that the attendant might forget, he went back and pressed a piece of broken silver weighing about two taels into the man's hand. “Oh! What's this for?” “It's for you. Help me find Young Hu, and I'll thank you again when you do.” The attendant was astonished. This Mr Wang had once asked for his tea to be topped up seventeen or eighteen times and made a midday meal of two flatbreads. Now he casually handed over two taels of silver. He must have made money. Yet his clothes did not suggest much wealth. There had to be some reason why he was willing to spend two whole taels merely to find Young Hu. “Well, I... this is too generous.” The attendant asked, “But may I know why you're looking for him, sir?” “He's a good friend.” Wang smiled and said no more.

Having made these arrangements, he felt less disappointed. The attendant dealt all day with people from every walk of life and had a wide circle of acquaintances. If he made a real effort, he would surely find Hu. Wang's remaining fear was that Hu might have difficulty finding the house. As soon as he returned, he sent for a sheet of plum-red paper, wrote “Residence of Wang Youling of Minhou” in large characters, and had it pasted on the gate.

He now had to prepare to report his arrival and deliver his letter. Before visiting the provincial treasury, he wished to see a friend at the judicial commissioner's office. The provincial judicial commissioner, commonly called the niesi and respectfully the nietai, supervised criminal justice throughout the province. Wang's friend, a man surnamed Yu from Shaoxing, was the office's legal adviser. Shaoxing secretaries worked in government offices great and small throughout the eighteen provinces, giving rise to the saying, “No Shaoxing men, no government office.” For a local magistrate in particular, the first task on receiving a post was to engage advisers for criminal justice and revenue. With capable men in both positions, his administration could run smoothly and bring him reputation and wealth. Wang's friend was an outstanding legal adviser. He knew the Great Qing Code backwards and carried countless precedents in his head. In criminal cases, one applied the statute where there was one and relied on precedent where there was none. With a precedent to support the decision, the case papers sent to the Board of Punishments would not be rejected. “Secretary Yu” of the provincial judicial offices in Jiangsu and Zhejiang was known even to officials at that Board. They rarely questioned cases he had handled. Successive judicial commissioners therefore retained him with flattering words and generous pay.

Yu's uncle had served on private official staffs in Fujian, and Yu and Wang had known each other since childhood, though they seldom met. Seeing Wang arrive that day, Yu assumed he had come for a reason and asked directly, “Brother Xuexuan, what brings you here?” “There are two matters on which I'd like your advice,” Wang replied. “You know I had purchased qualification as a salt-station superintendent. Last year I went to the capital, changed categories, and obtained assignment to this province.” For a salt-station superintendent, changing categories naturally meant qualifying for a local magistracy. Yu himself had purchased an eighth-rank title so that he could secure imperial honours for three generations of his ancestors. Now that Wang outranked him, he drawled in his Shaoxing accent, “Congratulations! I shall have to call you ‘Your Excellency.’” “Why tease an old friend? Tell me, what's happening with Provincial Treasurer Chunshou's case?” “So you know about that affair too!” Yu asked another question. “Do you know who stands behind Governor Huang?” “A little. His examination classmates are very powerful at court.” “Exactly. Why ask any more?”

“But I heard that an imperial commissioner had been sent to investigate. Is that true?” “It makes no difference whether there's an investigation,” Yu said. “If there is one, it'll be conducted by one of their own.” Wang understood the implication. He did not wish to reveal his connection with He Guiqing, so he could not pursue the discussion openly. But there was one thing he needed to know. “Has this person of their own been to Hangzhou yet?” “Oh!” Yu looked at him closely. “You seem to know quite a lot, Brother Xuexuan.” “Not at all. That's why I've come to ask you.” After a moment's thought, Yu lowered his voice. “Since we're old friends and you've asked, I cannot refuse to answer. But the governor's future depends on this case. One mustn't spread stories carelessly; offending him would gain nothing. If the matter does not concern you, it's best to leave it alone. That's the sensible way to keep yourself safe.” Wang now had to give a definite explanation. Wishing to protect the reputation of a man he respected, however, he would not state his relationship with He plainly. He merely said that he had obtained a letter from He through an intermediary and did not know whether Huang would honour it.

“So that's it! Congratulations! He certainly will.” “How can you be sure?” “I'll tell you frankly. Education Commissioner He has already been here. Why should an education commissioner from another province suddenly come to Zhejiang? His visit might arouse suspicion and harm Governor Huang's reputation, so his movements were kept extremely secret. Fortunately, his orders were to investigate secretly, so that was not in itself improper. Think of it: if He has gone to such lengths to protect his old examination classmate, how could Huang fail to honour a letter from him?” “Ah!” Wang could not help smiling. He had been anxious, afraid that the friendship was less close than He himself claimed. Now that someone else had confirmed it, he could believe it without reservation.

“Let me tell you something else,” Yu continued. “When ordered to investigate, Huang reported that Chunshou, ‘distressed by the shortage of treasury funds and the difficulties of grain transport, suffered an attack of liver disease and consequently took his own life; there were no other circumstances.’ Can a man write those last words lightly? If there were any other circumstances, whatever they might be, he would be guilty of deceiving the throne. If he escaped execution, he would still go to prison. He is relying entirely on Commissioner He to conceal the truth. Just consider how much responsibility He has assumed on his behalf.” This made Wang anxious for He Guiqing. Helping to conceal the truth meant sharing the guilt of deceiving the emperor. If the affair came to light, the consequences would be disastrous.

For all his shrewdness, Yu could not guess this concern. He was simply pleased for his old friend and patted him on the shoulder. “Go and deliver the letter at once. I guarantee the provincial treasury will post your appointment within ten days. When that happens, I'll recommend a good fellow from my home district to handle your legal work.” “Exactly!” Wang hurriedly clasped his hands in thanks. “I shall have to rely on you for that. Please find me both a legal and a revenue adviser.” “Certainly. Leave it all to me. Now go and attend to your business.”

That same day, Wang reported to the provincial treasury, submitting his visiting card with four taels of silver wrapped as a gratuity for the gatekeepers. Expectant magistrates were innumerable. Unless a man had powerful backing, the provincial treasurer would not receive him individually. Wang knew this rule, but propriety required him to make the attempt. When the gatekeeper returned with the message, “His Excellency is unwell and will invite you to call another day,” Wang thanked him for his trouble and left. His blue-cloth chair carried him from the treasury to the governor's office in Youshengguan Lane. Seeing several large green-cloth chairs beside the screen wall, the bearers dared not approach and stopped some distance away. Wang got out, exchanged a glance with Gao, and entered ahead of him. The governor's gatekeepers were particularly high-handed. One look at Wang's hat insignia told them he was an expectant magistrate. When Gao took his card from its case and offered it, a gatekeeper did not even look properly at him, but called, “Little Eight! Enter him in the callers' register!”

The youth addressed as Little Eight had delicate features but no shortage of arrogance. “Bring the card here,” he told Gao. At the treasury, at least the card had been taken inside. Here, even submitting it seemed pointless. Gao, however, knew the world. Calmly he produced a gratuity and handed it to the gatekeeper. The man weighed it in his hand, looked slightly less forbidding, and asked, “Your master's surname?” “Wang.” Gao took out He Guiqing's letter. “There is also a letter. Would you be so good as to deliver it?” In consideration of the gratuity, the gatekeeper replied as though yielding reluctantly, “Very well, very well. I'll take it in for you.” He rose languidly, picked up a red-tasselled hat, put it on, and went inside. The governor's compound was extensive; the central passage alone took a considerable time to traverse. Wang waited patiently. Yet the delay became so long that not only master and servant grew uneasy; even the men in the gatehouse were puzzled. “What's happened? Second Master Liu has been in there ages.”

“Perhaps His Excellency has other instructions for him.” That was a reasonable guess, but it chilled Wang's hopes. Huang must have ignored He's letter and attached no importance to him. Otherwise, why leave a man awaiting an audience while giving instructions about something else? “Second Master Liu is coming out,” Gao said quietly. Wang looked up and noticed the change at once. Liu no longer moved with his earlier indolence. He was hurrying, his eyes fixed in their direction, as though eager to communicate important news. Wang's spirits rose. He turned towards Liu, who came straight to him, made a respectful salute, and smiled. “Your Honour Wang! Please come and sit in the gatehouse.”

Why this change from arrogance to deference? Apart from Wang and Gao, everyone in the gatehouse and all the idlers nearby looked astonished. Some drifted closer, hoping to learn who this seventh-rank official with a crystal hat button might be. Even the governor's celebrated Second Master Liu was treating him with extraordinary courtesy. Inside, Liu gave Wang the best seat, poured tea, and presented it himself. “Where is Your Honour's residence?” he asked. “In Qinghefang.” Wang supplied the address, and Liu had someone write it down. “This is the situation,” he said. “His Excellency asks to keep your card for now. The commissioners and intendants are with him at present, and if he invited you in, he fears there would be no time for a proper conversation. He would like you to dine here this evening. There is no need for formal dress. An invitation will be sent to your residence shortly.” “Oh, indeed.” Wang answered composedly, “The governor is most kind.”

“He also said that you are a fellow provincial and an old family connection, and should not be received by the ordinary rules. Please come early this evening. I shall be here. Your steward need only ask for Liu Er.” Gao was standing outside the door. Hearing this, he quietly came in. Wang saw him and said, “Gao Sheng, come and pay your respects to Second Master Liu.” “Second Master Liu!” Gao made a salute. Liu returned it. Servants were politely addressed as “Second Master” regardless of their actual place among their brothers, so he replied, “Second Master Gao!” Then he added, “We're all friends here. If you need anything, just let me know. There's no need for ceremony.” “Thank you. I'm sure we'll have much to trouble you with in future. We shall be grateful for your help.”

Wang had risen. Liu called out, “Where is His Honour Wang's chair? Bring it over at once!” It had been waiting outside the western outer gate. Only when it reached the main entrance did Wang walk out with the measured gait of an official, Liu following close behind with a deferential bend of the body. The servants and bearers of the commissioners and intendants watched sideways and whispered. Liu was being more attentive than he was even to the magistrate of the provincial capital's principal county. Soon after Wang returned home, Huang's invitation arrived. He naturally went at the appointed hour. Although Liu had said that ordinary clothes would suffice, Wang did not dare take the governor's politeness literally. He wore formal robes and prepared a visiting card, merely asking Gao to bring a change of clothes.

Liu was waiting when his chair arrived at the governor's office. He led Wang to the western reception room. “Please sit down, Your Honour. I'll announce you.” Before long, a cough sounded beyond a side door leading to the private quarters. An attendant appeared, carrying a silver water pipe in one hand and lifting the padded curtain with the other. Knowing that Huang was coming, Wang rose immediately and stood respectfully at the lower end of the room. Huang wore informal clothes. He had a long face, a broad nose, and sunken cheeks. As his eyes moved about, they fixed hard upon whatever they noticed. One glance was enough to show that he would be difficult to serve. Wang dared not delay. He hurried forward, knelt, announced his name, and paid his respects. “Please, there is no need!” Huang returned a bow, and his attendant came forward to raise the guest. The host courteously invited him to sit on the heated platform. Wang declined and took a chair in the less honoured position, sitting with his body turned slightly sideways in deference. Huang sat above him, a small tea table between them.

“Of all my examination classmates, Genyun is my closest friend. Since you and he have been friends from childhood, Brother Xuexuan, you are one of us. And we come from the same province. There is no need to be bound by ordinary ceremony.” “I am deeply grateful for Your Excellency's kindness,” Wang replied, “but the proper courtesies must be observed. I shall look to you for guidance in everything.” “Not at all. It is I who shall have to draw on your abilities...” From there, Huang began asking about his family and experience. After a while, an attendant came to ask whether dinner should be served and reported that the other guests had arrived. “Then let us go.” The host rose and invited Wang forward. “We can continue at table.”

Two other men were waiting in the inner room. Both were secretaries in the governor's office. One, surnamed Zhu, drafted memorials to the throne; the other, surnamed Qin, handled social correspondence. Both held provincial degrees but had failed at the metropolitan examination, and Huang had invited them to join his staff. Wang had no choice but to accept the place of honour at dinner. Yet in both rank and scholarly distinction, a man who had purchased qualification as a county magistrate hardly deserved to sit above the others, and he felt extremely awkward. Fortunately, he had read several practical books and had travelled south just as Hong and Yang's army swept eastward. His unusual experiences and observations kept the conversation lively and helped conceal his sense of inferiority.

When the wine had begun to take effect, an attendant came in and whispered in Huang's ear. The governor answered loudly, “Bring it at once!” What arrived was an official dispatch bearing a large purple seal. Huang opened it, read it, and handed it to Secretary Zhu, whose brows drew together before he had read more than a few lines. “Jiangning has fallen,” Huang told Wang calmly. “This is a communication from the governor of Jiangsu.” “So it could not be held after all!” Wang asked in distress, “What of Governor-General Lu?” “He died in the disaster. An unjust death!” Huang said. “The rebels used mines to breach the walls in two places. After they entered, Magistrate Liu of Shangyuan resisted so fiercely that they were already retreating. Unfortunately, Governor-General Lu was returning from the garrison general's office to his own headquarters and encountered some of the fleeing rebels. His guards and chair-bearers abandoned the chair and ran. He died there without even a proper fight. A grievous injustice!”

Outwardly, Huang remained composed, almost indifferent. This was a practised manner, intended to steady others by concealing his own feelings. Inside, he too was alarmed. He thought of Chang Dachun, Jiang Wenqing, and Lu Jianying: governors and governors-general had died one after another along the rebels' route. Provincial grandees enjoyed splendour, authority, and power far beyond those of officials in the capital. Yet in war they were responsible for their territories and had to stand or fall with their cities. Even had Lu escaped the rebels, he could not have escaped dismissal and investigation for losing troops and territory. Death would still have awaited him. The thought frightened Huang.

After further discussion of events and two more rounds of wine, he suddenly asked Wang, “Brother Xuexuan, your observations are more direct than ours. Now that Jiangning has fallen, what do you think will happen?” Wang considered before replying. “The rebels are advancing with extraordinary force, while the lower Yangtze region is weakly defended. Its people have known no war for over a century and are deeply unsettled. The area around Suzhou and Changzhou gives particular cause for concern.” “Fortunately, Xiang Xinran is pursuing them. Since retaking Wuchang, he has won eight victories in eight engagements and has been appointed imperial commissioner. Perhaps he can recover Jiangning.” This was Secretary Qin's opinion. Wang disagreed, but Qin was a member of the governor's staff and a new acquaintance. It would be awkward to contradict him, so Wang merely smiled. “Let me ask this,” Huang continued. “If Suzhou and Changzhou fall, the enemy will soon enter our province. Brother Xuexuan”—he looked closely at Wang—“can you suggest a plan?”

There was an element of examination in the question, and Wang dared not answer carelessly. After considering it, he replied calmly, “Hong and Yang's advance has become a spreading fire, and the court is exerting all its strength against it. Yet even a celebrated commander such as General Xiang has had little opportunity to display his abilities. Retaking Wuchang and winning eight successive engagements are great achievements, but they owe something to circumstances created by others...” “What do you mean by that?” Huang interrupted. Wang had intended a tactful hint. Pressed to explain, he had to speak plainly. He smiled apologetically towards Qin. “To tell the truth, Hong and Yang took the civilians with them and moved their whole army east. General Xiang, following behind, has merely recovered places they abandoned.” “Mm.” Huang nodded and turned to Qin. “That judgement is not unduly harsh.” He then looked back at Wang, inviting him to continue. “In my humble opinion, the immediate task is to hold out until reinforcements arrive. If Suzhou and Changzhou can resist for a time, the court will certainly send its best troops to converge from several directions. A stalemate will follow. Victory may not come quickly, but at least the situation will have been stabilised. Our province should therefore not wait until the enemy reaches its border before sending troops. The best policy is to go beyond our borders and meet them there.”

Huang stared at him, then struck the table in approval. “‘Go beyond our borders and meet them there’—excellent!” He was thinking that fighting outside Zhejiang might keep the war from entering the province. His responsibility for defending its territory would then not arise. If his troops were defeated elsewhere, there would still be room to shift the blame. Better still, the court had repeatedly ordered provinces not to regard fighting in other regions as no concern of their own, but to cooperate in defence and suppression. Sending forces across the border would accord perfectly with imperial wishes. A memorial announcing it would surely receive a favourable response. Secretary Zhu, who drafted the memorials, also thought Wang's phrase excellent. There was much to be made of it in writing, and he nodded repeatedly.

“The plan is good,” Huang said, “but carrying it out will not be easy. Of the two tasks, training troops and raising military funds, the harder is finding the money.” “Indeed,” Wang replied. “With territory comes wealth; with wealth, military funds; with funds, troops.” “And with troops, territory!” Zhu completed the circle. Everyone clapped and laughed. They discussed ways of raising money. Wang thought it essential to preserve the source of military revenue—that is, to hold the prosperous districts. The conversation then turned to grain transport. Having experienced the canal's shallow, silted channels himself, he remarked that changing times might bring this ancient system into decline. With war spreading through the lower Yangtze region, keeping the canal open would be harder still. A change to sea transport was therefore inevitable, and the sooner preparations began, the better. He thought these views reasonably practical, and Huang and the two secretaries listened with sustained interest.

Then a warning suddenly occurred to him. This was his first audience with the governor. Whatever the merits of his opinions, speaking so freely and expansively might make him appear shallow and presumptuous. Regretting it, he said almost nothing for the rest of the meal. Over tea afterwards, Huang finally addressed Wang's own business. “Since you've only just returned to the province,” he said, “let us wait until you've seen the provincial treasurer.” “Yes, Your Excellency.” Wang lowered his head. “I must look to you for advancement.” “You are too kind.” As he spoke, Huang lifted his teacup. This was the signal to the attendant on duty: the interview was over. The moment he saw it, the man called out in a long, loud voice, “Show the guest out!” Wang hurriedly rose and saluted. Huang accompanied him towards the door. At the front of the hall, Wang asked him not to come farther, but the host continued. Only after repeated protests at the reception-room doorway did Huang bow slightly and turn back.

Liu again led Wang out. Wang was uneasy, unable to judge the governor's attitude. At moments Huang seemed to value him; at others, to be merely polite. He wanted to ask Liu, but maintaining his official dignity made such a conversation on the way out inappropriate. He decided to send Gao to sound things out the next day. Beyond the main hall, two new lanterns bearing the character Wang stood beside his blue-cloth chair in the gateway. Liu opened the curtain himself. Only after Wang had backed into his seat did he speak softly. “Rest easy, Your Honour. That's how my master is. When he means to help someone, he doesn't announce it. He'll give instructions to the provincial treasurer. The treasurer is a bannerman and particular about ceremony. Be careful not to overlook anything.” “Indeed, indeed!” Wang clasped his hands inside the chair. “I'm deeply grateful for your help.” Those few words enabled him to go to bed at peace. He wondered at himself: after so many years of idleness without urgency, why was he now anxious on every side when an appointment was almost certain? He pondered it for a long time before understanding. This office was not for himself alone. He also had to fulfil Hu Xueyan's hopes, which made the prospect of success or failure weigh more heavily upon him.

Thinking of Hu reminded him of something. He nudged his wife beside him. “Did anyone call today?” “Do you mean Young Master Hu?” His wife was a sincere and kindly woman. “I've been hoping all day too. Afraid we might miss him, I sent the older maid to the gate again and again. But no, he hasn't come.” “It's very strange...” “It's your own fault,” she said. “After receiving such an immense kindness, you never even asked about his family or where he lived. Of all the muddle-headed people in the world, you must come first.” “I don't know what I was thinking.” Wang recalled the occasion. “It happened so suddenly that I could hardly believe it. It was like a wonderful dream. I wanted it to continue and didn't want to examine it too closely, for fear that even the dream would vanish.”

“If it was a dream, it was a very strange and wonderful one,” she said with happy amazement. “Who could have imagined that you would then meet His Excellency He in Tongzhou?” “Exactly. We had lost touch for years, and I never paid much attention to the court gazettes or the biographical registers of successful examination candidates. I had no idea he had done so well.” Wang added, “Just think: such an excellent connection was there all along, and I never used it. Instead, I stayed here in misery. Isn't it ridiculous?” “At least our hardships are almost over. It all comes back to the good your father did in his lifetime. Even meeting Young Master Hu must have been a reward for his hidden virtue.” Wang agreed wholeheartedly. “An official's office offers many opportunities to do good. A local magistrate controls both justice and revenue. It's easy to commit wrongs, but just as easy to accumulate merit. My father knew poverty and hard study, and understood people's difficulties. He really did a great deal of good.” “You must follow his example and plant some blessings for your children and grandchildren.” Then she added anxiously, “And kindness must be repaid. Not knowing where Young Master Hu is makes me very uneasy.”

“Ah!” Wang was even more troubled. “Don't speak of it again. If you do, I won't sleep at all.” Knowing that he had to rise early to visit the provincial treasury, she said no more. Before dawn, she quietly got up and woke the maids and older women. By the time Wang rose, everything was ready. After breakfast, he dressed carefully, entered his chair, and set off in high spirits. The visit was fruitless. Provincial Treasurer Lingui had gone to Shanghai on business concerning the sea transport of grain and would not return for at least ten days or a fortnight. Disappointed, Wang could only console himself with the thought that good things seldom came without delays.

With nothing to do, he spent his days either waiting at home for Hu or visiting Secretary Yu to learn the news. The court gazettes frequently carried edicts urging the provinces to organise local militias. The model was the fortified defence and denial of supplies used against the White Lotus rebels during the Jiaqing reign. Senior officials residing in their home provinces were to draw upon the old principle of mutual assistance, organise local forces, and defend their districts. Besides directing these men to work with local officials and gentry, the edicts required senior officials in the capital and members of the academies and censorial offices to recommend people they knew: men who understood affairs, were fair-minded, and already enjoyed public esteem. Entrusted with initiating the work, such men should manage it properly and command public confidence. The gentry were also reminded that, having received imperial favour, they should secure their communities and unite against the enemy. Persuading the population, raising contributions, clearing moats, and building stockades were all to be arranged according to local conditions. No official was to handle the funds. Governors and governors-general could recommend rewards whenever the work proved effective.

“Do you see?” Yu said, pointing to the provision that no official should handle the funds. “The court's distress is plain. Local officials know only how to squeeze the people; they won't work conscientiously.” “The plan is well framed,” Wang replied. “With that provision, the gentry needn't fear official interference and can act freely. But everything depends first on finding the right people. There are plenty of disreputable gentry too. If they choose to appropriate the money and monopolise control, any official who asks questions can be fended off with the imperial edict. The abuses could be endless.” Yu nodded. “I wonder who will be appointed in Zhejiang. Dai Chunshi will surely be among them.” “Who is Dai Chunshi?” Wang asked. “Is he the former vice-minister famous for his landscape paintings?” “Yes, exactly.”

A few days later, the gazette did indeed carry an edict appointing the retired vice-minister of War Dai Xi, grand secretariat academicians Zhu Pinfang and Zhu Lan, and Hunan governor Lufei Quan to supervise Zhejiang's militia arrangements. Lufei Quan's surname was not Lu but the compound Lufei, belonging to a clan found only in Jiaxing, Zhejiang. “The times are changing,” Yu remarked at their next meeting. “At the beginning of our dynasty, great campaigns were entrusted to imperial relatives as commanders-in-chief. Later they went to senior banner officials, usually sons of hereditary military families. Now they are being given to Han Chinese—and to scholars at that. This is a great change in the affairs of the state. I wonder how effective warfare learned from books will prove.” Wang reflected that it was true. Beyond Zhejiang, the militia organisers he knew of included Vice-Minister of Rites Zeng Guofan in Hunan and Grand Secretariat Academician Lü Xianji in Anhui. Elsewhere too, the work was led by senior civil officials of the first or second rank, living at home, who had risen through the examinations. Grand Secretariat Academician Xu Naizhao had even been ordered to assist with military affairs in the lower Yangtze region. Scholars were not merely commanding troops; they were going to the battlefield.

“Brother Xuexuan,” Yu continued, “living in a peaceful age is a blessing. But troubled times offer an opportunity to men who can accomplish things. I spend my life preparing the way for others, moving from one private staff to another. My own course is already set. You must not miss this chance.” Such encouragement made Wang's ambition burn more brightly and his desire to serve more urgent. Every day he awaited Lingui's return, hoping that after an audience and an appointment he could finally begin some real work.

One evening after supper, Wang had just spread out a map he had drawn himself, intending to consult it while reading Records of Imperial Military Achievements by lamplight. Gao suddenly entered in a red-tasselled hat and saluted. “Congratulations, Your Honour! Your appointment from the provincial treasurer has arrived!” “What?” Only then did Wang notice the official document in Gao's hand. “The treasury sent it by special messenger.” Gao presented it. Wang opened it. He was appointed executive director of the Sea Transport Bureau. This office had been established specifically to manage the change from canal to sea transport for the grain tax. The provincial treasurer concurrently held the title of director-general, but the executive director was the man actually in charge. Wang was disappointed not to receive a substantive local magistracy, yet it was still good news. “Where is he?” he asked.

He meant the messenger. Gao replied, “Still outside. He's a clerk from the provincial treasury.” “Oh.” Wang consulted him. “Do you think I should receive him?” “There is no need, but he should be given a reward.” “Of course, of course.” Mrs Wang had thought of that long before. An appointment messenger would expect a reward, and she had kept a red packet ready for days. She now brought it out herself. Gao hurriedly saluted and congratulated her too, and husband and wife exchanged congratulations. After the packet of four taels had been taken outside, the older maids, cook, and chair-bearers heard the news and came together to kneel and offer their good wishes. Mrs Wang rewarded each with a tael. Everyone was happy that night. Only Wang felt that something was still missing.

After the bustle subsided, he remembered an important question and called Gao back. “Has the provincial treasurer returned?” “He arrived this afternoon and went straight to see the governor. The instructions must have been emphatic, since your appointment was sent out this very evening.” “Then I must go early tomorrow to thank him.” “Yes. I've already told the bearers. After that, you'll have calls to make. I'm going to the gatehouse now to prepare. Most important is the visiting list. Leave out one name, and you've offended someone.” Wang nodded repeatedly, thoroughly satisfied. While Gao went to prepare the list and visiting cards, he took up his own brush in the private rooms. He wrote a detailed letter to He Guiqing at Jiangyin, describing his return to Hangzhou, his audience with Governor Huang, and his appointment as executive director of the Sea Transport Bureau.

By the time he finished, it was midnight. His wife personally brought him a light meal and urged him to bed. His head reached the pillow, but his mind would not rest. First he thought of engaging someone to handle his writing; then of the questions Treasurer Lingui might ask the next day. Would he choose his own date for taking office, or await instructions? By the time he had settled these matters in his mind, the clock was striking two. He rose after only three hours' sleep. Happiness had refreshed him so completely that he showed no sign of the short night. At the provincial treasury, he submitted his card and was admitted at once.

After Wang had knelt in thanks for the appointment and the customary greetings were over, Lingui spoke frankly. “You are the man the governor has sent me. I shall have much to rely on you for. There's no need to stand on ceremony. With the governor behind us, cooperation should be easy. You may decide some matters yourself.” Wang detected a strong note of resentment and quickly leaned forward. “I would not presume. Though the governor has favoured me, it is truly to Your Excellency that I owe my advancement. The distinction between superior and subordinate is part of the state's proper order. Naturally, I shall act on your instructions in everything.” “No, no!” Lingui waved his hands. “I'm not trying to keep you at a distance or shift responsibility. I mean what I say. This governor is difficult to serve, and grain transport is more difficult still. My predecessor lost his life over it, so I admit that I am wary. Nothing could be better than having you take this heavy burden of sea transport on your shoulders. As to how it is managed, you would do well to find out the governor's wishes. I can sit back, pass your submissions on, and approve rather than reject them. Wouldn't that suit everyone?” He was indeed speaking honestly. Wang concluded that Huang intended to control sea transport himself and had appointed Wang to act on his behalf. Lingui merely wished to avoid trouble. If the work went well, his name would appear in the recommendations for rewards; if badly, the governor above him would bear the burden. It was a sensible calculation.

Wang therefore said little, replying only with respect, “I am young and inexperienced. I shall look to Your Excellency for guidance in everything.” “I can hardly call it guidance. But since the sea-transport arrangements began under me, I can explain the situation to you.” “Yes, Your Excellency.” Wang straightened, leaned forward, and listened closely. “First, when do you intend to take up your duties?” “I await Your Excellency's instructions.” “The sooner the better.” Lingui called, “Attend me!” He sent an attendant for the imperial almanac and examined it. The day after next was auspicious for assuming office, so that date was chosen.

“One other question. How many men have you ready to bring with you?” Wang had no staff of his own. Had he obtained a magistracy, he would have asked Yu to find suitable men. He knew nothing of the Sea Transport Bureau's requirements and could not answer immediately. While hesitating, he suddenly remembered someone for whom he must reserve a position. “Only one man, surnamed Hu. He's extremely capable, though I don't know whether he'll agree to come.” “In that case, please do your best to retain the bureau's existing staff.” So that was it. Lingui feared that Wang would arrive with a group of his own followers to install and was making his wishes known in advance.

Wang thought the treasurer an honest enough man. “I shall follow Your Excellency's instructions.” He also left room for a possibility. “If the governor sends anyone to be placed, I shall report to you and discuss the arrangements.” “Very good.” Lingui turned to the business itself. “The change to sea transport for the grain of Jiangsu and Zhejiang is under the overall supervision of Ni Liangyao, recently appointed provincial treasurer of Jiangsu. You must be careful with him.” “Oh?” Wang paid particular attention. This was important. “It's fortunate that our governor enjoys imperial favour and has powerful backing. Otherwise, Ni might really have landed us in trouble.”

Ni had limited ability, and his management of the two provinces' sea shipments had not gone smoothly. Faced with stern imperial orders to hasten the work, he shifted responsibility onto Zhejiang, claiming that only sixty thousand-odd shi of its new grain had arrived. In fact, more than three hundred thousand shi had already reached Shanghai. Huang reported the facts, and an imperial edict sharply rebuked Ni. “With that grievance between us, the business will be difficult. Ni will be looking for faults at every opportunity. You must be careful. That's the first point.” “Yes. What is the second?” “Some places in Zhejiang are difficult too, especially Huzhou. The local gentry dominate affairs, and many large landholders owe grain taxes. This year's new grain must be dispatched early by imperial order, so the deadline is tighter than ever. The previous prefect was dismissed for delaying the shipments. I am now considering...”

Lingui suddenly stopped. What did he mean? Wang wondered. Was he thinking of appointing him acting prefect of Huzhou? That could not be right. Wang belonged to the magistrates' category and had never even acted in a substantive post. How could he be promoted out of nowhere? Perhaps he meant one of Huzhou's counties. That would be splendid. Of the prefecture's seven counties, Wucheng, Gui'an, and Deqing produced the most grain tax. All three were famously prosperous. To begin with a first-class county, serve for two or three years, and have someone above him offering support—then a prefecture might be within reach. “In short, there is Ni Liangyao outside the province and Huzhou within it. Handle those two well, and the rest will be easier. We can discuss other matters once you've taken over.” Lingui lifted his teacup to end the audience.

Leaving the treasury, Wang went straight to the governor's office. Liu greeted him with warm congratulations, then said, “His Excellency is discussing business with the intendants of the Hangzhou–Jiaxing–Huzhou and Ningbo–Shaoxing–Taizhou circuits. He may not have time to receive you. I'll go and see.” Huang was indeed consulting the two military circuit intendants about sending troops beyond the province to block the enemy. He had no time for Wang, but sent instructions through Liu: after taking over, Wang was to put the bureau in order without being constrained by other considerations. When a little time became available, Huang would summon him for a full discussion. The instruction not to be constrained plainly referred to Lingui. Taken together, the words of governor and treasurer showed Wang that, though nominally executive director, he had in fact assumed full responsibility for Zhejiang's grain shipments by sea.

“Let me tell you something privately,” Liu said, drawing him aside. “His Excellency has let it be known that, with military affairs so urgent, grain supplies concern the army's food, and the court is watching closely. If the sea shipments meet their deadlines, he can make a special recommendation for your exceptional promotion. Whether it brings you fortune or disaster is in your own hands.” “The governor thinks so highly of me that I scarcely know what to say. When you have a chance, please tell him that I shall not disappoint the confidence he has shown in promoting me.” Liu promised to convey the message. Then he discreetly handed Wang two biographical cards and smiled ingratiatingly. “One is my mother's brother; the other is my sworn brother. I hope Your Honour can find places for them.” “Certainly, certainly.” Wang agreed at once and put the cards away without reading them.

He then began making his official calls, following the list Gao had prepared in an order that avoided unnecessary travel. The garrison general, judicial commissioner, salt commissioner, Hangzhou–Jiaxing–Huzhou circuit intendant, and prefect of Hangzhou all counted as superiors and required formal visiting cards. The magistrates of Renhe and Qiantang were his equals and received ordinary cards. Beyond them were the expectant intendants, prefects, and magistrates. Most calls amounted only to leaving a card at the gate, since the host customarily declined to receive visitors. Even so, a whole day was not enough to finish. Back home, Wang sent specially to invite Yu to dinner. Over a little wine, he described the attitudes of the governor and treasurer. Yu was delighted for him. The executive directorship, he explained, would ordinarily go to an expectant circuit intendant, or at least an expectant prefect. For someone of Wang's standing, it was exceptional advancement. He should not regret failing to obtain a magistracy. This removed Wang's remaining disappointment. He asked about the procedures for taking office and requested a reliable man to handle his writing. Yu recommended a cousin surnamed Zhou, guaranteeing his diligence and trustworthiness. Wang gladly accepted and agreed to issue the formal letter of engagement the next day.

“There is something else on which I'd like your advice.” He showed Yu the two biographical cards. “These come through Liu Er at the governor's office. One is his uncle; the other his sworn brother.” “Uncle and brother indeed!” Yu laughed. “They've paid Liu for his help. The family relationships are merely a pretext.” “So that's it!” Wang had learned another lesson. “I suppose the older one is the uncle and the younger one the brother. How should I place them?” “Liu is an old fox of the first order. You cannot ignore him, but you shouldn't oblige him too readily either. If he thinks you easy to deceive, he'll keep asking for more and cause endless trouble.” Yu decided the matter for him. Both men held official qualifications below the recognised ranks. The younger, with more energy, could be made a transport escort officer; the older, better suited to sedentary work, could assist with incoming and outgoing correspondence. The arrangement was so appropriate that Wang accepted it with complete satisfaction.

Taking office and receiving congratulations occupied two or three noisy days. Only then could he settle down to work. His first task was to examine the correspondence and case files. He now discovered that Huang's report that more than three hundred thousand shi had reached Shanghai and been delivered to Ni was not entirely accurate. Most of the grain was still aboard canal boats and had not been handed over. Any mishap would carry serious responsibility. The shipments had to be hurried forward.

While he was pondering the difficulty, Huang sent a civil affairs officer with a message: “There is urgent official business. His Honour Wang is to come at once.” On arrival, Wang first knelt to thank the governor for his favour. Huang received the gesture without protest. As soon as Wang rose, he handed him a document. “Read this imperial edict first.” Wang knew that an imperial instruction transmitted through the Grand Council was called a court dispatch. He had often heard of such documents, but this was the first he had seen. The supposedly resplendent words of the sovereign were written on ordinary folded xuan paper, five lines to a page and twenty characters to a line. There was neither a seal nor a signature. Without the envelope bearing the Grand Council's seal, he could scarcely have believed that such an unremarkable sheet was an imperial edict. Thinking this, he read it through while holding it respectfully in both hands. His memory was good, and one reading fixed the whole content in his mind.

Once again, the edict urged haste in the grain shipments. The court had grown impatient with Ni Liangyao's repeated complaints that his appointed agents were ineffective and rebuked him sharply. It concluded: “The provincial treasurer shall, in accordance with the repeated imperial commands, expedite the grain received from Zhejiang together with that of Jiangsu still awaiting dispatch, ensuring that the vessels put to sea by the prescribed date. Should there be the slightest further delay, I shall punish Ni Liangyao severely.” “I have also received a letter from the capital,” Huang said. “Since Yangzhou fell, the defending commanders have proposed opening the sluices to drain the Huai waters and prevent the rebels' boats from moving east. If they reach Gaoyou and Baoying, the embankments of Lake Hongze may be breached to drown them. The whole region would become a sea, the people would drown with the enemy, and this year's grain would be lost as well. That's why the demand for sea shipments is now so urgent. If Ni fails again, he'll certainly lose his rank. We in Zhejiang must consider our position too.” Wang listened closely. At the last sentence, he understood everything. Zhejiang had not actually delivered its full quota. If Ni were dismissed and investigated, he would have nothing left to lose and might reveal all the facts. Huang's inaccurate report would then expose him to serious trouble.

There was only one sound course: get the rice to Shanghai as quickly as possible and have the seagoing vessels accept the full quantity. Wang said so, but Huang showed no reaction. No reaction was itself a reaction: displeasure. Wang thought that nothing short of a guarantee to deliver everything within five or ten days would satisfy him. Yet he could not honestly offer one. He could only reply, “I'll send agents out tonight to press the shipments forward. I shall neglect nothing, however small.” “That will have to do.” Huang spoke coolly, lifted his teacup, rose before his guest, gave a slight bow, and went inside. Wang was deeply discouraged. Only a few days into the job, his first working audience had ended like this. He felt not merely hurt but chilled. A superior who treated his subordinates in such a manner would indeed be difficult to serve.

Sitting in his chair, he brooded. The proud pleasure with which he had looked about on his first rides a few days earlier had vanished. Lost in thought, he scarcely noticed where they were passing. Then, through his distracted state, a figure suddenly caught his eye. Stamping his foot, he shouted, “Stop the chair! Stop!” The bearers, moving at a brisk pace, had no idea what was wrong. They tried desperately to halt but carried on for several steps before they could stop. Gao, walking beside the chair with the document folder under his arm, hurried to the front. His master had already lifted the curtain and thrust out his head, staring wide-eyed back along the street. The sudden commotion drew curious passers-by to a halt. Seeing them watching from a distance, Gao felt that his master's dignity was at risk and called softly, “Your Honour!”

At the sight of him, Wang said, “Quick! The man in the black lined robe—stop him!” There were plenty of men in black lined robes. What sort of man did he mean? Tall or short, stout or thin, and how old? Gao needed at least some description before he could search. While he hesitated, Wang lost patience. He beat on the carrying pole, demanding that the bearers lower the chair so he could leap out and pursue the man himself. This was still less seemly. Gao cried repeatedly, “Your Honour, your dignity! Who is it? Tell me, and I'll find him.” “Who else? Young Master Hu!” “Ah!” Gao set off at a run. He had heard his master describe Young Master Hu many times and already had a vivid picture of him in his mind. He ran along, examining the passers-by. One man wore a black robe but had a grey beard. Another was approaching middle age and had an unprepossessing appearance. He did not seem right either, but Gao asked his surname just in case. It was not Hu. Gao grew anxious and puzzled. He trusted that, though his master had only caught a passing glimpse, he could not have mistaken Hu Xueyan. Where could the man have gone in these few moments?
