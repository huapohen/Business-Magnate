CHAPTER THREE

As Gao hesitated, looking this way and that and wondering what to do, he suddenly spotted the man. The fellow eating at the improvised counter must be him. Hangzhou's eating houses still preserved customs from the Song dynasties. Upstairs were comfortable private seats; downstairs, cooked dishes were sold from large red-lacquered platters laid out on doors taken from their frames and used as counters. Long benches stood in front, where pedlars and labourers sat together for what was called a “door-plank meal.” A bowl of rice arrived heaped as high as a little pagoda. Anyone unused to it hardly knew where to put his chopsticks: should he begin at the summit or halfway down? The men in short working clothes therefore stared when this customer in a long gown sat down. A gentleman dining in such a place was unusual enough; more than that, they wanted to see how he would manage the rice. If he did not know the trick, the top would collapse. They were waiting for the joke.

Gao had reached him by then. Studying his profile, he felt almost certain and ventured a call: “Young Master Hu!” The men in working clothes laughed. What young master would come here for a door-plank meal? Though new to Hangzhou, Gao had already learned something of local ways. He realised that his form of address was ill chosen. Even if this was Hu, he might be embarrassed to answer. Gao therefore approached and asked, “Excuse me, is your surname Hu?” “Yes. What of it?” “And is your courtesy name Xueyan?” It was indeed Hu Xueyan. Setting down the bamboo chopsticks he had just picked up, he said, “I am Hu Xueyan, but I don't believe we've met...”

His faded clothes and unshaven face told Gao that Young Master Hu had fallen on hard times, which explained his choice of eating place. A public reunion in the street would make a story, and might not sound well for his master's official reputation. He therefore avoided naming Wang Youling. “My master's surname is Wang. You'll know him as soon as you see him. There's no need to eat here, Young Master Hu. Let me take you to him.” Without waiting for an answer, he put a handful of copper coins on the counter with one hand and helped Hu step over the bench with the other. Then he beckoned to a small hired sedan chair waiting nearby. Hu was bewildered and refused to get in. Catching Gao's arm, he asked, “Which Mr Wang is your master?” “He is...” Gao lowered his voice. “His official name is Youling.” “Ah!” Hu's eyes lit up. “It's him! Where is he now?” “His residence is in Qinghefang. Please get into the chair.” Once Hu was seated, Gao gave the bearers the address. As soon as the little chair moved away, he hurried back to Wang and briefly explained what had happened. Wang was overjoyed. He too climbed into his blue-cloth chair and urged the bearers to hurry.

The two chairs reached the Wang residence almost together. Gao had run ahead and ordered the central gates opened, so both were carried straight to the hall. When the friends stepped out and faced each other, each felt as though he were dreaming. Wang in particular was overwhelmed by the sight of Hu's poverty and desperation. His nose stung and his eyes grew hot. “Xueyan!” “Xuexuan!” After those greetings, neither could speak. Countless things crowded their throats and weighed upon their hearts, but with only one mouth apiece, neither knew what to say first.

Gao, standing beside them, had to intervene. “Perhaps Your Honour would take Young Master Hu into the reception room.” “Ah!” Wang came to himself. “Come, Xueyan. Sit down and rest before we talk. We needn't stay out here. Let's go to the back, where it's more comfortable.” He led Hu into the rear hall. Mrs Wang, who had been watching from behind a screen, hurried away. Catching sight of her moving skirt, Hu hesitated. This reminded Wang of something else. “My dear!” he called loudly. “Come and meet this brother of mine.” Their bond went beyond ordinary family friendship; they were truly like brothers. Mrs Wang therefore came out without embarrassment. Smiling, she pointed to Hu but spoke to her husband. “So this is the Young Master Hu you've been thinking of by day and dreaming of by night!”

“I hardly deserve that title.” Hu bowed deeply. She returned the bow and spoke with feeling. “Young Master Hu, we really don't know how to thank you. As soon as Xuexuan came back to Hangzhou, he went to look for you. He returned without finding you and sighed and fretted, not knowing what to do. I blamed him: how could anyone have so good a friend without asking where he lived? Now all is well. Where did you meet?” “In... in the street.” Hu looked embarrassed. Wang's first rush of emotion had subsided a little. He hurried his wife along. “My dear, we couldn't finish all we have to say in three days and nights. Leave the questions for now. Neither of us has eaten. See what is ready and bring a few dishes so we can have a drink.” “Certainly.” She smiled. “Take Young Master Hu to the study. It's quiet there.”

“Just so!” Wang led Hu to the study. His wife soon followed with the maid and older servant to look after them herself. Surrounded by such warmth, Hu felt that even the hardship and insecurity of the past half-year had been worth enduring. “Xuexuan,” he asked, “when did you return?” “Less than a month ago.” Wang was thoroughly content with his own circumstances, but the sight of Hu saddened him. “Xueyan, how did you come to this?” “It's a long story.” Hu hesitated. “What about you? You seem to have done very well.”

“And whom have I to thank but you? I've had one extraordinary encounter after another. That tale in Strange Stories Old and New about the unlucky man whose fortune turns through Dongting oranges is nothing beside my experience.” He paused, then exclaimed, “Well, now that I've found you, everything can be put right. Come! Today we drink until we're drunk.” Four dishes and two sets of cups and chopsticks had been laid on a square table nearby. When they sat down, Mrs Wang wrapped a cloth around the wine pot and poured for them herself. Hu hurriedly protested at the honour. “My dear,” Wang said, “drink a toast to my brother, then go and attend to the kitchen. Otherwise he'll feel obliged to keep standing on ceremony and won't be at ease.” She toasted Hu and withdrew, leaving a maid to serve them.

As they ate, Wang told his story, from meeting He Guiqing in Tongzhou to his appointment at the Sea Transport Bureau, filling in his own family background along the way. By the time he finished, the wine had gone cold. “Congratulations!” Hu's face was flushed with drink, and every trace of his defeated appearance had vanished. He smiled proudly. “My judgement was right after all. I could see that your bad luck was ending and your good fortune about to begin. And so it did.” “My good fortune began when I met you.” Wang looked ashamed. “Xueyan, no wonder my wife calls me muddle-headed. You did me such an immense kindness, yet I never even learned where you lived. Today you must tell me all about yourself.”

“Of course I shall.” Hu drank, planted both hands squarely on the corners of the table, and tilted his head slightly. “Xuexuan, what sort of man do you take me for?” Considering his manner and recalling his earlier impressions in the teahouse, Wang supposed that Hu must be the son of an official family, but one who had lowered his ambitions and preferred idling in teahouses to serious study. Naturally, he could not voice that judgement so bluntly. “Brother, don't be offended,” he said. “I took you for a well-born young idler.” “An idler?” Hu laughed. “You might have called me a liaogui'er instead!” That was the Hangzhou expression for a street loafer or local ruffian. “Then I can't guess. Tell me the truth. I'm terribly anxious to know.” “Very well. I learned my trade in a private bank...”

Hu's father had died, leaving the family poor, and he had entered a private bank as an apprentice while still a boy. In Hangzhou, such apprentices were called “trade students.” He began with sweeping floors and emptying chamber pots. But he was extraordinarily quick, a shrewd judge of people, persuasive in speech, and generous in his dealings. When his three-year apprenticeship ended, he immediately became one of the bank's most useful employees. At first he worked at the counter. Later, having won the confidence of both proprietor and manager, he was sent out to collect debts. He had never made a mistake.

The previous summer, when he learned from Wang that he held a salt-station superintendent's qualification and wanted to go north to register and purchase a higher one, Hu happened to have a debt to collect. It was exactly five hundred taels. The account had gone bad, and the bank had already accepted the loss and written it off. Any recovery would be an unexpected gain. No one else could collect it. The debtor had the backing of a Green Standard officer, and if he refused to pay, the bank preferred to avoid trouble rather than challenge him. But he and Hu got on well. Hu heard that the man had somehow come into money and went to see him. The debtor said that anyone else would have been refused, but Hu was a different matter. He readily fixed a date to settle the debt in full. Moved by sympathy for Wang's wasted abilities, Hu decided to help him. Since the bank had already treated the money as irrecoverable, he reasoned, he could lend it on to Wang. If Wang repaid it later, all the better. If not, the bank would be no worse off than before. There was a certain logic in the argument. Had he acted quietly, nobody would have known, and nobody would have thought to inquire for some time. The trouble was that he disclosed everything. He even drew up a promissory note in Wang's name and delivered it to the manager responsible for the bank's business.

Entrusted with the proprietor's affairs, how could the manager accept such unilateral reasoning? In view of Hu's past service, he did not pursue Hu's guarantor, but he told him to pack his bedding and leave. Word spread through the trade. Everyone said Hu was recklessly presumptuous. Luckily, this had been only five hundred taels. What if it had been five thousand or fifty thousand? If he took such liberties, might he not ruin an entire firm? With that reputation, no bank would employ him, although everyone knew his abilities. Some also suspected that, given his fondness for gambling, he had lost money and invented the story to cover its misappropriation. Once this further accusation spread, earning a living became harder still. “Thank heaven you've finally returned,” Hu said, as though relieved of a heavy burden. “Whatever happens about the money, your present position will at least enable me to clear my name.”

With moist eyes, Wang sighed deeply. “If we hadn't met again today, who would have imagined that I had unknowingly caused you so much suffering? Such kindness is beyond thanks. Tell me what I should do.” “That's for you to decide. How can I say?” “No, no!” Realising that he had expressed himself badly, Wang hurried to explain. “That's not what I mean. Your affairs are mine. I know how to restore your reputation fully. What I want to ask is what you intend to do next. Would you like to return to the bank?” Hu shook his head and quoted a Hangzhou saying: “Dried bean curd put back in the broth has lost its flavour.”

“Then do you want to set up on your own?” That was exactly what Hu wanted. But as he was about to admit it, another thought stopped him. Opening a bank was no simple matter. It required capital and powerful support. Wang had only just obtained an appointment; his resources were still limited. If Hu declared his ambition, Wang's loyalty and gratitude would compel him to do everything possible to help. He would take on a burden too heavy for him and be crushed beneath it. How could Hu allow that? Alarmed by the possibility, he changed his answer. “I don't want to earn my living in banking any more. Your bureau must employ quite a few people. Just find me some easy post where I can draw a salary without much work.” Wang smiled happily and imitated the Hangzhou accent. “There'll be no money for sitting idle!” Hu understood. Wang would give him an important position at the bureau. Then he could use his abilities to help his friend. Once Wang had prospered through that help, Hu could borrow a few thousand taels as capital with a clear conscience.

When they had eaten and drunk their fill, two bowls of excellent Longjing tea were brought in, and the conversation continued. Wang described Huang's character, recounted Chunshou's case as news, and spoke of the governor's difficult temper. From there, he turned to the audience he had attended that day. “What do you intend to do now?” Hu asked. He meant how Wang would get the outstanding grain to Shanghai quickly enough to complete delivery. “What can I do? Only press them as hard as possible.” “That's difficult.” Hu shook his head. “You officials have no idea what other people suffer. Once transport shifts to the sea, the canal boatmen lose their livelihood. To speak frankly, the associations would be delighted if the scheme failed. You want them to carry the rice to Shanghai by canal. You're in a hurry; they aren't. They'll drag things out until you miss the deadline. Then you'll discover how much power they have.” “Ah!” Wang sat bolt upright. “Then we're certain to miss it. What can be done?”

“There's always a way.” Hu tapped his temple. “Nothing in the world is beyond a solution; the difficulty is getting people to think. I have a plan that will save you a great deal of trouble. It'll cost a little extra, but that's money well spent if it saves the governor's rank.” Wang was doubtful, but might as well hear him out. He nodded. “Let's hear this excellent plan of yours.” “Rice is rice, wherever it comes from. Make up the shortage where it's needed. Buy rice in Shanghai, deliver the full quota, and the problem is solved.” Before he had finished, Wang sprang up in delight. “Excellent! Excellent! That's what we'll do.” “But there's one condition: not a word must leak out. This is a large quantity of rice. If the merchants hear of it, they'll immediately raise their prices. Too great a difference, and the business becomes difficult.”

“Yes.” Wang collected himself and considered the matter. “Xueyan, do you hold any official or scholarly qualification?” “I'm a commoner of the first rank.” “You ought to purchase a title. Even one below the regular ranks would make you an official of sorts and simplify your work. For now, I can only issue a letter of engagement...” Wang hesitated. “And I'm not even sure whether I can appoint you as a secretary.” “One thing at a time,” Hu said quickly, anxious not to embarrass him.

“How can it wait? If you're to help me, you need some recognised position.” Wang frowned. “There are too many loose ends. I suppose we must deal with them one by one. Xueyan, who is there in your family?” “My mother and my wife.” “Then I must pay my respects to your mother...” “No, no!” Hu hastily stopped him. “Not just now. The lane where I live is too narrow for a sedan chair, and there's nowhere fit to sit inside. A visit now would expose my poverty rather than do me honour. Another time.”

Knowing that Hu meant it, Wang let the matter drop. He rose. “Sit here a moment. I'll be back.” When he returned, he held a bank draft for fifty taels and simply told Hu to use it for the present. Hu accepted without ceremony, rose to leave, and promised to return the next day. “I won't keep you today. Come to the bureau first thing tomorrow; I'll be waiting especially for you. And one more thing: leave me your address.” Hu lived in Yuanbao Street and supplied the details. Wang then ordered Gao to prepare four kinds of carefully chosen gifts and take them there to pay his respects to Madam Hu, with a card identifying himself humbly as her nephew by family friendship.

Gao returned from delivering the presents in high spirits. Poor as Hu was, he remained remarkably open-handed and had given Gao a gratuity of four taels. “I tried to refuse; it was far too much,” Gao told his master. “But Young Master Hu insisted. He said he was only being generous with someone else's money.” “Then you should accept it.” Wang thought that, with Hu's ability and temperament, any opportunity would lead him rapidly to success. His own future might depend greatly on this man. He must make good use of him.

Early the next morning Hu arrived as promised, splendidly dressed. Gao, already instructed to wait for him, took him straight to Wang's private office. Wang asked, “Where is that bank?” “In the Lower City, at Salt Bridge. It's called Xinhe.” “Come with me. You handled the original transaction, so tell me how the note was worded. I don't want my account to contradict it.” Hu reflected, then recited slowly: “I, Wang Youling, expectant salt-station superintendent, acknowledge borrowing from Xinhe Bank, through the intermediary Hu Xueyan, five hundred taels of full-standard silver by the treasury weight, for necessary expenses in travelling to the capital to register. Repayment is agreed within two years, with interest at the prevailing market rate. Since a verbal statement is insufficient proof, this note is issued as a record.”

“How much interest would that make?” “It depends on the availability of money; there is no fixed rate,” Hu said. “At the high end, one point two per cent a month; at the low end, seven-tenths. If we take one per cent throughout, ten months would make about fifty taels.” Wang wrote an order authorising an advance of six hundred taels against official expenses and sent Gao to the accounts office. Soon the accounts clerk came himself with men carrying the silver: six official ingots, each weighing a hundred taels, newly received from the provincial treasury. Fresh from the mould and never used, they gleamed brilliantly. “Shall we go to Xinhe together?” “In that case, I'd better stay here,” Hu replied. “If I go, the manager will be embarrassed in front of me. There's something else: say a few good words about Xinhe, and don't mention that we've already met.”

This deepened Wang's understanding of his friend. Hu was generous-hearted—or at the very least, remarkably graceful in his methods. Who else would willingly surrender such a chance to hold his head high before those who had dismissed him? Yet Hu was prepared to deny himself that satisfaction in order to preserve another man's dignity. What breadth of mind! For that reason, Wang abandoned his own plan to put on formal robes and arrive with gongs and shouted commands to frighten the bank. Instead, he changed into ordinary clothes, took a small chair, wrapped the six ingots in a cloth bundle, placed them inside, and went quietly to Xinhe with Gao.

As soon as the chair stopped, Gao went ahead with the visiting card. Banks were exceptionally well informed about official affairs. Xinhe's manager, known as Fat Zhang, recognised the name of a man favoured by the governor. The name Wang Youling also sounded familiar in another connection. A moment's thought brought the memory back—and broke him into a sweat. There was nothing to do but receive the visitor and see what happened. By the time Zhang reached the entrance, Wang had stepped out of his chair. Zhang saluted, welcomed him into the reception room, and busied himself with tea, a water pipe, and the seat of honour. Smiling deferentially, he asked, “To what do we owe the honour of your visit? What instructions may we carry out?” Wang removed his dark spectacles and answered calmly, “You have a man here surnamed Hu. Please ask him to come and see me.” “Oh, you mean Hu Xueyan? He is no longer with us. Whatever Your Honour requires, you may tell me instead.” After a pause, Wang said, “May I ask your surname?” “Zhang, sir. Everyone calls me Fat Zhang. My employer has entrusted me with the business, so I have some authority in all the firm's affairs.” “Good.” Wang turned to Gao. “Bring in the silver.” Then he addressed Zhang again. “Your bank kindly lent me money last year. I've come to settle the matter.”

“There's no hurry at all. Please keep it for your use.” “That would hardly do. Repay one loan, and another can be obtained when needed. I know your firm has ample capital and an excellent reputation, and that this sum is of little concern to you. But I would prefer to settle it sooner rather than later. Please calculate the principal and interest and bring out the original note.” It was precisely the thought of that note that had made Zhang sweat. He could not produce it. Having regarded it as worthless, he no longer knew where it had gone. A banker dealt with officials, gentry, and wealthy merchants, and depended upon flexibility in social dealings. Zhang thought quickly. Without the note, he could hardly collect the debt. The man who had tied the knot must untie it: only by finding Hu could he hope for a satisfactory solution. For now, he might as well appear generous.

He bowed deeply and offered extravagant praise. “Your Honour is a gentleman of the highest benevolence. A client as kind as you is a blessing our little firm could scarcely hope for. How could we turn the God of Wealth away? Please keep the money until Xueyan returns. Meanwhile, if your bureau needs remittance services, we have dealings with the Big Three in Shanghai's southern city: Daheng, Dayu, and Dafeng. They know the sand-junk association extremely well. Freight payments for the sea grain shipments could be remitted through us to them with the greatest convenience, and our charges would be very modest. Why not allow us to serve you?” He did not understand the arrangements: Zhejiang did not pay the shipowners directly for sea freight. But Wang saw no need to explain that. He continued to insist on repaying the five hundred taels. Zhang repeatedly refused, until the exchange finally drove him to tell half the truth. “To be frank, Your Honour, we considered the loan absolutely secure, so the note itself hardly mattered. I don't know where it was put. Once we find it, we can come to collect. As for interest, that needn't be mentioned. A bank considers who its customer is when calculating interest. Your Honour will have many opportunities to favour us in future. If I charged you this little sum, my employer would certainly blame me.”

The speech was handsome enough. Remembering Hu's wishes, Wang decided to be more gracious still. He had Gao unwrap the bundle and lay five hundred and fifty taels on the table. Then he said calmly, “I am already much indebted to you. How could I refuse to pay interest? Your Mr Hu told me that the rate varied with the money market, from seven-tenths to one point two per cent a month. Let us take one per cent throughout. For ten months, that makes fifty taels. Here are five hundred and fifty in all. Please accept them and give me a simple receipt stating that principal and interest are paid in full. When you find my original note, kindly destroy it. I admire the consideration your firm shows its clients. My bureau handles substantial public funds. When Mr Hu returns, ask him to come and discuss a lasting business relationship between us.” Overjoyed, Zhang immediately wrote a receipt and gave it to Gao. He still protested against taking interest, but Wang insisted, leaving him only to accept with repeated thanks.

After Zhang had respectfully seen him into his chair, Wang felt that the whole affair had been both satisfying and amusing. He smiled inwardly. Eager for the bureau's business, Zhang would send someone to find Hu as soon as he left. Little did he know that Hu had already decided never to return to the bank. Letting him enjoy an empty promise for a while was a small punishment for having dismissed Hu. Back at the bureau, Wang told Hu what had happened. Hu feared that Xinhe might send someone to his home and discover the truth, making refusal awkward. He hurried back to prepare for that possibility. Wang changed into official robes and went to see the governor. Afraid of being denied an audience, he specifically told Liu Er that he had found an excellent solution to the grain-delivery problem and wished to explain it in person.

Liu was grateful that Wang had suitably placed both men whose cards he had submitted. He therefore spoke especially well of him inside. At the words “an excellent solution,” Huang granted an immediate audience, and his expression was entirely changed. When Wang explained Hu's scheme of substitution, Huang became very excited. But the matter was too important for an immediate decision. He sent attendants to summon the provincial treasurer and grain intendant for a secret consultation in the western reception room. Eager to complete the official business, all supported Wang's proposal, though not without reservations. “We have already reported to the throne that all the grain has reached Shanghai,” Lingui said. “If we now buy rice there to make up the shortage, and that censor gets wind of it and decides to have a little fun...” He hesitated. “It would be no laughing matter.” “The treasurer is right,” the grain intendant agreed. Glancing at Wang, he added as though speaking to himself, “It would help if someone could stand in front of the affair.”

He meant that someone should handle it openly while the senior officials professed ignorance. If it came to light, they would have room to evade responsibility or negotiate a way out. Both governor and treasurer understood. But who would put himself in that position? Their eyes drifted towards Wang. Without hesitation, he said, “I owe my advancement to Your Excellencies. Having been entrusted with sea transport, I cannot evade the responsibility. May the matter be left to me?” All three superiors immediately showed their approval. Huang spoke slowly. “The grain tax supplies the imperial storehouses. In wartime, provisions are the army's lifeblood, and expedient measures cannot be avoided. Since Brother Youling is willing to assume responsibility, you may discuss the details here.” With that, he rose and withdrew to the inner rooms. The governor appeared to have removed himself from the business. Mindful of Chunshou's fate, Lingui too aimed above all to leave himself a way to disclaim responsibility. The grain intendant, however, showed some willingness to act and worked carefully with Wang on the details.

The key was to find a large Shanghai grain merchant who would first supply a quantity of unpolished rice for delivery to Ni Liangyao, then receive Zhejiang's grain when it arrived. In other words, the merchant would sell first and buy later. Naturally, the purchase and sale prices would differ, as would the quality of the rice. Grain-tax rice was of very poor quality, so compensation would be needed, together with an allowance for handling losses. The source of that money had to be agreed in advance. “At this stage, there is no alternative,” the grain intendant said. “We shall have to add something to the collection of this year's new grain. For the moment, the provincial treasury must advance the money.”

“The treasury can advance it,” Lingui replied, “but you must consider my position too. I truly cannot bear this responsibility. I need an official instruction from the governor before releasing funds.” “A written instruction will probably be impossible. A definite verbal undertaking should be obtainable. We're all in the same boat now. Rest assured: if anything goes wrong later, I shall bear witness.” With matters put so plainly, Lingui could only nod. “Then that will have to do.” “As for what follows,” the grain intendant said to Wang with clasped hands, “we must leave the work to you.”

Wang found that harder to accept. He knew little of Shanghai. With Jiangning lost and everyone anxious, supplying so much grain in advance would entail great risk. He had no idea whether a merchant would undertake it. Seeing his hesitation, the intendant reassured him. “There is no need to be afraid. As I said, our interests are bound together. If difficulties arise, we shall all find a solution. We won't leave you alone in trouble. You have only just begun, Brother Wang, yet your ability is already evident. I admire it. Go ahead with confidence.” Encouraged by these words, Wang thought of Hu. The man who deserved admiration was someone else. The matter was now settled, and they agreed to pursue their separate tasks. Lingui and the grain intendant would see Huang about advancing the price difference and accounting for it later. Wang would immediately find a merchant willing to supply the many tens of thousands of shi of rice required.

Upon returning to the bureau, his first question was for Hu. He was told that Hu had not come back since going home. It was nearly noon, so presumably he would come after lunch. Yet three o'clock arrived with no sign of him. Wang grew anxious. There was much to discuss, and Hu should know it. Why was he taking his time so calmly? He had not imagined that Fat Zhang had detained him. Wang's unexpected visit had become the favourite topic of everyone at Xinhe. Hu had always been popular there. When he was dismissed, however, his conduct had seemed too outrageous and the trouble too great for anyone to help. Later, ashamed of his poverty, Hu avoided old acquaintances, and the ties had weakened. Now memories revived. Everyone said his judgement was exceptional, his methods and courage superior to other men's. Quite apart from the bureau connection and the important client it might bring, his abilities alone made him someone Xinhe ought to invite back if it wished to prosper. This strengthened Zhang's resolve. He had felt some guilt already. Hearing everyone's praise now added a personal concern. If their remarks reached the proprietor, Zhang would certainly be questioned. Even leaving everything else aside, how could he explain losing a note for five hundred taels? The proprietor had another bank in Shaoxing that needed a senior clerk. He might transfer Zhang there and make room for Hu. What would become of Zhang's dignity then?

He therefore found a young apprentice who knew Hu's address and set out in person. His plan was ready. Hu's salary of four taels a month would be paid from the date of his departure: forty taels for ten months. Zhang had a bank note made out for that amount and put in a red envelope. With gifts of tea and ham, he went to call. By chance, as he approached from one end of Yuanbao Street, Hu was returning from the bureau at the other. They met face to face. Hu saw him quickly and wanted to avoid him, but it was too late.

“Xueyan! Xueyan!” Zhang ran up, panting, his face red and heart pounding. That was useful: it concealed his embarrassment. “Mr Zhang!” Hu greeted him respectfully. “Have you been well?” “Well?” Zhang sounded almost reproachful. “Since you left, I've been like a man who has lost his right hand. Nothing goes smoothly.” Hu knew his powers of flattery. A few words from Zhang could make a man so pleased with himself that he became easy to manipulate. Hu therefore smiled without answering. “Xueyan!” Zhang examined him from head to foot. “You're doing rather well.” “Thanks to your good wishes.”

Hu did not invite him inside. Zhang therefore scolded the apprentice. “You fool! Take the tea and ham into the house.” As the boy moved towards the gate, Zhang followed. “It's my first visit to your mother. I must bring a little something. Fresh tea and aged ham—a small token of my own respect.” Hu had no choice but to invite him in. Zhang insisted on paying his respects to both Hu's mother and wife. Ordinary families observed less strict separation between men and women than official households, so both women came out, exchanged greetings, and heard many pleasant words from him.

Once they were seated, Zhang came to the point. He described Wang's unexpected visit and repayment in detail, omitting only the loss of the original note. Then he turned from events to his feelings. “Xueyan, can you guess what pleased me most about His Honour's visit?” “The chance to secure an important customer, naturally.” That was precisely right, but Zhang would not admit it. “No. I'm not saying this to flatter you or make you grateful. The truth is that our employer was furious about that affair. As manager, I had no choice but to enforce the rules. All the while, I hoped Mr Wang would soon repay the money—or that I might come into a little fortune and cover the five hundred taels for you myself. Why? Simply so that you could return to Xinhe. Well, no more talk.” He produced the red envelope. “Here are ten months' back wages: a note for forty taels. Keep it safe. Come along!” As he spoke, he pressed the envelope into Hu's hand with his left and seized him with his right to lead him out.

“Wait a moment, Mr Zhang. I still don't understand what's happening. Where are we going?” “Where else? Xinhe.” Hu had asked deliberately. Once Zhang stated it plainly, he shook his head firmly. “A good horse doesn't turn back to graze the same patch. I appreciate your kindness, but no, thank you.” He handed the envelope back. Zhang pushed it away with both hands. “Now, Xueyan, that isn't right of you...” A long campaign of persuasion followed. Zhang's eloquence met Hu's resolve. However he appealed to reason or feeling, Hu would not yield.

By midday, the household invited him to stay for lunch, partly hoping that the offer would prompt him to leave. But Zhang had resolved to stake everything on success and would not let go. “Excellent! It's been so long since we talked properly. I'll add something to the meal.” He took out a piece of silver and shouted to the apprentice, “You little scamp! Go to the eating house at the end of the lane. Have them send four dishes: mulang tofu, sliced pork, crisp bean-curd rolls, and mixed meat and vegetables. And fetch two jin of Zhuyeqing wine.” Hu and his wife could not stop him. Once the wine was served, Hu could no longer remain silently on the defensive while Zhang did all the talking. He had to invent one reason after another for refusing. But Zhang's tongue was formidable. Like the magical duels in The Investiture of the Gods, every weapon Hu produced was met by a counterspell. There was one argument Zhang could not answer, but Hu was unwilling to use it. If he revealed his relationship with Wang and his forthcoming work at the bureau, could Zhang possibly insist that he return to stand behind a bank counter?

More wine was brought, and the conversation grew longer. Even Hu's wife began to lose patience. Just as the situation seemed impossible to resolve, an unexpected visitor arrived. “Oh!” Zhang stared. “Second Master Gao! How did you find your way here?” Sent to fetch Hu, Gao was quick-witted but could not answer immediately. Something in Zhang's tone seemed strange. Unsure what lay behind it, he dared not announce his purpose and merely looked at Hu. The truth could no longer be concealed—and perhaps need not be. Hu decided to use his final argument. But it would take time to explain. First he must settle Gao's business, then tell the story at leisure.

“Have you eaten?” he asked warmly. “There's food and wine ready. Sit down and have a cup.” “Thank you, but I wouldn't presume. When will you be free, Young Master Hu?” “I understand.” Hu glanced at the clock on the table. “I'll be there at four precisely.” “Then please come to the residence for dinner.” With his message delivered, Gao left. Hu smiled apologetically. “To tell you the truth, Mr Zhang, I had already seen Mr Wang before he visited Xinhe. I had reasons for not accompanying him, though there's no need to discuss them now. He has invited me to help at the Sea Transport Bureau, and I've agreed. So I cannot return to my old home. You must understand my difficulty.”

“Ah!” Zhang drew out the sound, opening his mouth in a display of astonished delight. “Congratulations, Xueyan! You truly are a carp that has leapt the Dragon Gate.” “Even after leaping the gate, I remain a carp. A man mustn't forget his beginnings. Banking is the trade I learned, and everyone in it is family to me. I shall still rely on your help.” “Not at all. It's we who must rely on you now.” Zhang suddenly lowered his voice. “In fact, there's something you can do at once. I told Mr Wang we'd like to do business with the bureau. Money is plentiful at present, and it's a pity to leave it idle. See if you can find a way to put ours to use. We'll be particularly generous about the commission.” “Very well.” Hu nodded seriously. “I'll bear it in mind.”

Zhang left pleased that the visit had not been wasted. Hu then hurried to Wang's residence. His account of Zhang's expressions and speeches made both men clap their hands and laugh. They found the affair thoroughly satisfying. “Enough of that. There's important business to discuss.” Wang related his audience with the governor and the consultation with the treasurer and grain intendant, then asked what should be done.

“It is a little complicated. But merchants seek profit. If the return is sufficient, they'll lick blood from a blade. Someone will accept the risk. What matters is having a guarantee.” “What sort of guarantee?” “An official undertaking from Zhejiang would be best, naturally. I doubt we can obtain that, so we must find another way. There will be one. First, how much grain must be advanced?” “I checked the accounts. The shortage is 145,000 shi.” “That isn't such a large amount,” Hu said. “I'll arrange for a bank to guarantee payment. That should put the grain merchant at ease.” “Excellent. Through Xinhe?” “Xinhe can arrange it with a Shanghai bank. That can certainly be done. But we must have a definite word from the governor. I advise you to see him directly, rather than let the message pass through others.”

“Well...” Wang was not convinced. “Since the treasurer and grain intendant are asking for instructions, they'll surely give me a definite answer. Another visit seems unnecessary.” “There's another reason.” Hu lowered his voice. “The governor may have further instructions—some expense he wants included, for example. He can't conveniently raise it himself, still less discuss it with the treasurer. He needs you to understand and provide an opening. Then he can tell you what he wants.” “Ah!” Wang understood and nodded repeatedly. “There's more. You must make arrangements at the treasury and the grain intendant's office too. Even if the men themselves are honest, their subordinates will all be envious. Who won't suppose this assignment offers you a chance to fill your pockets? Without some benefit, they're certain to make difficulties.” Wang was more astonished than ever. “I would never have thought of it. Xueyan, you're an expert at official life!” “The principles of being an official and doing business are the same.”

Wang almost laughed. On reflection, however, blunt as the remark was, it went straight to the truth. This affair had begun by taking a side path. Having chosen it, they might as well follow it through. So long as Zhejiang's full grain quota was delivered and the state's needs were not delayed, everything else could be arranged. If they faltered halfway and trouble arose, their superiors might stand above them, but Wang was the man acting openly. He would bear the first and heaviest blow. Seen in that light, Hu's advice was truly worth its weight in gold. He was a man Wang could not do without. “Xueyan, here's what I propose. I'll apply to purchase a title for you at once. Once you have the official receipt, no one can deny you're an official. Your position in the bureau will then be more presentable—at least that of a commissioner—and you'll find it easier to act.” “There's no hurry. Let's finish this assignment of yours first.”

Wang understood what he thought Hu meant. The assignment should yield three to five thousand taels, he reckoned. Then he could purchase a more substantial title for his friend. Presumably that was what Hu hoped for, and naturally he should oblige him. “Very well,” he said plainly. “Once I have the money, that will be the first thing I do for you. But what about now? You need some recognised position if you're to act for me.” “There's no need,” Hu replied. “Once Zhang has told people about our friendship, everyone will know. Whatever I undertake on your behalf, they'll naturally trust me.” “Very well. As you wish.” From that moment, Wang came to follow Hu's advice in everything.

That night, over more wine, they spoke at length of their ambitions. From his father's teaching, Wang knew that local magistrates, dismissed by some as ordinary officials immersed in worldly affairs, could in fact accomplish much. Since his studies had not brought success and he had purchased office, he had already abandoned dreams of the scholarly splendour of court. He would seek achievement and advancement along the practical path he had chosen. Hu's ideas were more practical still. One man remained attached to the ambition of official service; the other believed that excellence could be found in every trade and put making a fortune first. Wealth could bring office too. The only limitation was that purchased advancement reached no higher than a third-rank circuit intendancy, without the red hat button of a higher official. Their temperaments and outlooks were so alike that the longer they talked, the better they understood each other. Both felt that the pleasure of friendship surpassed everything else. It was after midnight when Gao finally escorted Hu home, carrying a lantern bearing the bureau's name.

Hu had exceptional energy. Despite the late night, he rose early the next morning. His main task was to visit Zhang at Xinhe. He felt that arriving empty-handed would look poor; some appropriate gesture was needed. Nor should Zhang alone receive a present. Hu had always been generous in his dealings, and now that he had a chance to show it, he intended to please everyone at the bank. Money alone would not do. He carefully considered each former colleague's circumstances, relationships, and tastes, then chose a gift suited to each. This occupied the whole morning. Finally, he hired a porter to carry the load and followed him to Xinhe at Salt Bridge. That won the entire bank over. Everyone felt the same thing: when Hu was unfortunate, he did not trouble his friends; when he prospered, he would always remember them.

Zhang was naturally the most excited. After leaving Hu's house the previous day, he had gone to the proprietor before returning to the bank. He boasted of his ability to recognise exceptional men. He had long known that Hu was destined for greater things, he said, and had always treated him especially well. It was because of their bond that Hu had left without resentment and still remembered Xinhe with affection. The proprietor believed this shameless self-praise, commended Zhang warmly, and gave clear instructions: the bureau must be secured as a client. Profit was secondary. Reputation and credit were involved, and those were a bank's real capital. If Xinhe handled the bureau's remittances, its Shanghai counterparts would regard it with new respect.

Both Zhang and Hu were formidable men. Behind closed doors, neither would reveal his true intentions. Hu began, “When I saw Mr Wang today, we discussed doing business with Xinhe. He said two or three banks were offering loans to the bureau—or rather, advances. Nothing has been settled because the interest rates differ. Naturally, when he heard that I was speaking for you, the situation changed. Mr Zhang, what terms would you offer?” Zhang did not answer directly. “Which banks?” Hu smiled. “How would anyone be willing to tell me that?” “Then tell me this: what is the rate on paper, and what is the private arrangement?” “Let's leave private arrangements aside for now and discuss the stated rate.” “Certainly,” Zhang said, lowering his voice. “But what about you? How much do you want added for yourself?” Hu shook his head vigorously. “Mr Wang has entrusted this to me. How could I take a secret cut behind his back? There's nothing to discuss on that score.”

“Even if you ask for nothing, we must show our appreciation.” Zhang continued, “How much is needed, and for how long? Tell me that first so I can make arrangements.” “At least two hundred thousand taels.” “Two hundred thousand?” Zhang was startled. “You know Xinhe's resources. We'd have to raise money elsewhere.” Obtaining funds from other banks would mean a higher interest rate. Hu understood the implication and smiled. “Then there's no point in discussing it further.” “That's not what I mean!” Zhang hastily corrected himself. “Since you bring us the business, we must do you credit. Even a larger sum could be managed. Leave that to us. Tell me the term.”

“Would you prefer a long one or a short one? We can arrange either accordingly.” If the term could be extended, Hu intended to use a substitution of his own and open a bank with Xinhe's capital. Zhang naturally avoided stating a preference. “We follow the client's wishes. Once you tell us what is required, we can arrange our funds.” Hu could not answer that. The bureau did not yet need cash; it needed Xinhe's guarantee. As for his own sudden idea of using Xinhe's capital to establish a bank, he would have to discuss it thoroughly with Wang before acting. He had no definite plan to state.

Zhang misunderstood his hesitation, assuming that Hu did want a personal margin added to the interest but was embarrassed to say so. He offered a hint. “Xueyan, let us speak privately as friends. Now that you've entered official life, you must keep up appearances, and some expenses will have to be advanced from your own pocket. I'll open an account for you. Draw whatever you need up to two thousand taels and repay it whenever you have the money. No interest.” Hu understood: Xinhe was offering two thousand taels in advance. Accept a man's money, and you must solve his problems. If he took it, he would have to fulfil the bank's requests. But an outright refusal would be awkward. Instead, he deliberately boasted. “There's no need. Xinhe is my old home. Where else would I deposit my money? In a few days, I expect five or six thousand taels. I'll leave it here and begin an account.” “Excellent! Bring it in, and I'll lend it out for you. I guarantee a good return.”

“We can discuss that later. Do you have much business with Shanghai's Big Three at present?” “Fairly well.” That meant not much. Hu was uneasy. After considering it, he decided they could no longer circle around each other. If both continued bluffing and concealing the truth, the matter would end in trouble.

His manner changed. “I understand what you want. A relationship with the bureau would make Xinhe's name more distinguished, and I'll do my best to bring it about. But first the bureau needs your help. If you can provide it, the benefits will be substantial.” Zhang grew still more eager. “Certainly. We shall do everything we can.” “Before I explain, there is one condition.” Hu's expression became stern. “What we discuss today comes from the governor. Not a word may leak out. If it does and trouble follows, neither I nor Mr Wang will be able to save you. Officials aren't reasonable when things go wrong. If the governor sends soldiers to seal Xinhe's doors, don't blame me.” The warning wiped the smile from Zhang's face and left him pale. “Oh! This is no joking matter. Let me close the door.”

With the door shut, they sat together in a secluded corner. Hu outlined the substitution scheme and asked two questions: did Zhang know a grain merchant he could recommend, and would Xinhe guarantee payment? The risk seemed enormous. Zhang could not agree at once. He rose and walked about the room, calculating silently. Seeing this, Hu decided to appeal to his interest. He drew him back into a seat and spoke quietly. “Judge the risk for yourself. Unless something happens on the waterway between Hangzhou and Shanghai—unless the grain boats sink and the rice cannot be supplied in repayment—there should be no loss. As for your return, here's my proposal. The period won't be long. From the day you undertake the guarantee, the bureau will treat the sum as though it had borrowed cash from Xinhe, paying interest by the day until the merchant's account is fully settled. How does that sound?”

Zhang's heart stirred. Without moving any funds, a written undertaking would earn interest as though he had made a cash loan. It was business requiring no immediate capital, with the bureau's reputation to enhance his own. What could be better? Weighing advantage against danger, he began to yield. Business depended on judgement and courage. Hadn't Hu shown unusual judgement in lending those five hundred taels to Wang? He had even sacrificed his livelihood over it. Personally, he had borne a great risk. Yet now the transaction had proved sound. With Hu's example before him, Zhang grew bolder and more inventive. He had decided to accept, but could not commit immediately. There was one thing he must do first: inquire at the provincial treasury about the grain's progress towards Shanghai and the treasurer's attitude to Wang. If both points were satisfactory, the business was settled.

“Xueyan, between friends like us, what cannot be discussed or trusted?” he said. “Consider it eight-tenths agreed. But for a transaction this size, I must consult my employer. I'll give you a definite answer by noon tomorrow. Will that do?” “Of course. But I have something to say too. We're both acting for others and cannot decide everything ourselves. To be frank, there's no need to insist on noon tomorrow. Take until the day after. If I hear nothing by then, I won't come back. That will save us both wasted time.” A final deadline had been set. Zhang admired Hu's decisiveness and willingness to take responsibility, and nodded repeatedly.

Back at the bureau, Hu and Wang exchanged reports. Wang was delighted: his morning had gone very well. He had first seen Lingui, who said the governor had agreed that the treasury should advance the price difference. How it would later be recovered through additional levies on the new grain would be decided separately and was no concern of Wang's. He had then seen Huang, who told him that some extra expense did not matter provided the work was done quickly. The governor showed him two edicts. One stated that the capital's Eight Banner forces numbered 150,000, required strict training, and must receive their overdue pay; the provinces were to expedite grain and silver to meet the state's needs. The other ordered reductions in civil and military officials' integrity allowances to supply military funds. Clearly, the court was struggling to meet its requirements. Huang promised to recommend Wang for promotion if the grain arrived promptly. “Then the matter is almost settled.” Hu knew Zhang would make inquiries. With the treasury's undertaking so definite, Xinhe could feel secure. There was no need to await Zhang's formal reply to know the outcome. “We should discuss going to Shanghai to find the right merchant.”

“My thought is that you should accompany me. The bureau will naturally send two officials, but that will be largely for appearances. The real work will depend on you.” Hu considered. “If you truly want me to handle it, you must let me do it my way.” “Certainly,” Wang answered without hesitation. “I'll follow your lead.”

To make the work easier, Wang finally issued a formal letter engaging Hu as an administrative clerk. He occupied a small room beside the private office and planned matters behind the scenes. First, a hundred thousand taels were drawn from the provincial treasury. Once the guarantee terms with Xinhe had been settled, the sum was deposited there, and thirty thousand taels were remitted to Daheng Bank in Shanghai. Of that amount, ten thousand were for official expenses; twenty thousand were to be forwarded to Huang Zonghan's home district. The latter arrangement was, of course, strictly secret.

Hu then selected two bureau commissioners: Zhou, a follower of Lingui, and Wu, who had connections with the grain intendant. He asked Wang to issue orders assigning them to accompany him to Shanghai. Each also received an additional two hundred taels for travelling expenses. Both had initially regarded Hu with some hostility, but once they learned that this payment was his proposal, they immediately sought his friendship. Hu invited Zhang as well. With the general affairs officer, cook, attendants, and others, the party numbered ten. They hired two fast Wuxi boats and took a large supply of local products intended as gifts. Boarding at Wan'an Bridge, the first great bridge within Hangzhou, they cast off, passed the city barrier, and travelled east along the canal.

It was the third month. Flat fields, rich green mulberry groves, golden rapeseed flowers, and peach and plum blossoms in shades of red and pink spread along both banks like a brocade. Inspired to poetry, Wang leaned against the side of his boat, gazing and murmuring lines. The others had no such literary inclination. Zhou and Wu, together with Hu and Zhang, made exactly four for mahjong. Zhang proposed the game, and Hu readily agreed. Zhang was about to send to the leading boat for the two commissioners when Hu stopped him. “Wait. Let's have the table ready first.” Hu had come prepared. He opened a trunk and took out a new set of ivory mahjong tiles with bamboo backs and an exquisite set of counters. He called the boatman's daughter, A'zhu, to lay the table and divide the counters. Fruit dishes stood on side tables, and fine tea was brewed. Only then did he ask the boatman to pause alongside the other vessel, put out a gangplank, and invite Zhou and Wu over.

The two were delighted by the preparations. “Splendid!” Zhou laughed. “There's real pleasure in travelling with our friend Hu.” “Enough talk,” Wu said impatiently. “Let's sit down. What rules are we playing?” They took their seats and drew for positions. Zhang proposed a game with a hundred taels to a stake, using the yaoban scoring scale: a twenty-point base and a three-hundred-point limit. A self-drawn limit hand would bring thirty taels from each of the other players, ninety in all. “That's too high,” Zhou said. “We're friends playing for amusement. Let's halve it.” “Yes, halve it,” Wu agreed. “I've brought only thirty taels. I can't afford to lose at that rate.”

“No need to worry. With a banker here, what have you to fear?” As he spoke, Hu gave Zhang a meaningful glance. Zhang understood. Taking out a stack of bank drafts, he laid a hundred-tael note before each commissioner. “I'll provide the capital,” he said with a smile. “If you win, I take ten per cent.” “And if we lose?” Wu asked. “If you lose,” Hu said, “repay it when you win.” It was a game in which they could win without really losing. Zhou and Wu were happier still. Good spirits brought good luck, and the bright, charming A'zhu repeatedly supplied hot towels and refreshments. Nothing could have made the game more comfortable.

After four rounds, Zhou, sitting immediately after Hu, had won heavily. Wu had done fairly well too. The losers were Zhang and Hu, both cheerful and gracious, apparently unconcerned. After they changed places, Wu's luck improved. For these next four rounds, it was he who sat after Hu. Zhou followed Wu, whose concentration on making high-scoring hands led him to discard freely. Zhou therefore did reasonably well too and lost little of what he had won. By the end of eight rounds, the boats had moored and darkness was approaching, so they stopped. Counting the counters, Wu had won a stake and a half, Zhou one stake. Zhang had neither won nor lost much, but the ten per cent due from their winnings made him a winner too. Hu alone had lost heavily. Including the table fee, there were four claims upon a single loser's purse.

“Leave it for now,” Zhou said generously. “We can play again tomorrow and settle then.” “Gambling debts are paid in cash,” Hu replied with a Hangzhou saying. “Besides, this is our first game together. Come, let's exchange the counters.” He opened his small bedside chest and took out bank drafts, paying each man and making up the odd amounts in silver. He seemed scarcely to calculate, merely taking a few handfuls and dividing them. Yet when the recipients checked, every payment was exact. Wu was greatly impressed and raised a thumb. “Brother Xueyan, you have the talent of a master of finance!” Wu had some literary learning. The phrase came from the New Book of Tang, in which Emperor Xuanzong praised Yang Guozhong for keeping his gambling accounts so clearly. Hu did not understand the allusion, but he knew how to conceal a gap in his knowledge. Assuming it was a compliment, he merely smiled his thanks.

Last came the table fee, collected hand by hand. With such high stakes, it amounted to sixteen or seventeen taels. Hu placed the counters before him and called, “A'zhu!” She was helping her mother cook at the stern. At his call, she answered brightly, “Coming!” She appeared in the cabin doorway, wiping her hands on a blue cotton apron and smiling with simple good humour. Her long, glossy black plait had swung forward over one shoulder, setting off her oval face with its clear complexion and rosy colour. She was strikingly attractive. Hu's quick eyes noticed Zhou and Wu staring at her, and another calculation formed in his mind. “Come here, A'zhu. Four taels in table fees,” he said. “Give them to your mother.” “Thank you, Mr Hu.” She made a curtsy.

“You're thanking the wrong man. Thank Mr Zhou and Mr Wu. Here!” He picked up a bank draft and beckoned. When she came close to the table, he spoke softly. “The fees came to more than four taels. Mr Zhou and Mr Wu are adding a special reward to make twenty in all. It's money for you to keep yourself.” Her eyes opened wider, and she was too delighted and surprised to know what to do. Zhang laughed. “A'zhu, Mr Zhou and Mr Wu are helping with your dowry. Aren't you going to thank them?” “Mr Zhang is always joking!” She blushed deeply, then lowered her eyes and paid her respects to Zhou and Wu. “Now we must add something ourselves,” Wu said to Zhou. Each gave her another ten taels. Never in her life had A'zhu possessed so much money. She thanked them again and again, then rose and hurried towards the stern, her long plait swinging. A moment later they heard breathless, laughing speech. She was evidently telling her mother of her good fortune.

They all found it entertaining and exchanged smiles. Just then, a gangplank was laid outside, followed by the boatman's call: “Mind your step, Your Honour Wang!” Wang had crossed over from his boat. Everyone rose to greet him as he entered cheerfully with a sheet of writing paper in his hand. Smiling, he asked Zhou and Wu, “How did the battle go?” They were embarrassed to have their superior refer to the gambling. Zhou blushed. “Fortune favoured us, thanks to Your Excellency.” “Good, good.” Wang pointed to Zhang. “Then our banker must have lost. A few taels mean little to a man of his resources.” “A pleasure to contribute, a pleasure.”

After some joking, A'zhu laid the table for dinner. The cooking aboard the fast Wuxi boats was famous, and special efforts had been made that day, so the food was finer still. Everyone had also brought provisions for the journey. There was not enough room on the main table, and two side tables had to be added. Hu had instructed the general affairs officer to bring plenty of aged Zhuyeqing wine. A jar was opened and warmed to precisely the right temperature. Its rich aroma filled the air as it was poured. Even Zhou, ordinarily a poor drinker, could not resist a cup. With such a meal and the lively, attractive A'zhu serving them, it should have been an occasion on which, as the ancient wit Chunyu Kun said, a man could drink an astonishing amount. Unfortunately, Wang's presence made everyone restrained. He conversed only with Wu. That afternoon, gazing from his boat, he had composed four seven-character quatrains entitled “Spring Views” and was very proud of them. Now he enthusiastically discussed which word lacked resonance and which should carry a departing tone. The others understood little and cared less, yet courtesy required them to look fascinated. They drank in gloomy silence. Fine food and mellow wine lost their flavour; even the girl's charms were wasted. Their ears suffered, and their eyes were deprived as well.

Hu saw that things were going wrong. His careful arrangements were being spoiled by Wang's pedantry. Fortunately, they had agreed that Hu should direct this journey. Seizing a pause, he gave Wang a glance, asking him to return early to his own boat and leave the others free to enjoy themselves. Warm with wine and delighted by his conversation, Wang at first missed the hint. Then he examined the others' expressions, thought a little, understood, and felt apologetic. “I've had enough wine,” he said tactfully. “You carry on.” He asked A'zhu for a small half-bowl of rice, ate it, and left the table. Hu knew that he had not really finished drinking, so he specially asked the boatman to prepare four dishes and warm a jin of wine to send to the leading boat.
