INSTALLMENT 005
A Swift Rise to Prominence, Volume I — Chapter Three, concluding part

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

“There!” Zhou said, straightening his back. “Now we can enjoy a proper drink.” The table was tidied, the cups rinsed, and more wine poured. There were still five at dinner: Wang had been replaced by Zhao, the general affairs officer, a capable man whom Hu had already won over in these few days. “How about a drinking game?” Wu suggested. “I only know finger-guessing,” Zhang said. “I can play that too,” Zhou put in. “It's the drinking I can't manage.” “Never mind. I'll find someone to drink for you.” Hu called out, “A'zhu, drink Mr Zhou's forfeits for him.” “Oh!” A'zhu immediately pushed out her lower lip and swayed her shoulders in a playful pout, like a little girl. “All right, all right!” Hu coaxed her as though soothing a child. “You needn't, you needn't!” She smiled prettily, then felt a little embarrassed. “How about this? For every cup Mr Zhou drinks, I'll drink one for him.” “And if he drinks ten?” Zhao asked. She considered, then declared firmly, “I'll drink ten too.”

Everyone applauded. Zhou laughed and waved his hands. “No, no! You're deliberately trying to get me drunk.” He made to leave the table, but Zhao and A'zhu caught him, one on either side. Wu declared sternly, “I'm master of the drinking game. Its rules are stricter than military orders. Mr Zhou has disobeyed me: one penalty cup to begin with!” “Let me plead for him,” Hu said. “No! Unless A'zhu does the pleading.” “Oh, Mr Wu, you really are joking!” she said with a laugh. “What has this to do with me?” “Aren't you drinking for him? Since you're so fond of Mr Zhou, why shouldn't you plead for him too?” What sort of reasoning was that? He had talked her into a muddle. She knew his argument was wrong, but could not refute it. Still, she would not admit to being particularly fond of Zhou.

“I serve all you gentlemen alike. If I'm fond of one, I'm fond of all...” She could not very well finish the thought, but the mischievous Zhang deliberately exposed it. “And if you dislike one, you dislike us all—is that it?” “Oh, no, no! You mustn't say that.” A'zhu looked flustered, her blush making her more charming still. “All the gentlemen are good, except one.” “Which one?” “You, Mr Zhang!” She laughed at her own retort, then turned with a swing of her waist and went to the stern for hot wine. When she returned, Wu began challenging each man in turn. They were all in high spirits, and with A'zhu moving among them like a butterfly among flowers, Zhou and Wu were beside themselves with pleasure. They drank until thoroughly drunk. So the days passed in cards, wine, and flirtation with A'zhu. The boats moved very slowly, but their passengers thought them too fast.

They did not reach Jiaxing until the fourth day. Wu hinted to Hu that several days aboard had become unbearably confining: he would like a walk ashore. That was an excuse. He really wanted another day's delay. Hu understood perfectly, spoke to Wang, and arranged to stay a day in Jiaxing. A visit to Jiaxing necessarily meant an excursion to South Lake. Wang joined them for tea upstairs in the Tower of Mist and Rain. The day happened to be overcast; spring haze hung over the water, and the willows blurred in the dim light. Wang felt another poem coming on. Zhang, however, could not sit still. “Shall we hire a boat and row about?” he suggested. “What for?” Wu objected. “We've been sitting in boats all the way and have had enough of it. If we're going boating, we might as well use our own.” Their own boat had A'zhu. South Lake had plenty of boatwomen too, but none necessarily surpassed her; and even if one did, so brief an encounter offered little prospect of anything further.

“I have an idea!” Zhang blurted out. Then, noticing Wang's attention, he stopped, drew Hu aside, and spoke privately. He wanted to seek out the amorous diversions concealed behind monastic doors. Hu too knew of certain practices in Jiaxing that disgraced the Buddhist establishment, but Wang's official position made such a visit unsuitable. They agreed that Zhang should take Zhou and Wu to form these “blissful connections,” while Hu accompanied Wang on a stroll. They therefore separated. With a little manoeuvring, Hu took Wang away first. Two small sedan chairs carried them to the busy streets, where they got out to browse and wandered into a bookshop. Wang wanted a collection of poems. Hu idly leafed through a newly arrived Peking Gazette and, noticing Huang Zonghan's name in an imperial edict, read more closely. The entry included Huang's memorial in reply to the inquiry into Chunshou's suicide: “The treasurer, distressed by the shortage of treasury funds and the difficulties of grain transport, suffered a recurrence of liver illness and consequently took his own life. There were no other circumstances.” The emperor's response was, “Noted.” Hu knew that this particular trouble was now behind Huang. Was He Guiqing responsible for that? Wang bought his poems and Hu the gazette. With nowhere else to go, and Zhou and Wu conveniently absent, they returned to the boats for a private talk.

After reading the edict, Wang felt both pleased and afraid. He was pleased that Huang had emerged unencumbered and stood high in imperial favour: Zhejiang's affairs should now be much easier to manage. Yet he had long heard of the governor's harshness and cunning. Huang might discard a bridge once he had crossed it and cease to heed He Guiqing. That would cost Wang an important source of support. “Mr Xuexuan,” Hu said, using a form of address he had recently adopted as suitable for both public and private occasions, “I think you're worrying too much. Governor Huang wants things done and wants credit for doing them. If we follow his wishes and do better than he himself expects, what can he possibly complain of? As the saying goes, the master shows you through the gate, but the practice is your own. Education Commissioner He has done enough by bringing you in. If you don't develop your own abilities, even the strongest patron is no use. Look!”

He pointed out another edict in the gazette: “Concerning the memorial jointly submitted by the Grand Secretaries, the Grand Council, and the Board of Punishments, as instructed, proposing a sentence for Xu Guangjin: Xu Guangjin, formerly acting Governor-General of Huguang and since dismissed, was specially appointed by Us as imperial commissioner to take charge of military affairs, yet was already dilatory in his progress along the route. When the rebels descended from Hunan and Hanyang and Wuchang fell in succession, he still clung to Yuezhou without devising a single plan. He gravely mishandled the deployment of forces and lost his opportunity. Let Xu Guangjin be sentenced, in accordance with the recommendation of Yucheng and his colleagues and the established law, to beheading after detention, the sentence to be carried out after autumn.” “This Commander Xu was an imperial commissioner personally appointed by the emperor. You could hardly have a stronger patron! But when a man fails, even that support is unreliable. He still faces execution.” Hu sounded deeply moved. “Everything else is uncertain. You can truly depend only on yourself. Even good connections depend on what you are. What friends can a good-for-nothing expect?” Wang nodded repeatedly. “Xueyan, this is no flattery: it's a pity you haven't had more schooling. Otherwise you'd certainly do better than He Genyun or Governor Huang.” “I don't see it that way. A merchant may seem at a disadvantage before an official, but business has pleasures of its own. An official faces all sorts of restrictions. A prosperous merchant can really enjoy himself!”

“Oh?” Wang was interested. “Then let us hear your ambitions!” This was one classical phrase Hu understood. “My ambition is rather different from other men's. I like plenty of money—the more the better!” He brought both hands round as though gathering a heap towards him. “But once I have it, I don't mean to paper the walls with bank notes and be satisfied just looking at them. I mean to spend it! The finest pleasure in the world is to find someone at the end of his tether, some fine fellow whom the lack of a single coin is almost driving to his death, and happen to have money on me.” He flung out a hand as though scattering silver and, almost acting out the scene, exclaimed, “Take it! Is that enough?” Wang burst out laughing. “It's a pleasure even to hear you say it!” “There's something else. A merchant who makes a fortune can enjoy it freely. He can build a great garden and put seventeen or eighteen concubines in it, and nobody has any right to complain. But an official who grows rich—I'm sorry, he can't please himself so freely! Leave everything else aside: having people point behind your back and call you a corrupt official would be unpleasant enough.” “Ah!” Wang was tempted. “The way you put it, I feel like giving up office and going into business myself.”

“That's not quite what I mean. Office has its pleasures too. At the very least, it brings honour to your ancestors and makes your parents happy. If I make a great fortune one day, my mother will naturally live in tremendous comfort. Yet there'll always be something missing. In her eyes, ten or a dozen maidservants wouldn't be worth as much as an imperial patent granting her a title.” “That isn't impossible either,” Wang assured him. “You simply couldn't obtain a patent making her a lady of the first rank.” Purchased advancement could reach a third-rank circuit intendancy, which naturally carried eligibility for such a patent. Hu did not yet dare entertain that ambition. “Obtaining a patent wouldn't be too difficult, of course. But actually living up to an official title, with all the standing that ought to go with it—that isn't easy.” He smiled and added, “I'm not disparaging officials, but some expectant gentlemen go years without a single assignment, until poverty has made them eat through their resources and pawn everything they own. That sort of office is hardly worth having.”

Wang felt the force of that remark from experience and valued his present opportunity all the more. “Xueyan,” he asked, “what about Zhou and Wu?” What about them in what respect? Hu could not answer so vague a question, but understood its intention. “Rest easy, Mr Xuexuan. Those two are completely in my hands. They'll do exactly as I wish. There's no need to worry about them. What concerns me now is whether we can find a large grain merchant willing to supply the rice in advance.” “Yes.” Wang too grew anxious. “I'm also afraid that, even if we find one, he won't trust us.” “That...” Hu shook his head. “That doesn't matter. Provided he has the resources, I can make him listen.” His confidence, together with what Wang had seen of his skill in winning people over, reassured Wang at once.

They talked until evening, when Zhang returned with Zhou and Wu, all well satisfied with their outing. A closer look revealed secretive smiles on their faces. Hu could not ask questions in Wang's presence, but knew they must have experienced something quite new to them. “Oh, I've just remembered something,” Zhang suddenly said with an air of complete seriousness. “I met a friend today, and it came up in conversation that a large grain firm in Songjiang has close ties to the grain-transport association. They have a hundred thousand shi or more of rice to sell. It might be worth making inquiries.” Before Hu could speak, Wang exclaimed excitedly, “Now we're on the right track!” “Why, Mr Xuexuan,” Hu asked in surprise, “we've only just heard of it. We know nothing of the circumstances. How can you tell it's the right track?” “So there are things even you don't understand!” Wang smiled triumphantly and explained his reasoning.

He had made a serious study of grain transport. Songjiang produced rice, lay at the boundary between Jiangsu and Zhejiang, and enjoyed excellent water communications. Its transport association was therefore large and ought also to have been rich. But its very size and wealth made it meat on the chopping board. The prefecture of Songjiang ranked with Chengdu in Sichuan and Changsha in Hunan as one of the three famously lucrative prefectural appointments, each for its own particular reasons. One important reason in Songjiang was the prefect's control of waterway labour and checkpoints. When the association needed passage through sluices or men to haul its boats, it had to accommodate him. After years of exploitation, the Songjiang association had accumulated an enormous deficit in its common funds and become an impoverished organisation. Wang judged that the grain firm was actually its own business. The origin of the rice now offered for sale was highly questionable: it might well have been siphoned off from the grain-tax shipments. The quality would be poor, but the price should certainly be low, greatly reducing the amount needed to cover the difference. “Excellent!” Hu had not studied these matters in depth and accepted Wang's account.

Zhang therefore went ashore again to find his friend and arrange a meeting with the merchant in Songjiang. It was to take place aboard their boats. Wang, attentive to such details, feared that going ashore to negotiate with a grain dealer would arouse suspicion. On returning, Zhang reported that everything was arranged. They would reach Songjiang on the third day and moor beneath Xiuye Bridge inside the city. His friend would bring someone from the grain firm to find them there. He also confirmed Wang's judgement. The firm, Tongyu, was indeed the financial backing of the Songjiang association. Besides trading grain, it operated a remittance bank. But the south was the domain of private banks, and remittance houses with northern connections found it hard to compete. Zhang had heard of Tongyu, but had never dealt with it and knew nothing of its credit. “Enjoy yourselves for one more day tomorrow,” Wang said, half indulgently and half in warning. “Once we reach Songjiang, there'll be serious work to do!” In fact, serious work began that very night. They dined aboard Wang's boat and discussed grain transport. Wang's knowledge came from books, so he knew the regulations, government orders, and precedents. Zhou, by contrast, was an experienced hand. As a convoy commissioner, he had made seven or eight journeys along the canal and knew a good deal about the abuses of the system. His scattered details were less orderly than Wang's account, but their basis in personal experience made them particularly vivid. When Hu brought the two accounts together in his mind, something new emerged. After a night's discussion, he understood both the regulations and their practical operation.

“There is something I should like to ask,” Hu said, for he still had questions. “What does ‘cash payment by the taxpayer, procurement by the authorities’ mean?” “The arrangement works as follows...” Wang explained that grain-tax collection took five forms. One, called “regular delivery,” required the grain to be taken directly to the thirteen granaries in the capital. Another, “redirected delivery,” sent it to the two granaries in Tongzhou. The two sets were known for short as the capital granaries and the Tongzhou granaries. A third category, “white grain,” consisted of glutinous rice, also delivered to the capital, for sacrificial offerings and as part of the grain salaries of princes and officials. This levy fell upon the five prefectures and one department of Suzhou, Songjiang, Changzhou, and Taicang in Jiangsu, and Jiaxing and Huzhou in Zhejiang. All three required payment in kind. Where special circumstances led to rice being replaced by other grains, or other grains by silver, the change required a specific imperial order and was called “altered collection.” The final form was “commuted collection”: a quota originally assessed in produce was collected in silver instead. This had four variants, of which “cash payment by the taxpayer, procurement by the authorities” was one. In other words, the taxpayer paid the silver equivalent of his grain assessment, and the government bought the rice for regular or redirected delivery on his behalf.

“I understand. May I ask another question? Under what circumstances is that arrangement permitted?” Zhou was the man to answer this practical detail. “There is no fixed rule. In general, it is for the convenience of both officials and taxpayers. Suppose an imperial order presses for grain to meet government needs. Treasury money can first be used to buy and ship the rice; silver is then collected to reimburse the advance. Some small households have no rice to deliver and willingly pay its market value instead. Naturally, the authorities are glad to procure it for them. There is another case: harvests vary from place to place. In districts with a good harvest, people naturally deliver rice. Beyond the basic quota, the officially prescribed allowances for losses in transport and aboard ship are collected in rice too, as are the additional, unauthorised levies imposed on top of those allowances. When this rice is taken to districts with poor harvests, its price is comparatively low. The people there can buy it to discharge their grain tax. Only an entry in the accounts is required. That too counts as cash payment by the taxpayer and procurement by the authorities.”

“So that is how it works! Then we needn't go about this in secret,” Hu said. “The military situation is urgent, and grain shipments by sea are being pressed forward. Why shouldn't we use the regular funds to buy and ship rice? As for Tongyu, if this is the association's legitimate allowance for transport losses, and official procurement against cash payments is permitted, selling it breaks no law. Even if the rice is stolen, we can treat it as their proper allowance.” “Exactly!” the ever-ready Zhang said. “The sacks don't have ‘Stolen’ written on them!” Wang, Zhou, and Wu exchanged glances and nodded slightly. They were plainly puzzled: why had so simple an argument never occurred to them? “That's true,” Wang said. “Of course it would be best to proceed that way. But the Sea Transport Bureau is responsible only for shipping. This arrangement belongs to the collection stage. Without an official communication from the provincial treasury and the grain intendant's office, how can I take over their duties?”

Now the effect of Hu's careful attentions to Zhou and Wu throughout the journey became clear. Both hurried to speak, then deferred to each other. Plainly they had the same thing to say; one could answer for both. Zhou was older and a personal follower of Treasurer Lingui, so he replied. “That needn't trouble you. Whatever supplementary document is required from the treasurer's office, I'll arrange it.” “And I'll do the same at the grain intendant's office.” “Then I am most grateful,” Wang said, clasping his hands. “I must trouble you both.” “It is our duty,” they answered together.

“Wait a moment!” Zhang suddenly interrupted. “This attractive calculation may not work out.” His reasoning was not without merit. Tongyu wanted to sell rice, while they wanted someone to advance it on loan. With different aims, the two sides might never reach an agreement. “Not necessarily,” Hu replied. “A business cannot go on selling without buying. Trade calls for negotiation. Everything depends on how you negotiate.” They all nodded, including Zhang. “Only if you do the talking,” he said with a smile. “Nobody else could manage it.” The remark was partly a joke, but true enough. Zhou privately suggested to Wang that the officials should appear briefly to establish the authenticity of the matter, then leave the actual negotiation to Hu and Zhang. Businessmen would find it easier to deal with one another. This was exactly what Wang wanted, and he readily agreed. Hu accepted the responsibility without demur and revised their plans for Songjiang. They had originally intended a single meeting aboard the boats with Tongyu's representatives. Now a conspicuous display of standing would make the negotiations easier.

With wind and current in their favour, they passed Jiashan and reached Fengjing, entering Huating County in Songjiang Prefecture. The next day they entered the city and moored beneath Xiuye Bridge, famous for the four-gilled perch with its “great mouth and fine scales.” Wang sent the general affairs officer ashore to hire a sedan chair. Then, both dressed in fresh clothes, he and Gao set out to pay calls, accompanied by porters carrying generous gifts of local produce. He first visited the prefect of Songjiang, presenting the formal card appropriate to an official call, then called on the magistrates of Huating and Lou counties. Huating, as the county containing the prefectural seat, was expected to act as host. Its magistrate promptly returned the visit, invited Wang to dinner, and sent men to attend to his needs. The prefect then sent over a full seafood banquet. Hu took charge, tipped the bearers generously, and told them to carry it back to the restaurant, where further instructions would follow. “Mr Xuexuan,” he said, “you, Zhou, and Wu should go to the Huating magistrate's dinner tonight. I have a use for the prefect's banquet.” “Certainly. Do as you think best.”

They had scarcely finished speaking when Zhang's friend arrived with the manager of Tongyu. On seeing Wang, both naturally paid their respects. Following Hu's advice, Wang deliberately assumed an imposing official manner. Zhang's friend was surnamed Liu; Tongyu's manager was Gu. Wang asked their names, exchanged a few civilities, then rose. “I have an engagement. You must excuse me.” Turning to Zhang, he added, “You gentlemen discuss matters. Treat everything exactly as though I were here. Whatever you agree shall stand.” Once he had left, Zhou and Wu explained that they were accompanying him to the Huating magistrate's dinner and departed too. The four remaining hosts and guests then settled down to a serious conversation.

They did not yet tackle the main issue, but sounded out the circumstances around it. Gu freely acknowledged that Tongyu was the collective property of the Songjiang grain-transport association. Hu then asked about the association itself. Though an outsider, he understood its customs, and his questions all touched the important points. He soon learned that its most senior member was a boat-service soldier named Wei, nearly eighty, blind in one eye, and enjoying retirement at home. The man now managing its affairs was Wei's final admitted disciple, You Laowu.

“Proper respect comes first,” Hu said to Zhang. “I should like our friends Liu and Gu to take us to pay our respects to Old Master Wei.” Both guests hastened to decline the honour implied by his courteous address. “You are too kind. We can simply convey your message to him.” “That wouldn't do,” Hu said. “Please don't regard me as an official. To tell you the truth, though I'm an outsider, I often pass myself off as a member. As the saying goes, a man may claim membership, but a member may not deny it. Still, in the presence of the real thing today, I mustn't pretend. A man away from home ought never to put on airs. I ask you both to lead the way. And there's something else. After paying my respects, I should like to invite the old gentleman out for a drink. We have a banquet—I don't know how good it is, but the Songjiang prefect sent it to my employer. I should like to offer it to him as a small mark of respect.” Both guests were impressed. This Hu knew how to conduct himself in the wider world and was well worth befriending. They ought to introduce him. They readily agreed, and the party went ashore and walked to Wei's house.

Old Wei had withdrawn from social life and no longer received visitors. Gu therefore dared not announce them without preparation. He explained to the household that a friend from Zhejiang had come and asked whether the old man wished to see him. Hu had anticipated this. He had brought a formal visiting card describing himself as a junior and handed it over to be taken in with the message. They had not sat long in the reception room before a member of the household invited them into the inner quarters. Liu and Gu immediately brightened. “Zhang,” Liu said, “our old gentleman rarely receives anyone inside. To tell the truth, we scarcely ever get to go in ourselves. Today we owe the honour to you two distinguished guests!” Hearing that, Hu knew he had made the right move.

Inside, they found a thin, wizened little man. Yet when he opened his single eye to look at them, it flashed so fiercely that they could hardly meet his gaze. There was indeed something exceptional about him. Hu greeted him with the ceremony due from a junior to a senior. Wei had difficulty moving and, rather as though trading on his age, repeatedly protested that he did not deserve the honour without stirring from his seat. Once they were settled, he examined Hu closely. “You must have something to tell me, my friend. Among men of the world, plain speaking is valued. Say it directly.” “My employer sent me. He says the elders of the grain-transport association must be treated with respect. He could not conveniently come himself in official dress, so he specially asked me to invite you. We are borrowing flowers to make an offering: the prefect sent him a banquet, and he wishes to devote it entirely to you.” “Oh?” Wei asked attentively. “He wants me to dine?” “Yes. He is attending the Huating magistrate's dinner now. When he returns, he would also like you to visit his boat.” “Thank you. Unfortunately, I don't move about easily.” “Then let us do this,” Hu said. “I'll have the banquet brought here.” “That would be too much. His Honour Wang's kind intention is enough. Now tell me what you need. If there is anything I can do, I shall certainly help.”

“Naturally, if we run into difficulties here, whom else could we ask? But, truly, my employer sent me to seek your advice in all sincerity. There is nothing beyond your powers. We, however, must know what it is proper to ask. That makes me a little reluctant to speak.” Hu was deliberately retreating in order to advance. At his first mention of sea transport, Wei's single eye opened wide and fixed him with a piercing stare. That too was expected. Hu had already reasoned that moving the grain shipments to sea would seriously threaten the canal associations' existence. With countless livelihoods at stake, they were bound to fight the change desperately, openly and in secret. Wei's hostility confirmed his judgement. Since the judgement was sound, the matter would be easier to handle. He calmly finished explaining his purpose. Wei's manner changed again. His gaze softened a little, but the welcome had gone from his face. “My friend,” he said slowly, “do you realise that our association is about to lose its livelihood?” “I do.” “Then you must understand my difficulty.” Wei nodded. “I don't know much about Tongyu's affairs now, but business is business. If you have money to buy rice and Tongyu refuses to sell, nobody anywhere could defend that. I would certainly speak for what is fair. But if you want an advance of goods, a merchant puts up his capital to earn a return and must make his calculations. I can't give you an answer to that now.”

It was a refusal, and one Hu had anticipated. “Sir,” he answered firmly, “would you hear a little more from me?” “My dear friend, what are you saying? You've done me the kindness of visiting. I ought at least to keep you three days, talk things over properly, and make a friend of you. If you have advice, I could ask for nothing better. Why ask whether I'm willing to listen? Do you find me arrogant?” “Then I spoke badly.” Hu smiled. “My employer's business is actually of great consequence to your association. Let us speak plainly. If the sea shipment misses its deadline, the officials will naturally be punished. But the consequences for the association will be worse.” He went on to explain the balance of interests. Any delay would not have occurred at sea, but on the canal journey to the port. An inquiry into responsibility might leave Zhejiang's association liable for ruinous compensation. The associations' secret code was known as Tongcao—“all grain-transport men united.” They formed one body, sharing prosperity and adversity. How could the Songjiang association stand by and watch? This appeal to their obligation of brotherhood struck Wei at a vulnerable point. His resistance immediately weakened.

“Besides, sea transport is only an experiment for now. Nobody knows whether it will eventually replace the canal altogether, whether the old arrangements will be kept, or whether both routes will be used. Frankly, the association ought not to help the people who oppose canal transport and advocate shipment by sea.” “What do you mean?” Wei asked with close attention. “You should know, sir, that plenty of people want to speak for the association. My employer is one. But they must have grounds on which to help. If the association lets itself down, those who want sea transport will argue all the louder: ‘Look! Even this short stretch is full of difficulties. The canal men are simply no use any more!’ Now consider the opposite. Suppose the canal shipment arrives on time, without the slightest delay. Once it has put to sea, a gale might overturn a couple of sand junks. Then the association's supporters would have something powerful to say!” Wei heard him out without answering. He merely told the attendants beside him, “Go and fetch Laowu.”

The affair had plainly taken a promising turn. Hu had an excellent sense of how far to go on such occasions. No further argument was needed; he had only to keep Wei happy. Their serious business concluded, they turned belatedly to social conversation. Wei said he had lost count of his visits to Hangzhou. That was hardly surprising: Hangzhou marked the beginning of the canal, and Gongchen Bridge outside the city had special associations with the boatmen. Wei recalled many old Hangzhou stories about which Hu knew so little that he could offer no answer. Instead, he eagerly asked for more, and they talked congenially. They were deep in conversation when You Laowu arrived. He was about forty, small and composed. Anyone experienced in the world would have recognised at a glance a formidable man. Wei himself introduced Hu and Zhang. Since they were friends of his master, You was exceedingly polite and addressed Hu as “Mr Hu.” “This friend of ours comes from the home of the patriarchs,” Wei said. The three founders of the Qing Brotherhood, the secret society within the grain-transport association—Weng, Qian, and Pan—were all said to have attained enlightenment at Gongchen Bridge in Hangzhou. That was what he meant.

“Then we're practically one family,” You said. “You mustn't stand on ceremony, Mr Hu.” Before Hu could reply, Wei continued, “Our friend Hu is a man who knows the world. We must make a friend of him. Laowu, call him Uncle. He is like the Little Master Outside the Gate.” You immediately changed his form of address, warmly calling him “Uncle.” Hu was genuinely overwhelmed by the honour. He knew the story of the Little Master Outside the Gate. One of the three founders—he could not remember which—was said to have had a young personal attendant of exceptional loyalty and trustworthiness. Even when the founders discussed secret matters, they did not exclude him. Yet although he was treated as one of them, he had never joined the brotherhood and technically remained outside its threshold. They therefore honoured him as the Little Master Outside the Gate. Whenever an incense-hall ceremony was held, he too received a share of the incense offerings. Wei's comparison meant that he was admitting Hu as a trusted intimate. Above all, to have him addressed as Uncle put him on Wei's own generational level. In future, at least in the Songjiang district, the association would treat him as an honoured guest. That was no small achievement for someone just entering this world. Naturally, he protested vigorously that he did not deserve it. But Wei's word was law among his people. Whatever Hu said, they obeyed the old man's instruction and called him Uncle, including Zhang's friend Liu and Tongyu's manager Gu.

“Laowu! His Honour Wang of the Zhejiang Sea Transport Bureau has also sent us a banquet. It was a gift from our own prefect, and he has specially passed it on to me. Such an unusual honour must be acknowledged.” Wei continued, “If a man gives me a foot of respect, I give him ten feet in return. Go aboard his boat first and kowtow to thank him for me.” “There's no need! I can convey your thanks,” Hu protested, delighted inwardly. But he must explain matters to Wang beforehand. When You came, Wang must not put on an indiscriminate display of official superiority. He therefore added, “Besides, my employer has gone to dine with your county magistrate.” “Then I'll go first thing tomorrow.” Hu asked You to send men to the restaurant and bring the seafood banquet to Wei's house. Wei had adopted a vegetarian diet and devoted himself to Buddhist prayers, so he declined to join them and was represented by You. Both You and Hu now found themselves half host and half guest. In the end, Zhang took the seat of honour.

After the opening courtesies and three rounds of wine, they turned to business. Hu repeated what he had told Wei. You responded warmly, “Everything can be discussed, everything!” Yet he gave no definite answer. As the man responsible for running the association, he faced many difficulties. Filling the deficit in its accounts was the lesser problem. The shift to sea transport had placed the Jiangsu associations in desperate straits. Without grain to carry, their income had fallen sharply, and the brethren's livelihoods had to be maintained. They also needed to campaign for the abolition of sea transport and the restoration of the canal route. Every approach, every favour sought, every person cultivated cost substantial sums. They were relying entirely on selling these hundred-odd thousand shi of rice. If they advanced it to Zhejiang's bureau, they might earn something on the price difference, but would eventually receive rice again. That was wholly at odds with their aim of selling the stock to obtain cash. Watching You's expression, Hu noticed that, although he kept up his part in the conversation, his mind was elsewhere. He was calculating. This was no longer a question of credit, of fearing that the bureau would leave its obligations unmet. Some other difficulty lay behind it. In business, one must consider the other man's position. Hu spoke sincerely. “Brother You, since we're one family, there is nothing we cannot discuss. If you have a difficulty, why not say so plainly, and we can consider it together? Your problems are ours. We mustn't think only of ourselves.” No wonder the old master valued him, You thought. The man really knew how to speak properly. He answered gratefully, “Uncle, you are most considerate. But the old master has given his instructions, so you needn't worry. This is our first meeting, and Mr Zhang is here too. Let us drink freely for now. Tomorrow we shall do as instructed.”

That was precisely what Wei had meant by returning ten feet of respect for one. Hu reflected that You had given so handsome an answer because his own words had shown the proper understanding. If he regarded the business as settled, it would be—for this one occasion. You would certainly keep his word. But now that Hu knew of the difficulty and had spoken of it, to accept this gracious answer, pretend ignorance, and leave the matter there would make him a shabby sort of fellow. Their friendship would end here. There would be no second occasion. “That won't do. Otherwise I shall feel uneasy. Brother You,” Hu said earnestly, “let me say again that you must undertake this only if it is manageable on your side. If it would strain you, we'd rather find another way. A man making his way in the world mustn't harm his friends.” “Since you put it like that, Uncle, I would hardly be treating you as family if I failed to speak frankly.” You paused, then continued, “There is a difficulty, though it isn't beyond discussion. I hope you won't laugh at us, but our Songjiang association only looks prosperous. We've lived on borrowed money ever since the Qianlong reign. Otherwise we wouldn't be so eager to sell this stock. It's nearly the end of the third month. Before long, the lean fifth and sixth months will be upon us, when the old harvest is gone and the new one isn't ready. Rice prices are bound to rise. Wouldn't it be better to hold the stock and wait?”

“Ah, now I understand!” Hu looked at Zhang. “This is where we need your help.” Even You understood the proposal: a bank would lend the association money, to be repaid when the rice was sold. He had considered that himself. Unfortunately, bankers followed advantage wherever it led. As soon as grain transport shifted to the sea, they all began courting the sand-junk association. When it came to lending to the canal men, their tone suggested fear of the risk. Unwilling to risk humiliation by asking, You had decided that relying on himself was better than seeking help and was prepared to sacrifice the stock for ready cash. Zhang, meanwhile, was now acting entirely as Hu's assistant, taking his lead from every hint. He answered without hesitation, “We should be delighted to help. Just tell us what is needed!” At that, You and Gu exchanged a glance. They looked surprised and a little doubtful. Hu understood: Zhang had agreed so readily and casually that he sounded insincere.

Hu therefore prompted him, speaking seriously in the Hangzhou dialect. “Mr Zhang, a man's word is as good as silver. Don't treat serious business as a game!” Zhang took the hint and protested emphatically. “How could a businessman dare do that? If Brother You needs a loan, a very large sum would be beyond my resources. But anything around a hundred thousand taels—leave that to me. What do you say, Brother You?” “That would be about right,” You answered, half seriously and half in jest. “Ours is an impoverished association. Mind you don't end up with a bad debt.” “Nonsense!” Zhang said. “I have every confidence. First, there's the Songjiang association's credit and good name. Second, there's the standing of the Zhejiang Sea Transport Bureau. Third, the rice is there as well. Surely those three guarantees are enough?” You was reassured. The banker had made his own calculations and was not merely speaking to please them. Deeply gratified, he said, “Excellent! That meets everyone's needs. In truth, Uncle, you're the one helping us. Otherwise, selling the stock for cash wouldn't have been easy at such short notice.” He repeatedly clasped his hands towards Hu in thanks. Hu too was delighted. The affair had gone remarkably smoothly. Hosts and guests drank to their hearts' content and parted in the highest spirits. They agreed to meet the next morning and immediately travel together to Shanghai. There they would arrange how Tongyu was to deliver the rice, and how Zhang was to assemble the silver and lend it to the Songjiang association.

You personally escorted them back to Xiuye Bridge, where they immediately noticed something unusual. The landing was ordinarily neither bustling nor deserted, with a dozen or more boats of various sizes crowded together. Now only their two boats remained, their bows facing the stone steps, giving them the best position and the easiest access. “Why!” Zhang exclaimed. “What's happened? All the other boats have gone. Does a water ghost haunt this place?” “No, no!” You answered at once. “It's perfectly safe. I was afraid that so many boats together would be noisy and keep you awake, so I arranged for them to move.” Only now did they see the extent of You's authority on the local canal, as well as the sincerity of his hospitality. Hu and Zhang thanked him repeatedly. “It's late, and His Honour Wang must have retired. I won't disturb him. I'll come to pay my respects early tomorrow.” You took warm leave of them and waited until they had boarded before departing.

A'zhu was still awake. As she wrung out towels and poured tea, she happily told them that the Songjiang association had sent all sorts of provisions: a shi of excellent white rice, four chickens, ten jin of pork, firewood, charcoal, oil, candles—even rough paper. They had also sent someone to invite her father and the general affairs officer ashore for a bath and dinner. The two had only just returned, thoroughly drunk, and fallen straight asleep. “I've passed through this landing a dozen times and never seen anything like it. Mr Hu,” she said innocently, “you must belong to the brotherhood, mustn't you?” “Indeed he does!” Zhang laughed. “A'zhu, you've had a real stroke of luck on this trip. How will you thank Mr Hu?” “I ought to, certainly.” She smiled. “I'll make him a pair of shoes.” “Who wants those?” “Then I'll cook him a couple of dishes.” “Worse still.” Zhang was deliberately teasing her. This would not do, nor would that. At last she said helplessly, “Then I can only kowtow to thank him.”

“Exactly!” Zhang laughed. “But not just to Mr Hu. You'll have to kowtow to his mother and his wife as well.” “Why?” “Silly girl!” Hu could not help laughing. “Can't you see that Mr Zhang is making fun of you?” She still did not understand, so Zhang explained. “Why, can't you work that out? Once you've entered his household as his concubine, won't you have to kowtow to his mother?” That embarrassed her. Rolling her eyes, she scolded, “The fatter you get, the worse you are!” Then she turned and left. Zhang roared with laughter. “What an entertaining journey this is!”

“Enough of that,” Hu said. “Are you sure you can provide the loan you've promised? Among these men, a handsome undertaking matters. Once a word is spoken, the affair is settled. Don't make a stinking mess of it!” “Nonsense!” Zhang answered. “I have fifty thousand taels in Shanghai. I'll borrow another fifty thousand from the Big Three and can pay out the cash at once. But we share the responsibility!” “Naturally. The Sea Transport Bureau will guarantee it.”

With that settled, they each went to bed. Early the next morning, Hu was still asleep when someone shook him. Opening his eyes, he saw A'zhu beside the bed. “His Honour Wang sent Second Master Gao to ask you over.” “Oh!” Hu sat up, took his watch from beneath the pillow, and saw that it was only seven. She had already draped a lined silk jacket over his shoulders. As she leaned close, a faint fragrance reached him. Desire stirred, and he caught her hand and pulled her towards him. “Don't!” she protested softly, pointing towards the cabin wall. Her objection was not, in truth, unwillingness, but concern that there were ears in the next cabin. Hu did not want to press her too far. He lifted her hand, smelled it, and laughed quietly. “How sweet!” She snatched it back, lowered her head, and smiled. Then she tossed his clothes onto the bed and went away. At the cabin door, however, she turned, drew a slender finger twice across her cheek in a gesture of mock shame, made a face at him, and swayed out.

Hu remembered that Li the Infallible, the fortune-teller on City God Hill, had told him the previous month that romantic luck lay ahead. There seemed to be something in it. Then he reproached himself. At the point when one's fortunes were changing, this sort of distraction was especially to be avoided. What did “romantic luck” amount to? With money, a man could enjoy it every day! The thought drove the lively, charming A'zhu straight out of his mind. He dressed, hurried through his washing, and went to the leading boat to see Wang. Wang had waited to breakfast with him. As they ate, Hu gave a detailed account of the previous day's events, and Wang listened spellbound. When he finished, Wang shook his head as though unable to believe it. “What an extraordinary succession of encounters!” “Things have gone smoothly, but we mustn't become careless,” Hu said. “You must have heard something yesterday. How does the general situation look?” “I did! I heard quite a lot.”

The news all concerned Hong and Yang. Hong Xiuquan had founded a state and proclaimed himself king. He called it the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom, renamed Jiangning the Heavenly Capital, and took the title Heavenly King. He had appointed officials, established court ceremonies, and issued ten prohibitions, also called Heavenly Commandments, reportedly modelled on the Christian Ten Commandments. Naturally, the new state's troops were called the Taiping Army. One force, led by Lin Fengxiang, Chancellor of the Heavenly Office, and Li Kaifang, Chancellor of the Earthly Office, had taken Zhenjiang, crossed at Guazhou, and captured Yangzhou. It was preparing to move north against the region around Beijing. “Good heavens!” Hu exclaimed in alarm. “The Taiping Army is formidable!” “It certainly is, but the government forces have gained a foothold too,” Wang replied. “Imperial Commissioner Xiang has pursued them to Jiangning and encamped at Xiaolingwei, east of the city, preparing a siege. Another commissioner, Qishan, formerly Governor-General of Zhili, has brought cavalry and infantry from Zhili, Shaanxi, and Heilongjiang down through Henan to meet them head-on. I think the situation may gradually improve. It all depends on how the two great tasks of training troops and raising military funds are handled.”

“Then grain prices should look promising?” “Of course. In any reign or dynasty, once war begins, grain prices are bound to rise. Grain merchants who store their supplies well and avoid losses invariably make fortunes.” “Then that settles it,” Hu said with satisfaction. “Our present arrangement really does help the Songjiang association.” Wang nodded and gazed into space, apparently thinking. The expression on his face was so peculiar that Hu could not interpret it.

“Mr Xuexuan,” he could not help asking, “what excellent idea have you thought of?” “Yes, I do have an idea. Tell me whether it would work.” Wang lowered his voice. “Why let someone else make the profit when we could make it ourselves? Could we discuss a loan with Zhang, buy Tongyu's rice outright to fulfil the delivery, then hold Zhejiang's shipment ourselves and sell when prices improve?” “It's a good idea in itself. But we can't do it. First, we have nowhere to store the rice...” “That doesn't matter!” Wang broke in. “We can go into partnership with Tongyu and use their storage.” “That would be worse. Mr Xuexuan,” Hu said gravely, “among these men, a spoken undertaking must stand. We can't go back on what we've promised the Songjiang association. Otherwise they'll despise us, and we won't get anywhere in future.” Wang trusted Hu completely. At once he abandoned his “excellent idea,” saying repeatedly, “Yes, yes. I'll follow your advice.”

“One thing more. When You Laowu comes over, please make a special point of showing him respect.” “I understand.” Before long, You boarded to pay his respects, kneeling and kowtowing. Wang treated him with great courtesy and devoted considerable attention to him. Then they cast off, left the city, and travelled east along the Wusong River. The next morning they reached Shanghai.
