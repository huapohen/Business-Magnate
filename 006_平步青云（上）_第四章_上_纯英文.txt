CHAPTER FOUR

Shanghai's county town had been fortified in the thirty-second year of the Ming Jiajing reign, originally as a defence against Japanese raiders. The walls extended nine li around the town and stood two zhang and four chi high. There were six gates, large and small. The principal eastern, southern, western, and northern gates were named Chaozong, Kuahai, Yifeng, and Yanhai. Two others, Baodai and Chaoyang, were commonly called the Little East Gate and Little South Gate. Their boats moored outside the Little East Gate.

As the boats arrived, someone on the landing waved. You Laowu, standing in the bow, answered with a gesture. Before the gangplank was properly in place, the man came aboard in a few quick steps, as agile as though walking on level ground. One glance showed that he was accustomed to life on the water. “A'xiang,” You asked, “is everything ready?” “Everything. I've had rooms kept at the Gaosheng Inn by the North Gate, and notified Sanduo Hall. The chairs are waiting on the landing.” “Good. Go back and attend to things. Make sure nothing is overlooked.” Once A'xiang had left, You returned to the cabin. Hu and Zhang were discussing where to stay and in what order to conduct their business. Neither knew Shanghai particularly well, and after considerable discussion they had settled nothing. You's arrival made further deliberation unnecessary. He told Hu that he had sent a man ahead and prepared everything. They need not trouble themselves. Shanghai County belonged to Songjiang Prefecture, he added, so he was their host. All their needs in Shanghai would be supplied by him as his “official duty.”

“How could we accept that?” Hu protested. “And those words ‘official duty’—Brother You, you're positively insulting us!” You only smiled, then asked, “Uncle, do you know Shanghai well?” “No.” “Then let's go ashore at once. There are plenty of places to enjoy ourselves. We mustn't waste time.” They all went ashore, Wang included. Several blue-cloth sedan chairs were already waiting at the landing. Only ten years had passed since the opening of the five treaty ports, but Shanghai had become luxurious. Violations of the restrictions governing dress and display counted for little. Apart from Wang, every man in the party rode in a large blue-cloth chair for the first time in his life.

The chairs entered the city and turned north. When they stopped, the travellers found themselves among inns of various sizes. The larger establishments proclaimed in gilded lettering that they offered “temporary residences for officials”; the smaller promised “comfortable lodgings for merchants.” The Gaosheng Inn naturally belonged to the former category. You had reserved a courtyard with five spacious, clean rooms. Their luggage had arrived too. Once they were settled, he drew Hu aside and quietly asked, “Is His Honour Wang a very proper gentleman?” That was difficult to answer. Hu replied, “Brother You, you must have a reason for asking. Tell me that first.” “Here's the arrangement. I'll give everyone a welcoming meal, then we'll visit the City God Temple. The bankers' guildhall is in the garden behind it, so Mr Zhang can conveniently call on his fellow bankers. Tonight I'd like to entertain you all with courtesans. If His Honour Wang doesn't care to come, we'll make other arrangements.” So that was it! Wang seemed a man with a taste for romance, Hu thought, but visiting courtesans compromised official propriety. He would have to ask him personally. “There's still plenty of time,” You continued. “Perhaps we should eat first and discuss it over tea at the temple later.” “Yes, exactly. Let's do that.”

You's welcoming meal was at the leading restaurant for local Shanghai cooking, inside the Little East Gate, before the City God Temple, in Huacaobang's Guiyuan Lane—properly called Guanyi Lane. Wang had already said that he wanted only a modest meal and would decline a formal banquet. You therefore ordered local dishes such as zaobotou, tufei, and juancai. Their flavours were strong and rich. But this was a moment when hungry men were easily satisfied, and nobody found them too heavy.

Afterwards, they walked to the temple, which was only a few steps away. The county temple was the City God Temple. Worship of this deity began under the Northern Qi, developing from the earth gods of the Qin and Han. At first the cult existed only south of the Yangtze. Somewhere in that cultured southeastern region, some clever person had devised the excellent notion of assigning the City God a definite identity: he was the local official of the underworld. A metropolitan City God corresponded to a governor, a county City God to a county magistrate. Like an earthly yamen, his establishment had its runners and administrative offices. In the unseen world, he could arrest people and hear cases. Thus ordinary people who suffered injustice had a final court of appeal. The county magistrate acknowledged this local colleague of equal standing; moreover, the magistrate of the dead appeared to exercise authority over the magistrate of the living, so the latter could not help feeling some apprehension. The Complete Book of Happiness and Benevolence, a manual on local administration, expressly instructed a magistrate arriving in his jurisdiction to spend a night of ritual fasting at the City God Temple one or three days before taking office. This served, first, as a courtesy visit, an advance request for forbearance; and second, as an opportunity to seek advice in dreams about such matters as the local bullies and corrupt gentry who preyed on the people, or the hidden circumstances of unresolved injustices.

The City God was not appointed by the court but chosen by the people, much as the worthy and capable were selected for office in the living world. The guiding principle was that intelligence and uprightness made a spirit worthy of divine honour. Without uprightness, he would not wish to redress the people's wrongs; without intelligence, he would be unable to do so. Shanghai's City God was such a popular choice, one of the three most famous in the southeast. Suzhou had Huang Xie, Lord Chunshen; Hangzhou had Wen Tianxiang. Shanghai had originally belonged to Lord Chunshen's fief, but the people of Suzhou had already claimed him, so Shanghai had to choose another. Their choice was Qin Yubo, a native of Daming Prefecture. Late in the Yuan dynasty he had risen to a departmental directorship in the Fujian provincial administration. When the realm fell into disorder and rival leaders rose, he abandoned office and sought refuge in Shanghai. After Zhu Yuanzhang, founder of the Ming, won the empire, he summoned Qin to court, appointed him an academician serving as an imperial reader, then sent him out as prefect of Longzhou. On retiring, Qin returned not to Daming but to Shanghai, his adopted home. After his death, he repeatedly manifested his powers to protect the people, who therefore chose him as their City God.

Like the Great Xiangguo Monastery in Kaifeng, Shanghai's City God Temple was a lively market full of food and diversions. Inside the first entrance gate, miscellaneous shops lined both sides. A theatre stage stood in the centre of the second gate, with the passage running beneath it. Stalls on either side sold snacks such as sweet osmanthus rice porridge and rice balls in fermented rice wine. Beyond the stage lay an enormous open court. Engravers occupied the western gallery; in the eastern stood a teahouse used as a meeting place by the county yamen's clerks and runners. People arranging lawsuits or seeking favours made contact there. Farther north stood the temple's main hall, flanked by four stone bailiffs. Legend said they had drifted in from the sea, probably former subordinates of Qin Yubo in Fujian who had crossed the East Sea to rejoin their old master. Inside the hall, a great abacus hung over the doorway facing the god, with eight large characters beside it: “Man has a thousand calculations; Heaven has one.” This was the parting admonition offered to worshippers as they left. Opposite the abacus, above a statue about a zhang high, hung a plaque bearing the City God's formal title, “Divine Lord of Jinshan.” Beyond lay the rear hall, where both the god and his wife were worshipped. Her sleeping chamber was on the western side, a secluded inner room that ordinary mortals were permitted to view on her birthday.

The temple's chief attraction was Yuyuan, the finest garden within Shanghai's walls. It had originally belonged to Pan Yunduan, who had served as provincial treasurer of Sichuan during the Ming Jiajing reign. Naturally, the upheavals at the end of the Ming had left it neglected. By the middle of the Qianlong reign, prosperity was at its height and the country extraordinarily wealthy. To provide the City God, protector of this coastal district, with somewhere to relax after his official duties, local people raised money to buy the ruined garden from Pan's descendants and restore it. The work took more than twenty years and enormous sums of silver. Because it lay northwest of the temple, it was called the West Garden. An East Garden already stood on the other side, commonly known as the City God Temple's Rear Garden. The bankers maintained and repaired the East Garden each year. It opened only for the birthdays of the City God and his wife, and for the elegant orchid gathering in early summer. Yuyuan, however, remained open all year, with several teahouses and a very large storytelling hall. You entertained the party with tea in Qingfen Hall, popularly called the Osmanthus Hall. A bird-fighting contest was taking place that day. Several of the participants were his brethren, who greeted him warmly and urged him to join them. Like cricket fighting, the sport allowed heavy betting. You invited Zhou, Wu, and Zhang to take part, leaving Hu free to speak privately with Wang.

“Mr Xuexuan,” Hu asked casually, “would you care for a little diversion tonight?” Assuming he meant cards, Wang shook his head. “You go ahead as usual. I'm no hand at gambling.” “We'd be admiring flowers, not bamboo.” Wang understood. Bamboo meant bamboo-backed playing tiles; flowers, needless to say, meant lingering among the alluring blossoms of the pleasure quarters like drifting catkins. He immediately smiled. “Flowers and bamboo—what a delightful turn of phrase!” Close though they were, this was the first time they had discussed visiting courtesans together. Wang was older and could never quite put aside the thought that he was an official. Embarrassed inwardly, he could manage only this evasive answer. Hu understood human nature thoroughly. He knew Wang was tempted, but might not wish to dine with courtesans in Zhou and Wu's company. Even if he agreed, he might find little pleasure in it. Hu therefore formed another plan. For the moment he said nothing and turned to official business. They agreed to rest that day. On the following day Wang would pay his calls, while Hu and Zhang joined You to arrange the loan.

“One important matter remains,” Wang said. “We must promptly arrange the twenty thousand taels Governor Huang wants remitted to Fujian.” “I know. Secrecy matters more than speed in that affair. I'll see to it. You needn't worry.” Hu rose as he spoke. You was a man who heard and saw everything around him. Seeing Hu get up, he excused himself from the game. They met, spoke quietly, and made their arrangements. That evening the visitors from Hangzhou separated into three unconnected parties. One went to Sanduo Hall. Another consisted of Gao alone, accompanied around town by a junior member of the association whom You had assigned to him. When everyone else had gone, only Wang remained aboard, dressed in ordinary clothes, with a pair of dark crystal spectacles beside him, reading as he waited. Just after nightfall, Hu slipped away from Sanduo Hall. One of You's men was already waiting. They took a sedan chair to the landing outside the Little East Gate and collected Wang. Their guide told the bearers, “Meijia Lane.”

Meijia Lane was fairly secluded, but its winding approach led to a place of unexpected charm. When the chairs arrived, the guide softly tapped the brass ring on a small stone-framed gate, which was promptly opened. A woman of about forty received them, speaking in a particularly pleasing Suzhou accent. Only when they reached the lighted reception room did Wang, looking through his dark glasses, see that she retained considerable grace despite her faded youth. The room was arranged with neatness and taste, quite free of vulgarity, and he felt at ease. “Third Aunt,” the guide told their hostess, “these are Mr Wang and Mr Hu, both distinguished guests. Take special care of them.” She answered with repeated assent. Her manner showed not merely compliance but a measure of fear. After the guide had left, she began the usual sequence of polite questions. By the end of this conventional exchange, she had established her guests' identities—or rather, the false identities they supplied: Mr Wang and Mr Hu were country gentlemen from Hangzhou. Fruit was set out and tea served. Then Third Aunt called inside, “Young lady, come and meet Mr Wang and Mr Hu!”

The lined gauze curtain, blue-green as lake water, lifted, and a beauty appeared. Wang's eyes immediately brightened. He removed his dark spectacles and studied the young woman as she approached with the grace of a willow in the breeze. Her lined silk jacket was the pale blue of a sky cleared by rain. Though its high, ingot-shaped collar rose stiffly, the waist was fitted closely enough to reveal her slender figure. Instead of a skirt, she wore dark lined trousers edged with broad, coloured lace imported from the West. She wore little powder or rouge, but her hair was dressed to a glossy black sheen. A gold ear-pick ornament set with jade adorned her coiffure; she wore no other jewellery. In such a household, this counted as exceptionally simple dress. As she came nearer, Wang could see her elongated oval face, poised between the shapes conventionally compared to an egg and a melon seed. Her eyes were particularly beautiful, changing from dark to bright as she glanced about, like lustrous satin from the West. Long lashes lent those glances a startling charm. She was in the full bloom of young womanhood, like an autumn moon nearing fullness or spring flowers at their height, and seemed an opportunity not to be missed. Smiling, she greeted the guests with easy assurance as Third Aunt directed, then said, “Please come and sit in my room, gentlemen.”

Within, they found a different scene again. Nothing suggested a house of courtesans. It might have been the chamber of a cultivated daughter of a good family. Besides the rosewood furniture, there was a bookcase. Paintings and calligraphy hung on the walls, including a landscape by Dai Xi and clerical-script calligraphy by Deng Shiru, both celebrated recent masters. Small ornaments filled a display stand. Incense burned in a bronze vessel of an unusually novel shape, its thin smoke rising with a fragrance somewhere between orchid and musk that stirred the senses.

“Your tea, Mr Wang.” She placed a covered cup before him, took several pine nuts from the fruit dish, rubbed off their skins between her hands, and held them directly to his lips. Wang almost wanted to take her fingers into his mouth as well, but did not. Instead, he caught her hand. “Young lady, what is your name?” “My personal name is Wanxiang.” “Which characters?” “Wan, as in the nine plots planted with orchids; xiang, as in the fragrance worthy of a king.” “What elegant speech!” Wang asked, “Wanxiang, who taught you to read?” “Read? If I'd had an education, would I have ended up in a place like this?” She smiled with a trace of sadness. Wang did not recognise the practised manner of the women in these private establishments. At once he was filled with pity for a beauty cast adrift. Holding her hand, he seemed burdened with more feeling than he could put into words.

Seeing that matters were well under way, Hu stood. “Mr Xuexuan, I must take my leave.” “Wait, wait!” Wang beckoned. “Stay a little longer.” “There's no need.” Intent on leaving them to themselves, Hu started towards the door. “I'll come back later.” “Wanxiang, I think Mr Hu is angry with you.” At that, Hu stopped. She came over and took his arm. “Did you hear Mr Wang, Mr Hu? Please sit down and keep our gentleman company. It's still early to leave.” “‘Our gentleman’ already! How affectionate!” Hu teased. “If you insist on keeping me now, you won't be able to get rid of me later. I'll stay here as an extra lodger with a bed to myself.” “A bed to yourself or a bed with company—I don't know what you mean!” As she spoke, she glanced at Wang, then immediately looked away.

Wang had never before experienced the charm of such a glance. He felt almost transported. “An evening like this must have wine!” He looked inquiringly at Hu, asking without words whether courtesan dinners were customary here. Before Hu could answer, Wanxiang hastened to say, “It's already being prepared. Would you like a little something to eat first?” Without waiting for a reply, she lifted the curtain and went out, presumably to hurry the kitchen. “I never imagined there could be such a refined place!” Wang said with great satisfaction, watching her go.

“Mr Xuexuan,” Hu said with a smile, “I don't think you'll be able to go home tonight, even if you want to.” “Why?” “Didn't you notice Wanxiang's manner? She's already hinted that you can stay.” “Which words?” “‘It's still early to leave.’ Doesn't that suggest you needn't leave at all?” On reflection, it did. Now Wang hesitated. “Here's what I suggest. I'd better go early after all,” Hu planned for him. “When I left Sanduo Hall, I only said I was accompanying you to visit a relative you hadn't seen in years. I'll tell them he insisted on your staying and that you won't return until tomorrow.” Delighted, Wang nodded repeatedly. “Exactly. I do have a cousin in Shanghai, surnamed Liang.”

They had barely finished when Third Aunt entered with a maid carrying trays. As she laid the table, she asked what wine the guests preferred and modestly apologised for her simple household kitchen and its lack of worthy dishes. Her hospitality was smoothly managed from every angle. Four exquisite plates of cold meats appeared, followed by warmed wine. But one essential person was missing. “Where's Wanxiang?” Hu asked. “Coming!” she answered from outside, then entered with a small pot of sweet soup made from red dates, lily bulbs, and lotus seeds. She said she had cooked it herself. Whether that was true or not, it tasted especially sweet to Wang. They ate the refreshment, then began drinking. Wanxiang kept filling their cups and serving them food. Otherwise she turned slightly, let Wang hold her hand, and quietly listened to Hu. He felt there was really no reason to remain. At a suitable moment he said he must return to Sanduo Hall, promised to collect Wang personally the next morning, and left.

Once outside, a thought struck him. Instead of returning to Sanduo Hall, he went back to the boat. At his call from the landing, the boatman looked out of the rear cabin in surprise. “Why, Mr Hu! Alone?” “I took His Honour Wang to see a cousin. They haven't met for years and have enough to talk about all night. I doubt he'll be back today.” Hu stepped aboard. “The others are drinking at Sanduo Hall. I'm not feeling well, so I've come back for an early night.” “Have you eaten? In case any of you gentlemen wanted supper, I had my wife prepare some rice porridge.” “Excellent. I'll have a bowl, with a few light dishes.” The boatman agreed and went aft. Hu entered the cabin alone. His pillowcase and bedding had been changed, the floor and furniture wiped spotless. The glass shade of the foreign lamp on the table shone clean, and several books for light reading were neatly stacked. Once seated, he noticed a faint fragrance. Looking round, he found a string of pearl orchids beside his pillow. He picked it up and examined it. There seemed to be oil on the fine copper wire holding the flowers. After a moment he understood: it must be the osmanthus oil from A'zhu's hair. How had flowers she wore in her hair come to lie beside his pillow? An intriguing puzzle. As he considered it, the curtain hook sounded and she appeared.

“I haven't made tea in a covered cup,” she said without any form of address or preamble. “You drink so much that I've used a teapot instead.” Hu did not answer at first. He looked at her closely, noticing her sleepy eyes and a red patch on her right cheek. “Have you just got up?” “Mm.” As she poured, she gave a drowsy smile. “I don't know why. I've been terribly tired all day.” “There's a name for that: spring drowsiness. Have you been having spring dreams too?” “Dreams are dreams,” she protested. “What do you mean by spring dreams? You and Fat Zhang always have something pointed behind your words. Still...” She stopped. “Still what?” “You're better than those Mr Zhous and Mr Wus, at least. Always pawing at people. It's disgusting.”

“Thank you for the compliment. Are these flowers yours?” “Oh!” Her eyes widened. “How did you get them?” “I found them beside my pillow. I can't understand it. Did you leave them especially for me?” “Who'd give them to you?” she protested as though wrongly accused. “I was tidying the cabin this afternoon and got tired, so I took a nap on your bed. They must have fallen off then.”

“Lucky I came back and found them. Otherwise there could have been trouble!” “Why?” she asked defiantly. “It isn't anything serious.” “You really don't care!” Hu laughed. “Think about it. Flowers from your hair turn up beside my pillow. What would other people think?” “I don't know. Nothing good, I'm sure.” “It would sound good to me.” “What would?” “Come here and I'll tell you.” When she approached, he said quietly with a laugh, “They'd think we'd shared a bed.” “You dreadful man!” She blushed scarlet and struck him.

Whether she struck too hard or he pulled her towards him, she lost her balance and fell against him. “Will you hit people again?” Hu asked, holding her by the waist. “Let go, let go!” she said softly, twisting twice, though without much force. He did not release her, but helped her sit beside him on the bed. “There's nobody here today. I won't let you get away this time.” “You wouldn't dare!” She stared at him. “Don't my father and mother count as people?” “They won't come meddling in your affairs.” Before he had finished, her mother called, “A'zhu, ask Mr Hu whether he wants some wine warmed.” She sprang up. Hu failed to catch her, and she was already at the cabin door. After answering her mother, she turned back. “Do you want wine?” “Come here. I'll tell you.” “I won't! I'm not deaf. I can hear you from there.”

“I had a headache and didn't want any. Now it's gone, so naturally I'll have a cup.” “Hmph!” A'zhu curled her lip. “You were pretending to be ill all along! Looking so sneaky—who knows what you're up to?” She lifted the curtain and went out. Soon she returned with wine and dishes and laid out his cup and chopsticks. Hu asked her to eat with him, but she refused. She did not leave, however. Leaning against the cabin doorway, she bit her lip and played with the end of her long plait. Hu drank and watched her, smiling now and then as he contentedly raised his cup. At last she could not help asking, “What are you smiling at?” “I can't tell you yet.” “When can you?” “One of these days. You'll find out for yourself.” “Hmph!” she scoffed. “Who knows what scheme you're hatching? If you have something to say, say it plainly!”

After considering her words, Hu understood what she meant. He beckoned. “It isn't something that can be explained in two or three sentences, and you don't look as though you're ready for a serious conversation. There's nobody else here. We don't often get a chance to talk. Sit down and listen.” “Then I will!” As though encouraging herself, she added, “What is there to be afraid of?” Once she sat down, he asked, “How old are you?” “Why ask that?” “Why, we're talking! A conversation can range over anything. If you won't tell me, I'll tell you: I'm thirty-one.” She laughed. “I didn't ask your age.” “There's no harm in saying it. I suppose you're twenty-six.” “What?” She was surprised and displeased. “Nonsense! What makes you think I'm twenty-six? Adding ten years for no reason! Do I really look that old?” “Then you're sixteen?” Hu nodded. “That sounds more like it.” She realised she had fallen into his trap. “You officials are dreadful! Full of tricks. One has to be on guard every moment.” She shook her head emphatically, as though deeply dismayed. “It's impossible. One careless moment and you're deceived.”

“It's not that I'm dreadful. You won't give a straight answer!” He picked up a piece of dried fish in tea-seed oil and held it towards her mouth. “I don't want it!” She turned away, whether embarrassed or deliberately refusing his kindness was unclear. “Taste it! They've given me fish that's gone off!” He angrily dropped it onto the plate. She was fooled again. Taking his chopsticks, she turned her head and put a piece in her mouth. At once his expression changed to a mischievous, slightly triumphant smile. She was annoyed, yet found the exchange curiously pleasing, and deliberately pouted. Hu laughed. “A'zhu, you'd better be sensible and listen to me. Otherwise, with the least little trick, I can have you running round and round like a horse on the rim of a vat!” It was a Wuxi saying. His awkward imitation of the accent made her laugh aloud. When she stopped, she said, “Don't make a spectacle of yourself!” Then she repeated the proverb with clear, accurate pronunciation: unmistakably native Wuxi speech.

“A'zhu, why do you usually speak with a Huzhou accent?” “I am originally from Wuxi!” “How did you become one of us Zhejiang people, then?” “‘An old sheep froze to death in the sixth month’—it's a long story.” She shook her head, apparently reluctant to tell it. But her background was exactly what Hu wanted to learn, and he would not let the subject drop. After a little gentle persuasion, she finally spoke. Her voice was very low, lest her parents hear, for it was their story she was telling.

“My mother came from a respectable family...” It ought to have been a long story, but A'zhu made it brief. Her mother was the daughter of a scholarly household living beside a river. The family owned a boat, used for visiting friends and relatives, tending ancestral graves, and collecting rents. The boatman was surnamed Zhang. He was young, so they called him Young Zhang. The daughter admired his honest character. They became lovers, and she conceived A'zhu. Discovery would have meant disaster. After discussing it privately, they decided to flee together. An honest man, Zhang would not let the young lady take so much as a blade of grass from her family, lest he be accused of abducting her and absconding with their property. Yet they did take one thing: the boat. Across Lake Tai lay Wuxing. They braved the dangerous crossing only because they feared her family would pursue them down the canal. Later inquiries showed that they had chosen correctly. She had never returned to Wuxi. For fifteen years, their life on the water had run only between Wuxing and Hangzhou, and Hangzhou and Shanghai. Because she was describing a secret love affair, and her own parents' at that, A'zhu blushed repeatedly and was acutely embarrassed. When she finally finished, she let out a long breath, straightened her back, and raised her face, visibly relieved. “No wonder,” Hu said with a serious expression. “Your mother came from a good family, and your father is a good man. That explains how they came to have such a likeable daughter.” It was no extraordinary compliment, but A'zhu heard “likeable” with particular clarity. The blush that had faded returned at once.

“Are you your parents' only child?” “I had a younger brother. He died when he was five.” “Then they'll depend on you in their old age?” A'zhu did not answer, and her face clouded. The subject always distressed her. Her parents had discussed several possible marriages for her. One idea was to take in a son-in-law from the same trade, who would acquire the boat along with his bride. A'zhu had been indulged from childhood. Her mother's background had also given her a temperament and upbringing unlike those of other boatmen's daughters. Moreover, her father's fast Wuxi boat was finely equipped, offered attentive service, and was especially famous for its cooking. Its regular customers were officials and wealthy families. A'zhu's expectations had been raised early. She did not wish to marry a barefoot boatman. Whenever her parents mentioned the prospect, her face became rigid, as though a knife could not cut into it.

One evening the previous year, she had accidentally overheard them discussing her. “A'zhu is fifteen,” her father said. “Her birthday falls early, so she's practically sixteen. Time passes quickly. We can't delay.” Her mother was silent for a long while, then sighed. “Ah, the higher matches are beyond us, and the lower ones won't do.” “It can't all be left to her! What she'd like is a young gentleman, so she could become a young mistress straight away. Isn't that an impossible fancy?” At this, A'zhu could not help crying. First, she was angry that her father had misrepresented her: she had never thought any such thing. Second, she resented his low opinion of her. Becoming a young mistress was hardly so extraordinary. It wasn't as though she wished to become empress. Why call it an impossible fancy? “If she wants comfort, she'd have to become someone's concubine.” “How could we allow that?” her mother said. “Even if A'zhu agreed, I wouldn't.” “Nor would I,” her father promptly answered. “It seems we'd better find an honest fellow. Life may be a little hard, but at least they'd be one husband and one wife.” “A'zhu can't endure hardship.” “It's not that she can't endure it. You're afraid to let her.” “That isn't what I mean. There must be some hope, some prospect of getting on. I've helped you work a boat all my life. If you now expect A'zhu to do the same, ask yourself whether that's fair to either of us.” These were harsh words. Her father fell silent, apparently feeling too guilty to reply.

“I was thinking it would be best to find some poor scholar,” her mother continued more gently, “a decent man willing to work hard and improve himself, and marry A'zhu to him...” “Enough, enough!” her father interrupted impatiently. “I'll finish it for you. The poor scholar studies by lamplight late into the night and rises with the cock before dawn. In the end he comes first in the imperial examination, and A'zhu becomes a lady of the first rank. You've listened to so many stories that you've lost yourself in them!” “Such things aren't impossible! He needn't come first. I'd be quite content for her to be the wife of a licentiate.” “You'd be content; he wouldn't. Once a man succeeds, he'll begin to look down on A'zhu. Haven't you heard of Chen Shimei disowning his first wife, or Zhao Wuniang living on husks? By then, crying for her would be too late!” After this scolding, her mother only sighed. Before long both parents were snoring, but A'zhu lay awake all night. To this day, any mention of her future left a knot in her heart.

For all his extraordinary shrewdness, Hu could not guess what she was thinking. Seeing her stare in silence, he prompted her again. “Why don't you say something?” Filled with resentment and nowhere to direct it, she happened to vent it on him. “There's nothing to say!” she snapped. Hu was startled. He did not know why she was so angry, but was not offended; he found it rather amusing. She, however, knew immediately that she had been wrong. If her parents discovered that she had spoken to a guest in that manner, a scolding would follow. Unwilling to admit her fault aloud, she picked up the wine pot and filled his cup, offering an apology through the gesture. Hu understood now. His question must have touched a sensitive spot. He ought to comfort her. He took her hand, and she let him, recognising that this was a gesture of reassurance rather than flirtation.

“A'zhu,” he said quietly, “I know you're unhappy about something. Life is like that: eight or nine things in ten go against our wishes. Some troubles can't even be told to one's parents. That's truly suffering without being able to speak.” Before he had finished, tears were streaming down her face. Every word had reached her heart. She could not speak of her distress, yet here was someone who understood it without being told. The mixture of pain, sweetness, and relief was unlike anything she had known. In that moment, she felt that her heart had found somewhere to rest. Hu was surprised and a little uneasy. What hidden pain could have moved her so deeply? For a moment he sat dumbfounded, unable to speak. She did not notice his bewilderment. She took a handkerchief from a fastening beneath her arm and wiped her eyes. The sight of her weeping, like pear blossoms in rain, moved him still more. “A'zhu,” he said, “if something is troubling you, why not tell me? Speaking of it may make you feel better.” But how could she explain? After a long pause, she said only, “I was born unlucky.”

Born unlucky? Hu considered this. Though she came from a humble household, her parents cherished her. She was far better off than the children of concubines in grand families, who faced slights everywhere. What, then, was her misfortune? Unless... “I know,” he said as the thought occurred. “Your parents must have promised you in marriage when you were little, and you dislike the family.” “No, no!” She denied it hurriedly, then seized the opportunity to reveal a little. “You're only half right.” “Oh! They're discussing a marriage now?” She said nothing and lowered her head slightly: an evident admission. “What sort of family is it? Why don't you like them?” “Oh!” she said impatiently. “Let's not talk about it.” “Very well. We'll talk about something else.” His decisive tone cut through her troubles like a sharp knife through tangled thread, setting them aside. Her attention turned entirely to him.

“Don't keep asking about me,” she said. “Tell me about yourself.” “Where should I begin?” Hu smiled. “I don't know what you'd like to hear. You wouldn't understand official business...” “Who wants to discuss official business?” Then she meant private matters. He wondered what she had in mind. He would find that out before deciding what to do. “Let's do this,” he said. “You ask, and I'll answer. I promise to tell the truth.” A'zhu wanted to know who was in his household and whether he was married. But that hardly needed asking: of course he was. Was his wife good-natured? That too was pointless. Whether she was or not, her own parents would never agree, even if she herself wished to go with him. Then she remembered Zhang's teasing: once she entered the Hu household, she would naturally have to kowtow to Hu's mother and his wife. Clearly, then, he was married. But did he have children?

At that thought, a new idea occurred to her. If he had no children, there might be a way. Especially with an elderly mother alive and eager for a grandson, a wife who failed to produce one might find her mother-in-law insisting that her son take another woman. “Of the three unfilial acts, leaving no heir is the worst”: that was a publicly respectable reason. However unwilling the wife, she would have to swallow her resentment. If A'zhu entered the household and did not wish to live with the first wife, a separate establishment could be set up, with each woman treated as the principal wife in her own home. There was a recognised arrangement for that. It would increase Hu's expenses, of course, but she could not concern herself with that. In a moment she had resolved upon it. If he agreed, she would have a separate household and rent a home where her parents could live with her. If not, that would be the end of it.

Her expression cleared. Steadying herself and overcoming her embarrassment, she asked as though making casual conversation, “Mr Hu, how many little children do you have?” “Two.” Her heart sank. “Both sons?” she asked. “Sons? Girls!” “Oh!” She smiled. “Two young ladies!” “A'zhu,” Hu asked casually as he drank, “why do you want to know?” “We're only talking! Didn't you say a conversation could range over anything?” She went on, “And your mother—how old is she?” “Nearly sixty.” She wanted to ask whether his mother longed for a grandson, but that would have been too revealing. She hesitated and said nothing.

Watching her expression, Hu remembered the fortune-teller Li on City God Hill, who had predicted romantic luck the previous month. Apparently his reputation for infallibility was deserved. Yet Hu was wary. A'zhu seemed deeply attached. Unless he had serious intentions, he could not have a brief affair and then cast her aside. A devoted woman and a faithless man, entangled to the end, would only bring harm to both. He must not act rashly. This understanding made him still more composed. The calmer he became, the less able she was to contain herself. After much thought, she asked, “How old are the two young ladies?” “One is six and the other five.” “Has Mrs Hu had no happy news since?” “No.” Hu shook his head, then added, “None at all.”

“Flowers first, fruit afterwards. Your mother will have a grandson in the end.” It was a tentative probe, and Hu understood. Now he would have to reveal his position. A simple answer—“Yes, everyone says that, and I believe it too”—would close the subject. But he did not wish to say it. What should he say instead? As he hesitated, voices came from the bank. They sounded familiar. Presumably the party from Sanduo Hall was returning. Both listened. Sure enough, they heard the general affairs officer call, “Boatman! Put out the gangplank.” “Fat Zhang and the others are back!” A'zhu hurriedly rose and left.

Zhang came aboard first. Finding Hu contentedly drinking alone, he was astonished. “Why didn't you come back to Sanduo Hall? I thought you were with His Honour Wang all along.” Zhou and Wu followed, thoroughly drunk and slurring their words. Hu produced the story he had prepared to conceal Wang's whereabouts, then answered Zhang. “I was going back to Sanduo Hall. But then I remembered how much we have to do tomorrow. While you gentlemen enjoyed yourselves freely, I thought I might do some careful planning on my own. That way our serious business wouldn't suffer.” “A true friend!” Zhou declared, hiccupping and raising a thumb. “Brother Xueyan is a good friend. He knows what's proper! If anything troubles you, I'll stand up for you. We know a kindness must be repaid. We'll see to it, won't we, Wu?” He thumped his own chest, then patted Wu's shoulder. When A'zhu brought hot tea, he caught her hand and rambled drunken nonsense. She did not know whether to laugh or cry and kept looking at Hu. Her glances seemed both a plea for help and a request for understanding: it wasn't that she welcomed this; she simply could not manage the two drunken men. At last they got Zhou and Wu settled aboard the leading boat. Hu and Zhang could finally talk in peace. Zhang described the evening's entertainment, You's unstinting hospitality, and the ridiculous behaviour of the two commissioners, making A'zhu, who stood nearby, laugh repeatedly.

“When did you come back?” Zhang asked Hu. “Quite a while ago,” he answered casually. “Quite a while?” Zhang turned to look at A'zhu. Feeling exposed, she hurried away. That confirmed his suspicions. He looked after her, then at Hu's silent smile, and gave a knowing smile of his own. “What are you smiling at?” “Zhou and Wu. They've had their eyes on A'zhu all the way. Who'd have thought it? The cat that steals a morsel makes no noise!” “Don't talk nonsense!” Hu pointed outside. “Mind she doesn't hear.” “Then tell me the truth.” Zhang leaned his shining head closer and lowered his voice. “Have you succeeded?” “No!” Hu drew out the word. “Nothing of the sort!” “Then what did you talk about?” “Just idle conversation. Gone as soon as it was spoken. How could I remember all of it?” Hu composed his face. “Enough gossip. Did you discuss any real business with You today?”

“Yes, I meant to tell you. We've agreed to send joint invitations tomorrow to the managers of the Big Three. We'd like you to join us for the meal. We'll discuss the loan at the table.” “Good.” Hu added, “There's something else I'd like to discuss with you. But...” “Why!” Zhang, always ready to make a fuss, widened his eyes. “What's the matter? Why have you stopped?” The words had reached Hu's lips, but he swallowed them. He had remembered how freely Zhang talked. If he entrusted him with remitting Huang Zonghan's twenty thousand taels, the secret would certainly get out. Better to discuss it with You tomorrow. He was more dependable.
