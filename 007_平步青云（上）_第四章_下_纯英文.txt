INSTALLMENT 007
A Swift Rise to Prominence, Volume I — Chapter Four, concluding part

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

Early the next morning, Hu quietly went to Meijia Lane and brought Wang back to the boat. His Honour was radiant and light of step. Everyone supposed that a reunion with an old acquaintance far from home, followed by a whole night of pleasant conversation, accounted for his cheerful ease. Nobody knew that his discreet excursion in ordinary clothes had offered pleasures quite unlike the noisy drinking at Sanduo Hall: a room as small as a boat, with spring's enchantment as deep as the sea. Serious work was to begin that day. Wang summoned Zhou and Wu for a preliminary discussion with Hu. He had intended to pay his calls that morning, but Hu thought there was no need to hurry. “At noon, You and Zhang are entertaining the men from the Big Three. Once the loan is settled, Tongyu can immediately advance the rice. Mr Xuexuan, wait a little longer. It won't take much time—a day or two. Let us put our own arrangements in order first.” “Excellent!” Zhou was the first to agree. “When you call on the judicial commissioner tomorrow or the day after, you can discuss delivery of the grain directly. The assignment will look all the better.” “Very well. I'll take your advice,” Wang readily agreed.

“We won't ask you two gentlemen to the luncheon,” Hu continued. “Merchants are always nervous of officials. With you both present, the Big Three might feel constrained...” “Quite right!” Zhou interrupted. “No explanation is needed.” “But there is an important matter on which we need your help. The paperwork for cash payment by the taxpayer and official procurement must be dealt with at once. I know little of official procedure. What do you suggest, Mr Xuexuan?” “We must leave that to you two,” Wang said, looking at them. “You're experienced men and won't go wrong. If anything requires my intervention, just tell me.” Zhou and Wu exchanged thoughtful glances, both apparently uncertain where to begin. Hu waited a moment, then could not refrain from helping. “I don't think this can be explained clearly in a document. One of you ought to return to Hangzhou and report in person.” “Exactly!” Wu clapped his hands. “That was my thought too. Of course a document is still needed, but it can simply state that the official-procurement arrangement is proceeding favourably and that so-and-so has been specially ordered back to the provincial capital to report and seek instructions. That will do.”

“Good. Which of you will undertake the journey?” At this question, both hesitated again. They had only just reached this prosperous city and were most unwilling to leave before enjoying it fully. Besides, the affair was complicated. If their superiors asked questions they could not answer satisfactorily, the mission would be a failure. Once this apprehension arose, neither dared volunteer. “Well?” Wang was displeased. “It seems I shall have to go back myself.” “That would never do,” Zhou said in alarm. “I'll go.” Now that Zhou had spoken, Wu felt embarrassed too. “Naturally, I should go,” he said. They competed for the duty, though neither wanted it. Unwilling to impose a choice, Wang said, “Then let us draw lots.” “No need!” Zhou declared firmly. “It's settled: I'll go. Brother Wu writes well and should stay to help Your Honour with the documents. I'll leave this afternoon and return to report as soon as possible.”

“There's no need for such haste.” Hu gave a knowing smile. “I'll give you a farewell dinner tonight. You can leave tomorrow.” “Brother Xueyan is right,” Wu agreed. “Important though the business is, half a day won't matter. He and I will jointly entertain Brother Zhou tonight.” Wang too favoured a more leisurely departure. He praised Zhou warmly and hinted that, when recommendations for rewards were submitted, he would speak particularly well of him. Volunteering had yielded a considerable return.

Zhou's return to Hangzhou kept the general affairs officer exceptionally busy. First, a boat had to be hired. Zhou would have preferred one of their original fast Wuxi boats, with A'zhu serving him on the outward and return journeys. But these boats were fast only in name. They were suited to late departures, early arrivals, frequent stops, and leisurely sightseeing. For speed, he needed a different vessel, a double-oared “water flyer.” The second task was more troublesome, and again Hu's idea. Gifts were due to the governor, treasurer, and judicial commissioner in Hangzhou, to the grain intendant, and to the relevant secretaries and advisers in their offices. The foreign settlement, stretching for miles, offered countless rare and curious objects. A skilful buyer could please the recipient at little cost; an unskilful one might spend a fortune on something that attracted no notice, wasting his charms on a blind man. Choosing these dozen or more gifts was no easy assignment. Hu decided to ask You for a guide to take the general affairs officer and Gao to Hengdali, the largest foreign trading house in the settlement, to make their purchases.

Everyone had work to do that day. Wang, Zhou, and Wu remained aboard drafting reports and summaries, carefully weighing every word to show how difficult the assignment had been and how thoroughly and satisfactorily they had carried it out. Hu and Zhang naturally had still more important duties. At noon they joined You in entertaining the managers of the Big Three at a Western restaurant in the British concession, where they intended to discuss business. In the event, no business was discussed there. Courtesans were summoned as usual, and with so many women around, the occasion was unsuitable. After lunch, the party went for tea to the bankers' guildhall in the garden behind the City God Temple. Only then did Zhang raise the matter. Of the three banks, Daheng's manager, Sun, had the greatest seniority. Speaking for them all, he began by saying that money was very tight. “The outlook is poor. People with money want to keep the silver in their own hands, fearing bad debts if they lend it. Frankly, a bank has little money of its own.” This was the customary excuse. There were endless reasons for tight money, any of which could be invented to push up the interest. Zhang and Hu understood perfectly. You, however, had suffered at the hands of Shanghai bankers before and was already prejudiced against them. He was deeply displeased.

“I doubt it's the money that's tight. Perhaps the borrower's name isn't good enough.” His words had an edge, but his manner remained amiable, almost joking. “Our association is down on its luck. If Boss Yu of the sand-junk association asked, the money would loosen at once.” He meant Yu Fushan, a leading shipowner who possessed more than a hundred sand junks. They sailed north to Manchuria and south to Fujian and Guangdong, on routes known by their maritime positions as the Northern and Southern Seas. Yu had made his fortune on those routes and become the richest man in Shanghai County. The recent shift of grain shipments to the sea had brought him still greater profits. Near the Little South Gate he had lately built a vast residence, with imposing towers and halls incorporating Western methods. It counted as another sight to be set beside Shanghai's celebrated Eight Views, such as “Sunrise over Sea and Sky” and “Autumn Waves on the Huangpu.” The sand-junk men and the canal association had once kept to their separate waters, but the new grain-transport arrangements had brought their interests into sharp conflict. You's remark therefore carried considerable weight. Sun found it difficult to answer. “Brother You,” he said anxiously, “we cannot accept a single word of that. All clients are treated alike. As for friendship, your standing with us is exceptional. Very well: tell us what you require today.”

A man of You's standing could not lightly speak harshly. The resentment in his earlier remark had already fallen short of proper grace. Sun's answer now made it sound positively coercive, and You was still more uneasy. He hastily clasped his hands and smiled. “You are too kind. I rely entirely on your help.” Zhang ultimately stood with his fellow bankers, and he too bore responsibility for the guarantee. Sun's inability to withstand You had led him rashly to offer to do whatever was asked. If a transaction were concluded on the strength of one remark and trouble arose later, the banks would suffer, and Zhang would be involved as well. He therefore preferred straightforward business negotiation to appeals to brotherhood, though he expressed himself tactfully. “We're all friends here, and Brother You is a particularly good one. Nothing is beyond agreement. The Big Three are willing to help, and Brother You certainly won't let them lose by it. Isn't that so?” You immediately understood. “Exactly. Friendship is one thing, business another. Here's my suggestion.” He looked at Hu. “Young Uncle, let Mr Zhang discuss it with Mr Sun and the others. Whatever terms are proper, I'll accept. There's no need for us to stay.” Hu privately admired him. He was indeed the leader of an association and knew how to handle things handsomely. “Yes,” he readily answered. “I have something to discuss with you as well.” They rose together, nodded to the bankers, and moved to another table, leaving Zhang full authority to negotiate.

“Young Uncle,” You began, “the loan is one matter; Tongyu's advance of rice is another. Let each stand on its own. I've already ordered Tongyu to begin shipping. Where is the delivery to be made? Will you send someone, or shall I handle it all? Give me your instructions, and I'll have it done in three days.” “Excellent! With you and the old master showing us such friendship, there's no need for more words now. A long road tests a horse's strength; time reveals a man's heart. You'll see in future.” Hu continued, “I'll find out where the rice is to be delivered and report back. Whether we must send someone separately is an official detail I don't understand; I must ask about that too. If it were mine to decide, I would naturally entrust it to you and trouble the brethren to handle it for me.” “Good. Then it's settled. I'll tell Gu at Tongyu to attend upon you. His Honour Wang can give him whatever instructions are needed.” An affair upon which the careers of Zhejiang's senior officials depended was thus settled in a few words. Hu still had another important request.

“Brother You, there's another difficulty I need your help with.” He lowered his voice. “I have twenty thousand taels to remit to Fujian without anyone knowing. Can you arrange it?” You considered. “Is it silver, or a bank draft?” “A draft, naturally.” “Then it's very easy,” You said casually. “Write a letter yourself and enclose the draft. I'll find someone to deliver it and bring back a reply. Would that suit?” “That would be ideal. How long would it take?” “Only five or six days.” Hu was astonished. “So quickly?” “I'll ask someone aboard a steamship to do it.”

Steamships had appeared off the southeastern coast since the arrival of the first British vessel, the Jardine, in the fifteenth year of the Daoguang reign. Soon afterwards, the suppression of opium led to war. In the seventh month of the twenty-first Daoguang year, the xinchou year, British troops captured Zhenjiang and pressed towards Jiangning. They transported cannon to Zhongshan, preparing to bombard the city. The court was deeply alarmed and decided to negotiate peace. It appointed Qiying, Yilibu, and the Governor-General of Liangjiang, Niu Jian, as plenipotentiaries to negotiate with the British representative. A treaty of thirteen articles provided for a war indemnity, the cession of Hong Kong, and the opening of Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, and Shanghai to trade: the opening of the five treaty ports. Steamships of the British company came in increasing numbers. Regular vessels sailed between Shanghai and Fuzhou, but with stops at Ningbo and Wenzhou, the round trip took half a month. How could You promise five or six days? Hu was more puzzled than ever. “I'll arrange it through the British mission,” You explained. “They have ships that sail directly.”

“Oh!” It was a simple answer, but Hu's mind was busy. First, he was struck by the effectiveness of foreign methods. They thought nothing of crossing seas. Business depended upon convenient transport. If others used old vessels while he used new ones, his goods could arrive first and naturally command a better price. He had seen steamships: moored at the landing, they looked like warehouses and must carry enormous quantities. The idea deserved thought. Shipping goods by steamer might make a great fortune. Second, he realised how extensive You's connections were. He could even reach the foreign mission, and he acted promptly and generously. Hu ought to cultivate him wholeheartedly; the connection could be immensely useful. With that in mind, he employed all his skill in conversation, and they got on exceedingly well. He disclosed an appropriate amount about his relationship with Wang. You felt that this man deserved to be called chivalrous. To speak so openly also showed that Hu regarded him entirely as one of his own. What had begun as hospitality offered at his master's command now became a personal willingness to help.

At their table, conversation made them forget the time. At the other, the bankers had reached agreement. Tongyu would be the borrower. The Big Three and Zhang would together lend a hundred thousand taels for three months, with the possibility of renewal. You and Hu would act as guarantors. There was, however, one condition requiring Wang's consent: until the loan was repaid, all remittances of the Zhejiang Sea Transport Bureau's public funds in Shanghai must be handled by the Big Three. This amounted to an indirect form of security. “There's no need to ask His Honour,” Hu answered. “I can agree on his behalf.” “And the interest?” You asked. “As for interest...” Zhang glanced back at the men from the Big Three, then lowered his voice. “The stated annual rate is eleven per cent.” “That isn't expensive,” You said. It was a gracious response. Wishing to strengthen his friendship with You, Hu added, “Nor is it cheap!”

Zhang was shrewd. He had deliberately withheld something. Now, reading their expressions, he knew he must disclose it. Pretending to reproach them, he said, “Don't be impatient, you two! I haven't finished. The real figure is this.” He extended his thumb and forefinger. “Eight per cent?” Hu asked. “Exactly. The other three percentage points are the benefit due to you two for guaranteeing the loan.” “Leave me out,” Hu said at once. “My share goes to Brother You.” “Young Uncle, you're a true friend. But I have still less right to put a surcharge on top. Tell me, Mr Zhang,” You continued, turning to him, “where is your share?” “Mine?” Zhang laughed. “I'm the lender. What has that to do with me?” “That's not the way to look at it. I know that, although you're called the boss, you too are really an employee. To speak plainly, even the emperor doesn't send hungry soldiers on an assignment. I must consider your interests. This has been a difficult job. I'll undertake to borrow for the full three months. Make the interest nine per cent: eight openly, one privately. Treat that extra point as our benefit, which we give to you.” “How could I accept?” “No need for ceremony,” Hu said, entirely supporting You. “When shall we sign the agreement?”

“Tomorrow?” So it was settled. They agreed to meet at the same place the following afternoon, then parted. Zhang had more to discuss with the Big Three. Hu returned alone and reported what had happened. Wang, Zhou, and Wu were delighted and naturally praised him without restraint. Away from other ears, Hu quietly explained the arrangements for the Fujian remittance. Wang smiled with a crease in his brow. “Xueyan, everything is going so smoothly that I'm beginning to worry.” “About what?” “Some unexpected mishap. When things reach an extreme, they turn the other way. Too much joy brings sorrow.” “That depends on us,” Hu answered frankly. “I don't put much faith in such sayings. Unexpected trouble usually comes because this isn't working well enough.” He tapped his temple. “True,” Wang said. “You have a good head, Xueyan. Think: is there anything we ought to have done but haven't?” “You should write two letters: one to Governor Huang, one to Education Commissioner He.” “Yes. I'll begin at once.”

That evening Hu and Wu gave Zhou a farewell dinner at Sanduo Hall. On a second visit they counted as familiar patrons, but under the conventions of a changsan establishment, it was still too soon for an overnight stay. You's friends, however, were an exception. Both commissioners were admitted that night to their hosts' inner chambers. Wang did not pay his calls until the following day. He first visited the local magistrate of Shanghai and asked whether Ni Liangyao, director-general of the Jiangsu and Zhejiang sea grain shipments, who had been transferred from judicial commissioner to provincial treasurer of Jiangsu, was in the city. He learned that Ni had not returned to Suzhou and had established his residence at the Temple of the Queen of Heaven. Wang therefore went there and presented his official visiting card. Ni was an honest man of ordinary ability. Sea transport had gained him a promotion but left him overwhelmed. Huang had impeached him for muddling his duties and sending his family to stay temporarily in Hangzhou. An imperial edict rebuked him: “At a time of pressing military affairs, you summarily moved your dependants to a neighbouring province, causing alarm and uncertainty among the people. Your fault is difficult to excuse. Yet you plead that you were merely making a detour to your native district to visit your aged mother. Why are your intentions so deceitful?” Ni therefore greeted Wang with a torrent of complaints, pushing their official business aside.

Wang had learned much tact from Hu. Instead of showing displeasure at Ni's complaints, he expressed deep sympathy. When the family was mentioned, he asked where they lived and confidently promised to look after them. “I am truly grateful for your concern,” Ni said, his sincerity evident in both voice and expression. “I've heard that you are Governor Huang's foremost favourite. Besides asking you to look after my family, I must beg your help in smoothing matters over with him.” “You are too kind,” Wang answered earnestly. “Wherever I can be of service, I shall do everything you ask.” “Ah!” Ni felt both comforted and moved. “If everyone were as helpful and understanding as you, things would be easy.” After that, their official business went very smoothly. When Wang mentioned delivering the outstanding grain, Ni said he would follow Wang's wishes entirely and instruct his subordinates to give him every assistance. Then he returned to his troubles. He knew Huang's memorial claiming full delivery of Zhejiang's grain was untrue, yet dared not report the facts, defend his position, or argue the point. He could not afford to offend Huang.

“Governor Huang's examination cohort—the yiwei year, the fifteenth of Daoguang—is at the height of its influence. In Jiangsu alone, besides Education Commissioner He, Governor Xu belongs to it. In the capital, apart from Grand Councillor Peng, they have men in almost every ministry. Tell me, where could I go to have my grievance heard?” “Your service is known above, after all. What seems a loss may prove a gain. With your generous disposition, Your Excellency has many blessings ahead.” Ni was a simple, honest man and was moved even by these general words of consolation. Repeatedly clasping his hands, he said, “May your kind words prove true!” He showed no intention of ending the visit. That was an honour Wang could not ignore by taking his leave. He therefore stayed to discuss the political situation and various men in public life. From Ni he learned that He Guiqing had contributed to military funds and been referred to the appropriate board for generous recognition. He also frequently submitted memorials suggesting military arrangements and received favourable comments in vermilion. Imperial favour was strong. Naturally, all this greatly reassured Wang.

Leaving the temple, Wang reflected in his chair upon the journey. Everything had gone more smoothly than he could have expected. He found himself puzzled: did success depend upon ability or luck? Hu's achievements appeared entirely due to ability. His own position seemed the work of luck. Yet that was not right either. However capable Hu might be, without Wang he would still be in distress. There were countless talented men without opportunities. Hu too depended on fortune. And what of Wang himself? Had he not learned administrative affairs from childhood and studied important works on statecraft during his journey south from the capital, he would not have won Huang's appreciation. Even the strongest recommendation would merely have gained him some assignment from which he could make a little money. Huang would never have entrusted him with the weighty task of Zhejiang's sea grain transport. Ability, then, was necessary after all. But ability needed opportunity, and opportunity was luck. At that thought his confusion disappeared. Success required both. Li Guang had never become a marquis because he possessed ability without luck. Luck without ability would bring only a fleeting flowering of success. Now that fortune had arrived, Wang must make good use of ability. That ability lay in Hu. If he won Hu over, Hu's talents would become his own. Pursuing the thought, Wang reached a gratifying insight: a man's greatest ability was knowing how to employ others. Before employing them, he had to recognise their worth. With judgement and skill in handling people, he could make talented men willing to serve him, and his achievements would be immense. He therefore felt that his methods should change. Kindness must be combined with authority. Particularly with Hu, affection should be reinforced by political skill and the most attentive efforts to secure his allegiance.

Within half a month, all their official business had been satisfactorily settled. It was time to return to Hangzhou. To reward his subordinates, Wang gave a lavish banquet. Afterwards he announced, “You've all worked hard on this journey. We don't often come to Shanghai, so enjoy yourselves for a couple of days. Today is the fourth day of the fourth month. We shall definitely sail for Hangzhou on the seventh.” From the inner flap of his boot he took a stack of red envelopes, each bearing a name. Everyone received one, Zhang included. Inside was a bank draft. The amounts varied: Zhou received the most, a hundred taels; the general affairs officer the least, twenty. “Money for the end of your travelling staff,” Wang said, using a literary expression. “To provide for admiring flowers and buying wine.” Admiring flowers meant paying courtesans. Zhou and Wu had already noticed that A'zhu was now regarded as Hu's exclusive concern and could no longer be approached. Fortunately, each had a favourite at Sanduo Hall. With both money and time, they were happy to spend two days there. “You should go and enjoy yourself too,” Wang told Gao, whose red envelope also held twenty taels. “I'm going to stay with my relative for two days. You may take leave.” The relative was an excuse. He was staying in Meijia Lane, a secret still known only to Hu. That evening he invited Hu to a quiet drink in Wanxiang's room. There were “important matters” beyond official business to discuss. In the past fortnight, Wang had visited four times, and their attachment had grown warm. He no longer concealed his identity. Wanxiang was the subject he wished to discuss. After arranging matters for her, he also intended to make arrangements for A'zhu.

Hu guessed half his intention: the part concerning Wanxiang. He already had an idea, but thought it unwise to act rashly. First he must sound her out. As soon as they raised their cups, he said, “Wanxiang, His Honour Wang is going home.” Her expression changed at once and her eyes appeared to redden. Whether this was acting or sincere feeling was impossible to tell. Even if acting, however, it was convincing: sorrow at their parting gathered on her face so thickly that it seemed impossible to dispel. “When are you leaving?” “The seventh,” Wang answered. “So soon!” she exclaimed. “Today is the fourth.” Hu counted on his fingers. “The fifth, the sixth—there are still three days. Plenty of time. If you have anything to say, tell His Honour freely.” “I?” She turned away. “What can I say? Saying it would be useless. It can't be done.” “Why?” Hu pressed. “How do you know it can't?” She turned back, frowning, her lips closed and long eyelashes fluttering in apparent indecision. Several times she seemed about to speak, but finally only sighed and shook her head, setting her earrings swaying.

“Say whatever you wish,” Wang urged, taking her hand. “If I can do it, I will. If I can't, I'll explain why. Don't worry; we can discuss it.” “With whom? Only His Majesty the Emperor!” Her form of address for the emperor struck Wang as novel, especially in her soft southern accent. He smiled. “Is it so momentous that only the emperor can solve it?” “Naturally!” She seemed entirely convinced. “Unless His Majesty issues an edict promoting you to an appointment in Shanghai...” After all these turns, she merely wished to say she could not bear to be parted from him. Wang himself offered no answer for the moment; his helper spoke. “Wanxiang, do you mean that sincerely?” “Oh, Mr Hu!” She looked very solemn. “Must I cut out my heart and show it to you?” “I believe you, I believe you!” Wang hastened to reassure her.

“So do I,” Hu put in with a smile. “Wanxiang, why not return to Hangzhou on the same boat as His Honour on the seventh?” “Why wouldn't I? I'm only afraid he wouldn't agree.” “He agrees a thousand times over. He could wish for nothing better! There are only three days left. Begin your preparations.” Even Wang was surprised. Why was Hu so rashly deciding on another man's behalf that he should take a concubine? But knowing him well, he said nothing and watched. If Wang was surprised, Wanxiang was more bewildered still—and alarmed that pretence might become reality, leaving her unable to retreat. “Well?” Hu asked. “Shall we discuss it directly, face to face? Shall I speak to Third Aunt? Or should Brother You intervene?” Now he was talking about the price of releasing her. It sounded more real than ever. Wanxiang blinked repeatedly and looked awkward, but she was no novice. Composing herself, she sat down and answered with a suggestion of gratification. “Very well. It's my own person; I can negotiate for myself. First, may I ask what His Honour has in mind?”

How could Wang possibly answer? Naturally, Hu replied for him. “Don't you already know what he has in mind?” It was an evasion. His original proposal had been a bluff to test the depth of Wanxiang's feeling for Wang. Their exchanges had now given him a fair idea. It was time to discover Wang's wishes instead. “Of course I know,” she answered. “But I don't dare say it aloud. I can't imagine being so fortunate.” Now even Wang understood that, if he wished to take her as a concubine, she might not agree. Fearing that further discussion would embarrass them both, he put the matter aside. “We can talk about it later. Let us drink first.” That suited Hu exactly. He too thought her intentions sufficiently clear and saw no need to pursue the subject. “Yes, let's drink. You can discuss the rest in bed later.” Wanxiang knew she had lost the advantage. Apparently Wang had no serious intention either. Had she known, she could have made a more generous declaration. Now his position remained concealed while hers was exposed. Her hope of extracting a large sum during the remaining three days would probably come to nothing. Hu had tricked her into this. The grievance in her heart became a mournful expression and silence, which greatly stirred Wang's pity.

“What is it?” he asked, gently touching her shoulder. “Why are you suddenly unhappy?” At the question, she gave herself up to tears. With a sob, she covered her face with her handkerchief and hurried into the back room. Soft weeping followed. Wang felt distressed and, still more, moved. He wanted to do something, but Hu stopped him. “Leave her,” he said quietly. “Their tears are easily shed. Whenever they remember something painful, they can have a cry. It doesn't necessarily mean she's been hurt just now.” Wang felt suddenly bereft. He thought Hu's judgement too harsh, but nodded and remained seated to show his confidence in him.

“Mr Xuexuan, tell me what you want, and I'll arrange it.” “What I want...” Wang paused for a long while. “If I brought her home, I'm afraid people would talk.” His attachment was plain. Hu thought it right that he should consider his official reputation, but felt the point should be made more clearly. “Wanxiang may not want to go to Hangzhou either, so there's no need to consider bringing her into your household. I expect you'll often be coming to Shanghai. Why not keep her in a separate establishment? In the second or third month of spring, or in autumn when West Lake is at its best, you could bring her to Hangzhou for a while. I'd arrange a small house for you. What do you think?” “Excellent!” This exactly suited Wang. “Please discuss it with her and find out what it would cost.” “It would only mean contributing to her monthly expenses. Any extra money you give her when you visit, or jewellery and clothes you have made for her, would be a matter between you two. Nobody else could intervene.” Then Hu called, “Wanxiang!”

She came out slowly. Her powder and rouge had been renewed, but her eyes were still red. Looking fragile and pitiful, she sat close to Wang, frowning slightly and saying nothing. “What were you crying about?” he asked. “Who offended you?” “Ah, Mr Xuexuan, there's no need to ask,” Hu interposed. “Don't you understand her difficulty? If she goes to Hangzhou with you, she can't bear to leave Third Aunt. If she stays, she doesn't want to be parted from you. Torn between the two, she naturally feels unhappy. Am I right, Wanxiang?” Instead of answering him, she turned to Wang. “Just look at you. After all we've meant to each other, you understand less than Mr Hu.” “The bystander sees more clearly,” Wang said, while signalling to Hu to propose their arrangement. Hu gave a slight nod, then returned a glance asking him to leave them alone. “I'm a little dizzy,” Wang said. “I'll lie down on your bed for a while.” Once he had stretched out in the back room, Hu began negotiating with Wanxiang. How much did she need each month?

“I live quite frugally. Twenty or thirty taels at most.” “If His Honour gave you thirty taels a month, could you close your door to visitors and live quietly?” “Nothing could be better. But...” She shook her head and stopped. “Go on,” Hu urged. “Do you have debts? You may tell me.” “Truly, nobody is more understanding than you, Mr Hu. Who wouldn't wish to leave this life? But there are so many difficulties. If one tells other people, they think one is asking for an outrageous sum. Speaking only harms the friendship. Better to say nothing.”

From that tone, the sum would not be small. If she claimed debts of eighteen thousand taels, should they pay them? Hu had heard of a practice called “taking a bath.” A popular courtesan burdened with debts would seize upon a wealthy fool, swear eternal love beside his pillow, and declare that she would marry nobody else. He would spend a fortune paying her debts and buying her release, as though purchasing a bride with pearls. Yet within six months, or a year at most, she would grow restless in married life and devise every means of leaving him. It had been a scheme from the beginning. Wanxiang did not appear quite that sort. Still, even if her demands were met and she agreed to stop receiving clients, her conduct might not match her promise. If she continued attracting admirers while known as Wang's mistress, his reputation would suffer. Wouldn't that be foolish? Hu therefore smiled. “If you have so many difficulties, naturally we can't press you. But you should know that His Honour's feeling for you is quite sincere.” “I know. We all have hearts. Besides, with Fifth Master You's standing to consider, I could hardly fail to do my best. As long as His Honour is here, I shall certainly attend to him wholeheartedly.”

“You've clearly seen something of the world,” Hu praised her. “You speak differently from girls just entering the profession. I suppose I count as a matchmaker, and must consider both sides. Wanxiang, I think you and His Honour should be husband and wife for short periods, two or three times a year.” She broadly understood, but deliberately asked, “How would that work?” “For example, when he comes to Shanghai, he stays here. Naturally, you make yourself available to keep him company. Or, if he wishes, he brings you to Hangzhou to offer incense and see West Lake. Again, you'd be husband and wife for a little while. For ordinary expenses, he'd contribute twenty-five taels a month. There would certainly be extras as well; how much would depend on your own skill.” This was certainly acceptable. “Only one thing worries me. What if His Honour arrives while I'm occupied?” she asked hesitantly. “That would be awkward. Once one sets up in this business, every visitor provides one's livelihood. I can't afford to offend any of them. Mr Hu, I'm speaking frankly. Please don't be angry.” “Frankness is precisely what I like. If an earlier guest won't make way for a later one, there's still a solution. You can stay at an inn with His Honour as Mrs Wang and tell people here that you've gone to the country. Would that manoeuvre work?” Of course it would. Her difficulty solved, Wanxiang was delighted. “Really, Mr Hu, one would think you'd worked in our profession yourself. You know all the tricks!”

“Then shall I become your male attendant?” In a courtesan household, an unmarried female servant was called an elder sister, a married one an aunt, and a male servant an attendant. Only at his reply did Wanxiang realise her serious mistake. One could speak of working in any other trade, but to say that a man had worked in hers amounted to insulting the women of his family. An ill-tempered client, especially a northerner, might take immediate offence. She blushed deeply and hurried to apologise. “Mr Hu, a great man overlooks a small person's fault. I spoke wrongly. I truly deserve a beating.” Taking his hand, she pressed and rubbed it, repeatedly saying, “You mustn't be angry. Punish me however you wish, but please don't be angry.” Her voice grew loud enough to disturb Wang, who could not resist coming out to see what was happening. Hu was smiling silently while Wanxiang apologised in alarm. Wang was more puzzled still. Once the incident was explained, they all laughed and let it pass. Wanxiang's manner changed again. She felt that she now occupied a distinct position, and the division between Wang as host and Hu as guest became more pronounced. Wang naturally sensed it. He felt as unrestrained as though in his own home. They rinsed their cups, poured more wine, and drank with renewed pleasure.

“Xueyan, I have something to ask you too,” Wang said with lively interest. “I hear A'zhu's heart is entirely yours. What is going on?” Before Hu could answer, Wanxiang asked, “Who is A'zhu?” “Ask him.” Wang pointed to Hu. “The boatman's daughter,” Hu replied. “I have no mind for that sort of thing at present.” This vague explanation did not satisfy her curiosity. She pressed Wang for the full story, and he repeated what he had heard, adding considerable embellishment. Where he strayed too far from the facts, Hu corrected him with a word or two. Minor discrepancies he let pass.

“Why, that's splendid!” Wanxiang was eager to intervene. “Mr Hu, shall I be your matchmaker?” Not only Hu but Wang found the proposal extraordinary. “You don't even know her,” Wang said. “How could you arrange it?” “What does that matter? From the sound of it, the match depends upon her mother's agreement. And A'zhu seems to have something on her mind that she won't tell you men. Only if I go can we discover what it is.” Wang thought this reasonable. Nodding, he asked, “What do you say, Xueyan? Let Wanxiang try.” “Thank you, thank you,” Hu answered. “We'll see later.”

“I understand.” Wanxiang deliberately provoked him. “A devoted woman and a faithless man! You must not care for her.” “Now you're wronging him,” Wang laughed. “Whenever he has a free moment, he hides away on the boat. He and A'zhu never run out of things to say.” “Then why doesn't he take her home? Is the first wife formidable?” “He could rent another house and let her live separately.” “Exactly!” Wanxiang looked directly at Hu. “Priceless treasure is easier to find than a loving heart.” “That's what I think too.” Wang raised his voice and recited, “What was ordained in a former life—do not let the destined marriage pass!” They urged him on in turn, each supporting the other. Hu had made up his mind, but there was much he could not say in Wanxiang's presence. He only smiled and shook his head. Seeing that he would not be persuaded, she finally abandoned the attempt.
