CHAPTER SEVEN

This was the first time Hu had heard Old Zhang speak of his daughter. She had “told” one person to do this and another to do that; his tone suggested a servant carrying out his young mistress's instructions. Hu was a little surprised, but he also understood that A’zhu truly was the cherished jewel of the household. Her parents did whatever she asked. “Mr Hu,” Old Zhang continued, “I've brought a little skiff to row you over. It's much too noisy here. Even I find it tiresome. The moat is far quieter.” They went down from the bridge and boarded the skiff. Heading south beneath Wan'an Bridge, they turned east and passed through the water gate into the broad moat. On one side stood the city wall; on the other lay vegetable plots. The space was open and deserted. The Dragon Boat Festival was approaching, and the plum rains brought oppressive, steaming heat. As soon as they reached this place, Hu felt refreshed. “A’zhu certainly knows how to choose a spot!” he exclaimed. “There!” Old Zhang pointed behind him. “That's where our boat is.” It lay beneath an enormous willow leaning over the water, its dense green foliage sheltering more than half the vessel. Without looking carefully, one might easily have missed it. Even before reaching the boat, Hu felt his heart lift. Every thought of the Sea Transport Bureau and the bank vanished. He turned round and kept his eyes on the boat beneath the willow.

Someone aboard was looking out too—A’zhu, naturally. As they approached, he could see her more clearly. She wore a pale blue cotton blouse, washed and starched until it was crisp, with a waistcoat of soft black satin. As always, she held the end of her long black braid and twisted it between her fingers, a habit she could never break. When the skiff drew alongside, the boat hand helped him aboard the larger vessel. A’zhu turned towards the stern and called, “Mother, our distinguished guest has arrived at last! Such trouble to persuade him!” Her mother was cooking at the stern and could not leave the stove to greet him. She merely called back with a laugh, “A’zhu, have a conscience! Mr Hu came the moment we asked him. How can you say he was hard to persuade?” “I wonder who has no conscience?” A’zhu glanced sideways at Hu. “Other people's boats get the long journeys. Ours is only good for short ones.” Afraid that her daughter would offend their distinguished guest, her mother immediately scolded her. “You don't know when to stop! Every word makes it worse.” Then she put down her spatula and leaned out, wiping her hands on her apron as she smiled at Hu. “Mr Hu, what kept you so long? A’zhu has been going to the bow to look for you over and over...”

This embarrassed A’zhu. Her fair cheeks, already tinged with pink, flamed like a sunset sky. She hastily interrupted. “Who's been going to the bow over and over? What nonsense!” With a flick of her long braid, she turned and walked along the gunwale towards the rear cabin. A girl brought up on the water was accustomed to this. She seemed to sway as precariously as a willow in the wind, but was perfectly safe. Hu, however, was alarmed. “A’zhu! A’zhu! Be careful! Don't fall into the river!” She ignored him, though his anxious cries pleased her. Almost without realising it, she slowed down and began taking careful, orderly steps. “Just look at her, Mr Hu!” her mother said with an air of complete helplessness. “Wild as a mad girl. There's nothing I can do with her.” “That's enough of your nagging too!” Old Zhang told his wife. Then he turned to Hu. “Please come and sit in the cabin.”

Inside, Hu immediately noticed fresh paint and an even tidier appearance than before. He nodded. “You've had the boat repaired?” “It needed doing long ago. We could never put together enough money. We have your custom last time to thank.” “There will be more opportunities,” Hu said. “Mr Wang has been appointed to Huzhou, but he still has duties in Hangzhou. He'll often travel between the two. There are bound to be occasions when your boat is needed.” “Then we must ask you to keep us in mind.” “Ordinarily, arranging that sort of thing isn't my business. But your boat is a special case. I'll think of something.” He considered having the Sea Transport Bureau or the Huzhou prefecture hire Old Zhang's fast Wuxi boat on a long-term basis for official use, giving him a fixed monthly income. Before he had decided, A’zhu reappeared with a basin of water. This reminded Old Zhang of his duties as host. He stood. “First make yourself comfortable, Mr Hu, wash your face, and have some tea. What dishes would you like for the journey to Linping? I'll have A’zhu's mother come and discuss them with you presently.”

Once Old Zhang had gone, Hu relaxed. Rising with a smile, he said, “A’zhu, what a temper you have!” “And you still criticise me! Think of yourself. The moment you go ashore, you forget all about us. Never mind that you don't ordinarily visit. When Mr Wang goes to take up his appointment in Huzhou, there's a boat ready for him here, yet he deliberately won't use it. Tell me, is that reasonable?” As she spoke, she stood close beside Hu and helped unbutton and remove his outer jacket and long robe. Her voice was full of grievance, stirring feelings in him that he could scarcely name. When she lowered her head to undo a fastening beneath his arm, he reached out to touch her hair, black and glossy as polished lacquer. She ducked away from his hand and protested, “Keep your hands to yourself! You'll mess up my hair!”

“Do you comb it yourself?” “Naturally! I comb it, and Mother plaits it. Do you suppose people like us are fortunate enough to have maids and serving women to wait on us?” “You might be,” Hu said. “There's nothing so extraordinary about having servants.” At once she raised her eyes and searched his face, then looked down again. Gathering the skirt of his robe, she undid the last fastening, removed the outer garment, and wrung out a towel for him. He saw expectation in her eyes. Clearly she hoped he would explain what he had just said. But he already regretted his rash words, so he pretended not to understand and quickly turned to something else.

The new subject was enough to make A’zhu forget his remark immediately. He unwrapped the bundle he had brought. Inside was a small chest made in imitation of a safe, clad in sheet iron, painted dark green, and fitted with a concealed lock. “What sort of chest is that?” “A treasure chest, like the one in the story of Du Shiniang, who sank her treasure chest in anger.” He opened the lock. This chest, however, contained only four treasures: a bottle of perfume, a musical box, a fine ivory comb of the kind Japanese women wore as an ornament, and a lady's watch in pearl-set cloisonné enamel.

A’zhu was more astonished than delighted. She looked at one thing and touched another, unable to speak for a long while. Hu first placed the ivory comb in her hair. Then he wound the watch with its key, set it by his own gold watch, and hung it from one of her buttons. Next he wound the musical box and let its tinkling notes ring out. Finally he picked up the perfume. A’zhu suddenly cried, “No, no!” He looked surprised. “No what?” “Silly!” She smiled. “Don't open it!” Drawn by the unfamiliar chiming, Old Zhang and the boat hand peered into the cabin, trying to discover what was making the sound. A’zhu gave them no chance to inspect it. With the musical box in one hand and the perfume in the other, the ivory comb in her hair and the watch swinging from her clothes, she hurried to the stern to show her mother her treasures.

Hu heard mother and daughter exclaiming, laughing, and talking. At last the mother cautioned her: “Put them away safely. Don't keep bringing them out when other people are about. A'mao, the Hu girl, has light fingers. Mind she doesn't help herself.” “What is there to fear? I'll lock them in my treasure chest!” “What treasure chest?” “Look,” A’zhu said, presumably indicating its size with her hands. “It's this long and this wide, made of iron, with a hidden lock. I'm the only one who knows how to open it. No one can steal them.” “Oh, a jewellery box!” her mother said. “Silly girl, couldn't someone steal the box as well?” “Ah!” That had not occurred to A’zhu. She soon reappeared in the middle cabin, still delighted, but now looking a little anxious too.

Understanding her anxiety and wanting to reassure her, Hu beckoned. “A’zhu, come here. I have something to tell you.” “Go ahead!” she answered. Opening the chest, she put away the three treasures, leaving only the comb in her hair. Yet she continued to hold the open box and gaze into it. “Do you want to listen to me or not?” “Yes, yes! I'm listening.” She hurriedly locked it and sat opposite him, her chin supported by her right hand and her head tilted as she waited for him to speak. He found the pose so appealing that he too tilted his head and gazed at her. A’zhu's thoughts were apparently still in the treasure chest: she looked at him without seeing him.

She said nothing, and neither did he. Eventually she came to herself and asked in apologetic confusion, “What—what were you saying?” “Why!” Hu pretended to be astonished. “I've been talking all this time, and you haven't heard a word?” Taken in, she became even more apologetic. “I'm sorry,” she said with a conciliatory smile. “I suddenly remembered something important.” “What was it?” It had merely been an excuse. Now that he pressed her, she had to find something to make her story convincing. The more she strained to think, the less she could manage; the less she could manage, the more anxious she grew. Her face reddened, and for a long time she could not produce a word. “Never mind, never mind!” Hu could not bear to torment her. “If it's private, don't tell me.” “Yes, it's something private.” Relieved, she smiled. “Now say whatever you wanted to say. I'll listen carefully.”

“My advice is not to take your mother's warning too seriously,” he said, lowering his voice. “Don't let possessions weigh on your mind...” He explained his view of material things. People should make use of possessions, not become their servants. Naturally one should guard a treasured object against theft. But if fear of losing it prevented one from using it, or caused constant anxiety and distraction, one had become its servant. Better not to own it at all. “So,” he concluded, “you must change the way you think. If something is lost, it's lost. It isn't the end of the world! Otherwise my presents will have done you harm instead of good.” He had made his reasoning perfectly clear, but A’zhu strongly disagreed. “How generous you sound! ‘If it's lost, it's lost!’ You may not mind, but I do.” Suddenly her tone grew resentful. “That's just like you. You can throw something away without a second thought. You have no feeling at all—for people or possessions!” “You say ‘people or possessions.’ Which person do you mean?” “You can actually ask?” She gave a bitter little laugh. “That proves you've long since forgotten that person!”

“How did you manage to think of that?” Hu was a little exasperated. “We were talking about those presents. Why suddenly drag people into it? I tell you not to care too much about possessions precisely because I care about you. Can't you understand even that? Besides, busy as I am, I came the moment your mother invited me. What more do you want? As for hiring a boat for Mr Wang's departure, put yourself in my position. Given my standing with him, would it be proper for me to interfere in such a small matter?” “I know. The general affairs officer arranges all that. But what harm would there be in mentioning it?” “Wouldn't that be interfering? Surely you know there are commissions on these things. Once I intervened, the officer wouldn't dare take his commission. Other people wouldn't know I had a different reason for choosing your boat. They'd say I wanted the commission myself. How could I let people think that of me?” A’zhu gave him a quick glance, then lowered her eyes. Her long lashes fluttered, and she remained silent for some time.

The glance was brief as a flash of lightning, yet it contained regret, apology, admiration, gratitude, and a plea for understanding. It seemed to say: How could I know unless you explained? I don't know as much as you do. I never imagined there were so many things to consider. I thought you deliberately refused our boat because you wanted to avoid me, when really you wanted to help but couldn't. You came as soon as we asked, so you weren't avoiding me either. It seems I misjudged you! And you had to be pushed to the very last before you would explain. I was wrong, but so were you. You should have known how anxious I was. Why didn't you explain when you first arrived? If you'd told me sooner, would I have said all those hurtful things? Perhaps you don't mind, but do you know how bitterly I regret saying them?

However quick-witted Hu might be, he could scarcely unravel all the twists of a young woman's heart. He could see, though, that she felt sorry. He hated spoiling a pleasant occasion, so he dismissed the whole matter with a wave. “There, there. We've said our piece. Don't go worrying about it. Now, tell me something.” His last words were a little louder and seemed so sudden that A’zhu started and looked up with wide eyes. “What has your mother cooked for me today?” “I don't know yet.” “Really? That's odd. How can you not know? Unless...” He had been about to tease her: unless she had spent all her time at the bow, looking out for him. Just as the words reached his lips, he realised that this joke would be unwise and stopped. Though he had said nothing, his mischievous smile remained. A’zhu was sharp too. “Unless what?” she demanded at once. “Unless,” he improvised, “you were angry with me and couldn't be bothered to ask.” “You have no conscience, saying that!” Yet she did not seem angry. She simply turned and went out.

She soon returned with a tray bearing a covered bowl. When she lifted the lid, he saw fresh lotus seeds, lake-grown water caltrops, and gorgon seeds simmered in rock sugar: the choicest and most prized delicacies of the season. Two small dishes accompanied them, one yellow with osmanthus preserve, the other red with rose syrup, vividly coloured as well as richly fragrant. “I've spent all day preparing this one bowl for you. Yet you say I can't be bothered with you! Isn't that heartless?” Hu saw how carefully everything had been peeled, each seed perfectly whole. Only then did he realise the astonishing amount of work she had put in, and he felt deeply apologetic. “Eat!” she said. “Add whichever flavouring you like. I think the rose syrup is better.” “I can hardly bear to eat it. I'd like to keep it here to look at and smell.” “Oh!” A’zhu laughed. “You're just like a child.” With a little silver spoon, she stirred a spoonful of rose syrup into the bowl and pushed it towards him. “It won't taste as good when it's cold.”

“What about yours?” “Mine? I couldn't be bothered to make it for myself. But Father has benefited from your visit. He rarely gets such a delicate treat.” “A delicate treat indeed! The emperor in his palace could have nothing finer. No, wait,” Hu added. “Throughout the year, at every festival, the palace receives seasonal delicacies as tribute from everywhere. But they have so far to travel. How could he enjoy them the moment they first appear?” A’zhu was delighted. “Then are you saying you're luckier than the emperor?” She drew a finger down her cheek to shame him. “Boasting costs nothing! No one in the world has a thicker skin than you.” Then she laughed herself, turned, and went to the stern to help serve the meal.

Hu was not merely boasting. He truly felt a happiness he would not exchange for an emperor's throne. Sitting alone beside the cabin window, with fresh air flowing in, he ate his sweet dish and looked across the level fields and green countryside, his mind light and spacious beyond description. A’zhu, of course, still seemed to be before his eyes. Whenever he thought of her, he could see her and hear her; every frown and smile pleased him. He began seriously to consider their future together.

Before long, his thoughts were interrupted. A’zhu came to lay the meal, wiping the table and setting out bowls and chopsticks. “Four dishes and a soup, with two little plates as well. That should be enough for you. We've yellow croaker today, and water shield.” Before she had finished, her mother arrived with the food: a roast cooked over gentle heat, pork coated in rice meal and steamed in fresh lotus leaves, yellow croaker steamed with pickled melon, and crisp bean-curd rolls. There were also two cold dishes to go with the wine, cucumber with dried shrimps and assorted braised meats. Her mother poured Hu a cup of Zhuyeqing wine, accompanying it with courteous words. “Thank you, thank you!” He gestured at the table. “I couldn't possibly eat all this. Everyone come and join me!” “That would be quite against the rules!” But A’zhu's mother understood his unspoken meaning, and immediately softened her refusal. “Still, drinking alone is no pleasure. Let A’zhu offer you a cup.” This was precisely what A’zhu had hoped to hear. She turned at once to fetch a small wine cup, bringing her silver chopsticks too.

“Mr Hu, exactly when will you need the boat?” “We leave early on the seventh of the fifth month,” he replied. “The journey there and back will take at least two days.” “Better allow plenty of time,” A’zhu put in. “Two days won't be enough.” “Quite right. Let's double it and say four.” “And the food?” “Choose whatever you like!” Hu had come prepared. He took a banknote from the pocket of his short jacket and handed it over. “Take this for now. If it isn't enough, I'll make up the difference.” A’zhu's mother could read. Seeing that it was for twenty taels, she hastily answered, “It's more than enough! We couldn't possibly need so much.” “The Dragon Boat Festival is nearly here. Buy yourselves something to eat with what's left. Consider it my festival present in cash.” She thought a moment. “Very well. I'll keep the balance on account for you. You're sure to use our boat again.” With that, she withdrew.

Hu was too busy eating to talk, partly by design. Before touching his wine he set about the food, which he really did relish. After several mouthfuls of fish, he looked up with a smile. “A’zhu, I've had an idea. If only I had a mother-in-law like this, I should eat wonderfully well!” On the surface it was a joke; beneath it, a test. With a girl who was not sufficiently attached to him, such a remark would have been presumptuous and bound to provoke an angry rebuke. But A’zhu did not hear it as a test. To her he had revealed his true feelings and made a promise. Her cheeks reddened, her heart pounded, and she felt so shy that she longed to jump through the window into the river and swim away from his gaze. Fortunately he had glanced at her only while speaking, then bent over his food again. No one saw her embarrassment, but even she scarcely knew how to manage the turmoil inside her. Suddenly she sprang up and ran. Having acted without thinking, she then worried that he might suppose she was angry. “I'll go and see whether the soup is ready,” she said quickly.

It was only an excuse. With her cheeks so flushed, she was ashamed to face her mother as well. She returned to her own berth, pressed a hand over her pounding heart, touched her face, and kept telling herself to calm down. As she grew calmer, she remembered a conversation between her parents one night. Unaware that she could hear them through the cabin partition, they had spoken freely. “You can't keep a grown daughter at home,” her father had said. “A’zhu has no appetite for food or tea. She seems a little...” He stopped. “A little what?” “As if she's lovesick.” “You wretch!” her mother scolded. “How can you speak so disrespectfully of your own daughter?” “I'm only telling the truth. Am I not?” Her mother said nothing. After a long silence she asked, “What do you think of this Mr Hu?” “He's certain to get on in the world...” “I'm not asking whether he'll get on.” She interrupted. “I'm asking whether you think he has a conscience.”

“You're afraid he wouldn't treat A’zhu properly? I don't think that would happen. But you said you wouldn't let her become anyone's concubine. Why ask this now?” “What difference does my refusing make? A’zhu likes him. What can we do?” “How do you know? I've only noticed that she won't eat or drink. She's never mentioned Mr Hu to me.” “Of course she wouldn't mention him to you,” her mother said. “But she's spoken of him to me more times than I could count. Whatever we're discussing, she finds a way of bringing him in. That passenger we took to Shanghai this time was terribly stingy, wasn't he? When A’zhu spoke of him afterwards, it was always ‘Mr Hu isn't like him’ or ‘Mr Hu is what I call a good passenger.’ There, what do you make of that?”

“Then what exactly do you think now?” “I've come to see it this way: provided the two of them care for one another, I won't worry about her being wife or concubine. Still...” Her tone became respectful towards her husband. “Her father must decide.” “It's hardly for me to decide either. I told you long ago what I'd prefer: an honest fellow, with just the one wife. If life were a little harder, so be it. But you wouldn't agree, and neither would she. Then let the two of you settle it between yourselves.” “She's not my daughter alone. Don't push it all away and wash your hands of it,” her mother said. “Go and find out where Mr Hu lives. Manager Zhang at Xinhe must know. Ask him!” “And once I know? Do you mean to visit him?” “First to see him, and then to see his wife. If she's a tigress, A’zhu must put him out of her mind.”

That had been ten days ago. They had indeed found Mr Hu, and he had come as soon as he was invited. But had her mother seen Mrs Hu? What sort of person was she? A’zhu turned these questions over in her mind. How foolish she was! Why had she taken so long to understand something so obvious? If her mother had gone to the Hu house, she must have met Mrs Hu. Had the wife really been a tigress, Mother would have abandoned the idea herself. She would never have agreed to this short voyage, still less have treated him with such warmth and attention. Clearly, then, Mother had not only met Mrs Hu but found her kind and generous enough to be sure that her daughter would suffer no ill treatment if she married into the household. That was why she had welcomed him today exactly as she would a prospective son-in-law making his first visit. Wasn't it plain? Why had she thought round and round without seeing it? Now she understood, but her heart, only just beginning to settle, grew more agitated than ever.

“A’zhu!” she heard her mother call. “Come and take the soup in!” The call embarrassed her terribly. She touched her face: it was almost hot enough to burn her hand. Surely she must be as red as the pomegranate blossoms on shore. Yet she had to answer. “Coming!” she called first. “Hurry! The soup will get cold.” Desperate, she replied, “Mother, take it yourself. My hands are full.” “What are you doing?” Nothing at all. Like a bowl of hot soup, she was simply waiting there to cool. She washed her face again in cold water, removed her satin waistcoat, and had just begun undoing her blouse when the door opened. Startled, she quickly held the garment closed. “You might say something before coming in!” she complained to her mother. “You frightened the life out of me.” “And why choose this moment to wash?” her mother urged. “Hurry up and help entertain our guest.” How fortunate! Now she had both an explanation for being occupied and an excuse for her flushed face. Very pleased with herself, A’zhu dropped the towel, fastened her blouse, smoothed its hem, and returned to the forward cabin.

Hu had begun his rice and had just finished a bowl. When she reached to take it for a refill, he shook his head. “I've eaten far too much!” “Then have some more soup. Water-shield soup with three shredded ingredients is Mother's speciality.” “Everything is her speciality. When we entertain Mr Wang, serve much the same, with two extra stir-fried dishes and a steam-pot chicken.” “What's steam-pot chicken?” A’zhu laughed. “Qi-gu-ji! It sounds like the cry of those Jiangxi men who mend bowls!” Hu could not help laughing. When he recovered, he said, “So there is a dish you don't know! Steam-pot chicken comes from Yunnan. Mr Wang's family is from Fujian, but he grew up in Yunnan, so he likes Yunnan flavours. I first ate it at his house and learnt how to make it. I'll teach your mother later.” “No, no. Teach me.” A’zhu glanced towards the rear. “Don't let Mother know.” “Why ever not?” “Mother's always saying I'm clumsy and can't cook a single dish properly. I want to learn something she can't make—perhaps something she's never even seen. Then whatever I say about it will have to be right.”

“Very well, I'll teach you!” Hu explained how to make steam-pot chicken. “That's not difficult!” “It isn't. Only the special pot is hard to find. I'll give you one.” He added, “I must taste my new pupil's cooking and see whether it's better or worse than my other pupil's.” “Who is your other pupil?” Hu smiled without answering. A’zhu guessed, and a strange feeling rose in her, vaguely uncomfortable but impossible to ignore. Then she thought that refusing to ask would make her seem afraid of the subject, which would be very wrong. “Mrs Hu?” “Of course.” “Then she must make this dish just as it should be made?” “Not necessarily,” he said. “She isn't much of a cook and doesn't particularly like the kitchen.” “What does she like, then?”

Hu began to suspect that A’zhu was sounding him out about his wife's character. Perhaps her mother, too, had used the delivery of food as an excuse to visit and assess the situation. If he was right, they might expect some definite settlement today. This was difficult. He himself was nowhere near ready to make a final decision about A’zhu. He must find a tactful way of putting them off that would achieve his purpose without hurting her feelings.

“What's wrong? Have you suddenly lost your voice?” A’zhu asked when he remained silent. “I've just remembered something important,” he said to cover his thoughts. “What were we talking about?” A’zhu was no longer interested in his wife's preferences. Biting her lip, she lowered her eyes a little and stared fixedly at him. “Now it's my turn to ask,” Hu said with a smile. “Have you lost your voice too?” “I've suddenly thought of something as well. I want to see whether what you just said was true.” “Do you think I invented that important matter?” “It isn't a question of inventing it. You're trying to think how to leave!” “You do imagine things. Still...” Hu looked out of the window. “It's getting dark, landing here isn't easy, and it looks like rain. To tell you the truth, it hadn't occurred to me until you mentioned it. You've reminded me: I ought to go.”

A’zhu was angry. He had clearly been wanting to leave for some time, yet he had to make that clever remark. Turning outwards, she deliberately drew out her words as she called to the boat hand. “A’si! Put out the gangplank! Our guest is leaving!” “It's still early!” her mother immediately answered. “Stay a little longer, Mr Hu.” “Don't keep him! It's getting dark, it's going to rain, the road will be bad. If he slips, he'll blame you!” Plainly she was speaking in a temper, yet her clear, birdlike voice made it sound only pleasing and amusing. Hu could not bring himself to insist on leaving. “I'm not going,” he said with a smile. “A’zhu is driving me away.” “There she goes, forgetting her manners again. Pay no attention to her, Mr Hu. I'll clear the table and make you some tea.”

By the time the table had been cleared and a fresh bowl of the finest new Longjing tea prepared, the weather had indeed changed. Rain began pattering on the boat's awning. “In the plum-rain season it clears as suddenly as it clouds over. The weather will be fine again in a moment.” Hu understood why A’zhu's mother said this. She feared he would leave—or, even if he stayed, that he would be too worried about darkness, rain, and muddy roads to sit at ease. He never liked to leave others in such anxious uncertainty. Since he was here, he might as well settle down. If he truly wished to leave, he would have to find a way, even at midnight in a hailstorm. Better, then, to be generous about it. “Let it rain,” he said. “If the journey's difficult, it's difficult. It wouldn't be the first time I've slept on your boat.” Mother and daughter immediately brightened. “Exactly!” her mother said. “You can just as well leave first thing tomorrow.” “But I really do have important business this evening. Never mind,” he said expansively. “I'll write a note and ask your boat hand to deliver it.” “Certainly!” Her mother was about to tell A’zhu to fetch the writing things, but the quick-thinking girl was already doing so.

She opened a cupboard and took out a rosewood tray containing a complete set of writing materials: brush, inkstick, paper, and inkstone. They had been provided for passengers but had not been used for so long that the inkstone and inkstick were dusty. A’zhu wiped them clean, dipped a slender finger into her tea bowl, transferred a few drops to the inkstone, and began grinding ink for him. While she worked, he composed the note in his head. Writing was one of his weaknesses. Even these few sentences took considerable thought. He repeatedly smoothed the paper on the table and worked the brush against the inkstone before he finally settled on the wording. The note was addressed to Liu Qingsheng, asking him to tell Hu's household that urgent official business with Wang Youling required an all-night consultation and that he could not return home. It would have been simpler to send A’si directly to the house with this brief message. But Hu feared the boat hand would reveal his whereabouts, so he deliberately chose this roundabout route. With the matter settled, Hu relaxed. Watching him, A’zhu wondered whether he might be afraid of his wife. Then she reconsidered: this was one of his virtues. A dissolute young man would go out carousing and leave his wife alone at home without caring whether she spent the whole night waiting in fear and anxiety.

“Now, then!” he said over his tea. “If there's anything you want to discuss, go ahead.” There certainly was the question of her future marriage, but his putting it so plainly made A’zhu reluctant. “Really, you!” she said. “Must there be something to discuss before I can ask you to stay?” “Even idle conversation needs a subject. A’zhu, how many years have you lived in Huzhou?” “How could I say? We've come and gone so often that I've lost count.” “But you know the place well?” “I'm certainly no stranger to it, though I don't know it better than anyone. Why do you ask?” “Now that Mr Wang is going to be prefect there, I ought to learn something about it.” “Then let me ask you,” she said, suddenly attentive. “Are you going to Huzhou to become an official too?” Hu found that difficult to answer. After thinking a moment, he said, “I shall go to Huzhou often. But at most I'll be half official, half merchant.”

“What does ‘half official, half merchant’ mean? Holding office and doing business?” A sudden inspiration flashed through A’zhu's mind, like lightning revealing the right direction to someone lost in the wilderness at night. Her spirits rose at once. “What business will you do in Huzhou?” she asked eagerly. “Open a bank?” “Not a bank,” Hu replied. “I'm thinking of going into silk.” “Then you certainly must go to Huzhou!” she said with delighted pride. “Our Huzhou silk is the finest in the world!” “Yes. That's why foreigners come to buy it too.” By “the world,” A’zhu meant what people had traditionally meant for generations: the realm within the four seas. Hu had visited Shanghai and learnt something of the West. He now knew that there were skies beyond his own sky and people beyond his familiar world. The world he meant was therefore different from the one she imagined.

“So you mean to buy silk for the export houses!” A’zhu said. “That's always been the business of the Cantonese merchants.” “If others can do it, so can I.” Hu smiled. “You don't seem altogether ignorant of the trade, A’zhu.” “Of course not!” She lifted her face and straightened proudly. “Think where I come from!” “Good! Then tell me how silk is produced in Huzhou.” She knew this was no longer idle talk. If Hu intended to enter the trade, naturally he wanted as much precise information as possible. Afraid she might explain badly or even get something wrong, she fetched her mother to discuss it with them in detail.

“Well,” her mother began, “we raise silkworms in the countryside round Wuxi too. But most of our output is what we call full silk, whereas Huzhou produces chiefly fine silk...” “What is full silk?” Hu interrupted. “There are three grades. The best cocoons are reeled into fine silk. Good and medium cocoons produce full silk. Poor cocoons yield coarse silk. Coarse silk can't be put on the loom. Woven silk requires the fine and full grades: fine silk for the warp, full silk for the weft.” Hu immediately understood why the fine grade was superior. Near Hangzhou's Imperial Silk Weaving Office, in the lower part of the city, there were weaving workshops everywhere. He had heard that only good silk, strong and lustrous, could serve as warp thread.

“In Huzhou, girls know how to raise silkworms by eleven or twelve. It's hard work. The third and fourth months are called the silkworm months. People really do shut out even their closest relations. A sheet of red paper on the door serves as a shield against visitors. They don't even see their neighbours.” “I've heard there are many taboos too. Is that right?” “A great many,” A’zhu's mother replied. “Husband and wife mustn't sleep together, nor must anyone use improper language. The silkworm babies demand complete purity.” She went on to describe the process in detail. From hatching to cocooning, the worms passed through three periods of dormancy, taking roughly twenty-eight to forty days. They had to be fed at set times; even in the middle of the night, someone must rise to feed them without a moment's delay. Temperature was especially important. Doors and windows were kept tightly shut against draughts. If the weather changed suddenly and the room grew cold, a fire had to be lit. Often exhausted members of silkworm-raising households fell asleep unawares, allowing fires to break out and cause disaster. Of course, the worms needed mulberry leaves. Mulberry trees alone were useless, but too many worms and too few trees also caused difficulties. Sometimes people had to discard worms already halfway through their painstaking upbringing. Nothing was more painful to a silkworm-raising household.

Her account brought back what Hu already knew of silk. “Isn't the very best called Jili silk?” he asked. “That's what everyone says,” A’zhu's mother answered. “The place is seven li from Nanxun.” “So it's Qili—‘seven li’—silk, rather than Jili!” Hu exclaimed, pleased with his discovery. “You really must consult those who know the trade.” The characters for “seven” and the first syllable of Jili were different, but sounded alike in their speech. A’zhu was therefore puzzled and laughed at him. “What do you mean, ‘Qili silk, not Qili silk’? A man named Hu isn't named Hu? What sort of nonsense is that?”

Hu smiled without answering. He had no inclination to exchange jokes just then. Seven or eight ideas were turning in his mind. He quietened himself, began to put them in order, and returned to the interrupted subject. “I understand how the worms are raised now. What I still don't quite understand is how the silk is reeled and then sold.” A’zhu's mother explained the traditional method. Water was boiled in a large pot, a sheng measure of cocoons tipped in, and the contents stirred with a wooden stick. Two small reels stood above the pot, with a bamboo tube beneath. Once stirring had drawn out the filament ends, these were passed through the tube, taken once round a small reel, and led to the large reel on the ground. As the silk was drawn off, the pupae appeared. If a thread broke, one stirred again to find its end, continuing until all the silk had been reeled. “Reeling is hard work too,” she said. “Unless the cocoons are reeled quickly, the creatures inside break through them and make them useless. So the whole family, young and old, has to work day and night until it's finished. Imagine standing beside a pot of boiling water, stirring and drawing silk without stopping, with that smell from the scalded pupae. It's real suffering. If the cocoons are damp and soft and won't reel easily, it's worse still. Some won't yield an end however long you stir. They're called water cocoons. You can only fish them out and throw them away—all that work wasted.”

“Hard as it is, there must be happy times too.” “Of course. Nobody in the world would do something that was nothing but misery. Once the silk is reeled and the travelling silk buyers reach town, the happy time begins. Silk prices rise every year. The money from the new silk really does come in useful.” Here was what Hu most needed to understand. He knew the term “travelling silk buyer”: a merchant who brought large quantities of ready silver to purchase silk where it was produced. Those who operated local firms, gathering new silk for the buyers and making a profit in the process, were known as “silk hosts.” Every third and fourth month, lending to the travelling buyers was one of the banks' chief lines of business. Why, Hu thought, should he lend them money to buy silk and collect interest, instead of becoming a buyer himself? “I'd like to try being a silk buyer too. Are there any special tricks to it?” “That I couldn't tell you,” A’zhu's mother replied. “I suppose the first thing is to recognise good silk. The second is to know the market. Prices go up and down each year, but buying the new season's silk is always cheaper.”

“What makes the price rise and fall? If less silk is produced, the price goes up; or if more people want to buy it, that raises the price too. Is that how it works?” “I suppose all business works that way. But,” she added, “I've heard that silk prices are mostly made by people. They're in the hands of a few big firms.” Hu's spirits rose. So prices depended on manipulation by the large operators. That was a game he understood exceptionally well. A’zhu's mother was becoming increasingly animated, and she was discussing exactly what he wanted to know: the buying and selling of cocoons and silk. “Some families sell their cocoons because they don't have enough hands, or for some other reason,” she said. “Cocoon firms buy them. To open one, you have to obtain a broker's licence from the authorities. The trade has its Cocoon Guild. When the new cocoons reach the market, the members agree on the day buying begins and the day it ends. They agree on the prices too; nobody may raise them independently. But country people often lose out by selling cocoons. Unless they have no other choice, they sell silk.”

“Why do they lose out?” “Don't you understand even that?” A’zhu interrupted. “If the cocoon firm beats down your price, you have to sell. Leave them unsold, and the creatures inside break through. Then they're not worth a single copper!” “Yes, yes! I wasn't thinking.” Hu asked another question. “Once the cocoon firm has bought them, how does it sell them on?” “There are two ways. It can sell them to a reeling establishment, or reel the silk itself and sell that.” “Ah, I see. Now tell me about the silk firms. Do they also need a broker's licence from the ministry? And do they have a guild?”

“Of course. There are far more varieties of silk firm than cocoon firm, each with its own business. Only the larger ones are properly called silk firms. The smaller ones are ‘users,’ buying locally for local use. Those that buy and resell wholesale are called ‘transfer houses.’ Then there are Cantonese houses and export houses, which deal exclusively with the foreign devils. Those require even more capital. They may hold tens of thousands of liang in silk, waiting for a good price before selling it to the foreigners. Think how much capital that ties up! And the foreign devils are very cunning. If you ask a higher price, they don't tell you you're expensive. They smile pleasantly while secretly looking elsewhere. There's always someone with too much money tied up, desperate to sell for cash and willing to take a lower price. You go on waiting while they've already bought cheap stock, put it on a steamship, and sent it to the West...” “Wait, wait!” Hu interrupted loudly. “Let me think.”

Neither mother nor daughter knew what he wanted to think about. They saw him frown, tilt his head, and stare into space in deep concentration. He was thinking how to make money out of the foreigners. The great danger in business was lack of unity. Dealing with foreigners should be like the cocoon firms' buying arrangements: this was the price; take it or leave it. Then the foreigners would have to submit. But people's minds differed as much as their faces. Nor could one really blame them. With insufficient capital and no money circulating, they had no choice but to sell for cash, unless... Suddenly he saw it! Unless he could bring all the export houses under his influence. Naturally, no one person could swallow every opportunity in the world. He must bring his fellow merchants together and persuade them to follow his lead.

That would not be difficult either. Who would object to higher prices for export silk? For those who needed to sell for cash, there were two solutions. First: instead of selling to the foreigners, sell to him. Second: if they would not sell to him, they should still refrain from selling to the foreigners. Whatever funds they needed, they could borrow against their goods; he would guarantee them a better profit later than they could make now. If a man still insisted on selling to the foreigners, he must be receiving secret benefits from them, deliberately lowering his own price and betraying his fellows for outsiders. Hu could rally the trade to cut off all dealings with him. Then how far could his defiance go? “That's it! That's it!” Carried away by his cleverness, he clapped and laughed to himself as though possessed, making A’zhu double up with laughter. Her mother was different: she partly guessed his thoughts. “Are you planning to enter the silk trade, Mr Hu?” she asked with a smile. “I mean to become a travelling silk buyer.” “Just as I thought!” she said triumphantly. “Mr Hu means to trade in silk.” A’zhu, of course, was even more delighted. It was not only that she would have frequent opportunities to see him. Her own wish seemed to have come true so quickly that she felt the joyful surprise of an unexpected gift.

“But, Auntie...” Hu addressed A’zhu's mother as ganniang, a form familiar in Hangzhou and inherited from the Southern Song. It could mean an adoptive mother or a paternal aunt, and could be used for any older woman towards whom the speaker voluntarily assumed the respectful position of a younger relation. A’zhu's mother was overwhelmed by the honour. “Oh, I couldn't accept that!” she protested repeatedly, almost alarmed. “You mustn't call me that, Mr Hu!” While her mother demurred, A’zhu grew anxious. To her, “Auntie” meant almost the same thing as his earlier joke about calling Mother his mother-in-law. She appeared calm, but her heart kept racing. He was trying to draw closer, and Mother insisted on refusing. What sense did that make? Before Hu could answer, she spoke. “He's honouring you. Don't be so ungracious!” No one understands a daughter better than her mother. Hu's new “Auntie” immediately grasped what A’zhu meant and realised that there was no need to refuse so firmly. Yet she could hardly take back her words. Best to let the matter pass vaguely, then make no objection when he used the name again. That would settle her new position. She laughed and scolded A’zhu. “Look at you! Now you're the one lecturing me!”

Hu noticed every tone and glance exchanged by mother and daughter, but he was too eager to discuss serious business to attend to their meaning. “Auntie,” he said, “if I become a travelling silk buyer, how would you like to become a silk host?” “You're joking, Mr Hu.” Being a silk host meant operating a local silk firm. “I haven't got a silk firm. Where would I find silk to sell you?” “Never mind. I'll help you open one.” “Open what?” A’zhu broke in again. “A silk firm?” “Yes!” His answer was crisp and confident. Her mother looked from him to her daughter. He did not seem to be joking. But if he was serious, that was even more puzzling. “Mr Hu,” she asked cautiously, “why don't you open one yourself?” “That's exactly the right question!” Hu nodded repeatedly. “Why don't I? First, I'm not from Huzhou, and in business, to be frank, people do take advantage of newcomers. Second, Mr Wang will be prefect there. If I come as a visiting buyer, that's harmless; if I become a local proprietor, people will gossip. Though Mr Wang has nothing to do with it, they'll say such-and-such a firm has the prefect behind it. That breeds resentment and makes business difficult.”

Now A’zhu's mother understood. At the thought of stepping straight into the role of a business proprietor, she smiled until her eyes narrowed to two slits. “So you want me to represent the firm, Mr Hu. But...” Her spirits suddenly sank. “How can a woman take that public role?” “That's no difficulty. Have Old Zhang apply for the licence in his name. Behind the scenes, you'll run everything as the proprietress. Won't that amount to the same thing?” “Oh my! The proprietress!” A’zhu tossed her braid and laughed. “An auntie and a proprietress! I shall have to make myself very agreeable to you from now on!” Her laughter was rather exuberant and brought her father to see what was happening. As he peered in, Hu noticed him and beckoned. “Come in, Old Zhang! There's something I want to discuss with you.” An honest, simple man, Old Zhang was quite constrained in Hu's presence. He perched sideways on the edge of a chair, listening for instructions like a subordinate before his superior. Seeing this, Hu thought a solemn business discussion would be unsuitable and asked A’zhu to explain.

“Mr Hu wants to make you a proprietor!” A’zhu began, in the tone of someone who had no personal interest in the matter. Then she carefully explained his proposal. Old Zhang had never dreamed that obeying his wife's instruction to visit Xinhe and inquire for Hu's address would lead to such good fortune. Yet he had doubts. It was all too sudden and might not be as favourable as A’zhu made it sound. He therefore answered cautiously. “Thank you, Mr Hu,” he said slowly. “It sounds a good opportunity. I do have one or two friends in silk firms. But I'm afraid I might not manage it properly.” “Who is born knowing everything? Listen, Old Zhang. In business, the first thing is to work together; the second is to get on with people. I imagine you're well liked. As long as you follow my advice, I won't promise anything else, but I'll certainly make your life more comfortable than it is on the boat.” He added, “A man must think about the latter half of his life. Drifting about in a boat for ever is no lasting arrangement.” That sentence immediately touched Old Zhang. His wife and daughter naturally found it even more welcome. “That's sound sense, Mr Hu!” his wife said. “He's nearly fifty. He can't go on bearing hard work for ever. Better make plans while there's time. If we can help you establish this firm, we'll at least have a fixed place to return to, like leaves falling back to their roots.”

“You aren't helping me open a silk firm. I'm helping you open one,” Hu said firmly. “And if you already have friends in the trade, so much the better. First, Old Zhang, how much capital would it take?” “That depends on the size. A shopfront, some fittings, and two or three hundred taels in ready money would let you act as an intermediary for customers. That's a silk firm. Buying silk yourself, holding it in stock, and waiting for customers to come—that's a silk firm too.” “Then a thousand taels would be enough to begin?” “With a thousand taels, you wouldn't count as one of the smaller firms.” “Good!” Hu looked round at the couple and their daughter, finally fixing his gaze on Old Zhang. “I won't call it a gift. I'll lend you a thousand taels. You open the firm, and I'll commission you to buy silk. There'll be no interest on the thousand. Repay me when you've made a profit. How does that sound?”

“How could it be anything but good?” Old Zhang answered. “But, Mr Hu, business can make losses as well as profits. What if I lose the whole capital?” “Really!” His wife was indignant. “You haven't even begun, and already you're saying unlucky things.” “No, Auntie.” Hu approved of Old Zhang's attitude. “That's exactly how one ought to approach business. You won't succeed if you look only ahead, neglect what lies behind, and think of nothing but profit. Here is my advice, Old Zhang: don't sell this boat. Hire it out. If the silk firm fails, you can still live on the rent.” Old Zhang stared without speaking. His attention had wandered. Why had Mr Hu suddenly begun calling his wife “Auntie”?

“Father!” A’zhu nudged him. “He's talking to you! What are you thinking about?” “Oh, yes!” Old Zhang collected himself and recalled what Hu had said. “Mr Hu, surely it's too late for this year.” “Why?” “A silk firm needs a broker's licence. I hear it has to come from the capital. Sending the application and getting it back takes at least three months. By then the silk-buying season will be long past.” “Does buying silk have a season too?” “Naturally!” A’zhu's mother laughed. “You don't even know that, Mr Hu?” “Different trades are worlds apart. I've never handled this business. Though,” he added, “now I remember: bank loans to silk merchants are always made in the fourth and fifth months.” “Exactly. The new silk comes on the market then, and everyone wants to sell quickly. First, the lean fifth and sixth months fall between harvests, and people in the countryside are waiting for money. Second, if snow-white silk lies at home until it yellows, its price falls. Some people hold on for a better price, but even they wait only another month or so. Most silk firms do their year's business in a single season.”

This made Hu hesitate, for he had a plan of his own. Once Wang arrived in Huzhou, Fukang would naturally handle the prefectural treasury's receipts and payments, including public funds to be remitted to the provincial capital. Hu intended to use the silver received in Huzhou to buy silk there, ship it to Hangzhou, sell it for cash, and pay the money into the provincial treasury. It was business requiring no capital of his own. Outsiders must not see how this conjuring trick worked; that was why he was willing to help Old Zhang open a silk firm. Now, if the firm could not be established immediately, his plan to buy silk and ship it to Hangzhou would fail.

“What about this?” he asked Old Zhang. “We take over someone else's licence, or rent one. Would that work?” “I've heard of it being done. But I don't know what it would cost. Perhaps three or five hundred taels just for a year!” “Then let it be three or five hundred,” Hu said. “You can't make big money without spending a little. Go to Huzhou tomorrow and arrange it!” Why such haste to act the instant an idea occurred? Nothing was clear yet. How were they to begin once they reached Huzhou? Old Zhang, his wife, and his daughter all found themselves at a loss. Again it was A’zhu's mother who produced an idea. “Mr Hu, why don't you accompany Mr Wang when he takes up his post? We could row straight to Huzhou and deal with things there. With you on the spot to decide everything, it would all go smoothly.” “It would, but there are two difficulties. First, everyone knows how close Mr Wang and I are. I must avoid appearances that might excite suspicion, so I shouldn't accompany him to his appointment. Second, I have a bank about to open. I really can't get away.” “Oh, you have a bank as well?” “Yes,” Hu said. “I represent it publicly. There are major shareholders behind me.”

A’zhu's mother regarded him with still greater respect. She glanced at her husband, then turned back to Hu. “Then the journey to Linping...” “That stands as arranged,” he quickly assured her. “Tomorrow I'll have a banknote made out for a thousand taels, and Old Zhang can take it to Huzhou. He can find a licence and look for premises at the same time, getting the shop set up first. I should come to buy silk around the middle of the month. I suppose the boat can manage without Old Zhang?” “Of course it can,” she replied. “If we're short of hands, we can hire someone temporarily.” By now there was little room for objections. Old Zhang had spent decades rowing a boat. Suddenly to leave it, go ashore, and set out alone for Huzhou with a thousand taels to open a silk firm naturally frightened him. But under the encouragement and urging of Hu, his wife, and his daughter, he at last gained confidence. On arriving, he would first consult a few friends in the trade. After all, he had spent decades travelling the waterways. Even if he could not recognise every danger at a glance, he could judge whether a person's character was good or bad. As long as he held firm to that judgement, he should not be taken advantage of.

They talked in high spirits until late at night. A’zhu's mother prepared a late supper for Hu, and after he had eaten, A’zhu made up his bed herself. They wished one another a good night's rest and went to their separate berths. She had much she wanted to say to him, but being alone with a man in the middle of the night seemed to her a shocking impropriety. Reluctantly, she returned to her own bed. Of the five people aboard that night, only A’si had nothing on his mind. The other four were all thinking about the silk firm, and all connected it in their thoughts with A’zhu. Yet each saw it differently.

Her mother was the happiest. Two knots of worry had been undone at once. First, Old Zhang was nearly fifty. In such ballad books as Flowers Raining from Heaven and Destined to Meet Again, an official “approaching half a century” would “retire to his native place,” buy fields and land, and enjoy a comfortable old age as a country gentleman. Yet her husband was still rowing a boat! Now at last they could return to their roots and have a home in which to grow old and die. Her second worry concerned A’zhu. She was entirely willing for her daughter to marry Hu, but feared that as a concubine she would suffer. Mrs Hu seemed kind, yet there was only one husband. When he lavished affection on one woman, the other would be left lonely. Over time, resentment was inevitable. If A’zhu stayed in Huzhou, however, she would be free of the senior wife's temper, and her mother would be there to look after her. Could any arrangement be better? The thought left her perfectly content.

A’zhu imagined an even happier prospect. If she entered the Hu household, domestic quarrels would be the lesser concern. The very word “concubine” meant accepting an inferior position. How devoted and thoughtful he was to have found a way of giving each of his two households a wife of equal standing! He had not said so yet, but someone so considerate must have that in mind. First he would raise her father's status, making him a proprietor fit to become his father-in-law. Then she would be married openly and properly, with a matchmaker, a phoenix crown, and a ceremonial cape. She would ride in a bridal chair and perform the wedding bows like any other bride. People would call her “Mrs Hu.” No one would know she was only “the Mrs Hu in Huzhou”!

While she indulged these hopes, Hu too congratulated himself on his cleverness. Helping Old Zhang establish the firm was certainly intended, in part, to provide an arrangement for A’zhu. He believed in physiognomy and fortune-telling, though only halfway: half was destiny, half human action, and determined human effort could overcome fate. It was true that one should avoid romantic entanglements when passing from one phase of fortune to another. Yet surely that depended on the form the entanglement took. If he brought A’zhu home and wife and concubine quarrelled every few days, turning the house upside down, how could he concentrate on business? Under the present arrangement, it would be as though he had opened his own silk firm in Huzhou, with A’zhu and her parents doing their utmost to manage it. Naturally he would stay there when he visited. A’zhu would be neither senior wife nor household concubine, but a woman maintained in a separate establishment. They could decide the future as circumstances developed. If the firm prospered, she would have rendered the Hu family a real service. When he brought her home then, his wife would have little ground for objection. His plan seemed to cover every angle and make everything practicable. It was indeed a beautifully calculated piece of wishful thinking.

What he could never have imagined was that Old Zhang's mind had changed. A boatman with little education he might be, but he had self-respect. A’zhu wished to marry Hu and was willing to accept the lesser position; her mother strongly approved. Since mother and daughter were united, he would not oppose them. He had assumed that whether the two women lived together or in separate households, A’zhu would in any case be in Hangzhou. He could carry on his own business, keeping out of what did not concern him, and no one would gossip. Now it was different. Opening a silk firm and becoming a proprietor was certainly a miraculous rise, more than he could have wished for. But people would inevitably ask, “How did Old Zhang the boatman suddenly become a proprietor?” And what followed would be ugly. He could hardly go about explaining to everyone: “A’zhu is becoming Hu's concubine purely because she loves him. The silk firm is for his own business convenience. Even without the marriage, he'd still open it and still ask me to put my name to it and become the proprietor!” Even if he could say all this, others might not believe him. He therefore resolved that opening the silk firm and A’zhu's marriage to Hu must never be mixed together.

“Listen,” Old Zhang said softly, nudging his wife as they lay in their berth. “Have you spoken about A’zhu's future?” “No.” “Then why does he call you ‘Auntie’?” “He's being polite and honouring us.” “It is an honour. But cold rice and cold porridge are easy to swallow; cold, cutting words are not.” “What cutting words?” his wife asked in surprise. “Who's been gossiping?” “No one. It's just something I've been thinking...” “There, that's enough!” she interrupted impatiently. “Don't fail to recognise good fortune when it comes! Think about what matters. Which friends will you look up in Huzhou? Where will you find premises?” Old Zhang's feelings towards his wife were seven parts affection and respect, three parts fear. From her tone, he knew that explaining his thoughts would provoke a serious quarrel that very night. He therefore kept them to himself.
