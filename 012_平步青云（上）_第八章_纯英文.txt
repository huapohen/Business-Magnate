CHAPTER EIGHT

Fukang Bank opened for business. Its premises had been handsomely fitted out, and the four clerks behind the counter, all in brand-new gowns of imported blue cotton, greeted everyone with smiles. Liu Qingsheng wore a silk gown and a gauze jacket. His face glowing, he personally attended to customers with tremendous energy. Hu Xueyan received the fellow bankers and guests from official and commercial circles who came to offer their congratulations. Fat Zhang of Xinhe and Sun Deqing of Dayuan were both there. Everyone knew that Hu could get a hearing even with the governor, and might someday be needed to put in a word. Moreover, he was exceptionally well liked. His fellow bankers therefore gave him enthusiastic support, placing several tens of thousands of taels in goodwill deposits. Freshly cast horseshoe ingots and round silver ingots, dazzlingly bright, lay in casual heaps behind the counter, making passers-by stare. A banquet was served at noon, and it was after three before the guests dispersed. Left quietly alone, Hu considered the situation. The first day had gone well, but he must secure a few substantial accounts before the business could really develop. His first step must be to build a name. Once Fukang was well known, business would be brisk.

Suddenly an idea came to him. He sent for Liu. “Make me a list.” He carried a small notebook filled with symbols intelligible only to himself. It contained accounts of his dealings, the names of his acquaintances, and the birthdays of important officials and their mothers, wives, concubines, sons, and daughters. Consulting it, he dictated a series of account names: Fuji, Xiangji, Heji, Shende Hall, and others. When Liu had written them down, he asked, “Are we to open passbooks?” “Exactly. How many are there?” Liu counted them with the shaft of his brush. “Twelve altogether.” “Put twenty taels in each. Two hundred and forty in all. Charge it to my account.”

Only after Liu had completed the arrangements and brought the twelve passbooks did Hu explain the device. The names belonged to members of the governor's and provincial treasurer's households. Once accounts had been opened, deposits provided, and the passbooks presented, business would naturally follow. “The ladies' private savings may not amount to much,” Hu said. “They may scarcely count as business. But once word gets about, people will see Fukang's connections in quite a different light.” “So that's it!” Liu nodded in understanding. “How are we to get these passbooks inside?” “A good question! Tomorrow, take my card to Mr Liu Er at the governor's gate. The Fuji passbook is for him. Ask him to deliver the others. Cultivate him properly. Noon is the best time to go, when he has less to do. You might invite him out to a restaurant and ask him to explain the ways of official life. Our business depends entirely on knowing official circles and keeping well informed.” Liu agreed repeatedly. Hu's decision to let him approach Liu Er was a sign of trust, and he was very pleased.

At noon the following day, Liu went expressly to call on Liu Er, as Hu had directed. Since they shared a surname, he addressed him as “Second Uncle.” The affectionate form of address won Liu Er's goodwill; respect for Hu helped too, and the reception was most courteous. The eloquent young manager delivered a series of compliments and expressions of admiration before turning to business. He produced the Fuji passbook and presented it with both hands. Liu Er opened it, saw the twenty-tael deposit already entered, and asked in surprise, “What does this mean?” “We hope Second Uncle will favour Fukang with his business, so we have paid a little interest in advance.” Liu Er laughed. “Your proprietor's ideas really are like nobody else's!” Then, with a little hesitation, he added, “Now I can hardly avoid doing business with Fukang. Here, someone has just repaid me a sum. I'll deposit it with you.”

He opened the visiting-card box beside him, took out two notes, and handed them to Liu. At first glance they looked unlike the usual notes issued by remittance houses or private banks. Closer inspection confirmed it. They were printed on bark-fibre paper, with the words “Board of Revenue Official Note” in both Manchu and Chinese. The central inscription read, “One Hundred Taels of Full-Fineness Silver, Treasury Standard Weight.” Below came several lines: “These official notes are issued by the Board of Revenue with imperial sanction. For any person wishing to exchange official notes for silver or cash, they shall be treated on the same basis as silver. They may also be tendered as a proportion of payments due to the government, in accordance with regulations laid down by the Board. Counterfeiters shall be punished according to law.” Liu had not even known such official notes existed. “I haven't seen these in circulation,” he said with a smile. “This is an education for me.” “They have only just come into use in the capital,” Liu Er replied. “I hear the provincial treasury has already sent someone there to collect a supply. They'll soon be circulating here.”

One of the notes was for a hundred taels and the other for eighty. Liu entered their receipt in the passbook, although they were not yet in local circulation. He then produced the remaining passbooks and asked Liu Er to deliver them. “That's easily done. Leave them all to me. Now, what else can I do for you?” “You are too kind, Second Uncle. That is all.” “Then I won't keep you. Between ourselves, I have official business to report upstairs. We'll talk another day.” Leaving the governor's office, Liu did not return directly to Fukang. He stopped at Dayuan to see Sun Deqing, producing the two official notes for everyone to inspect and hoping to learn how the affair had come about. “I'd heard a vague report that official notes were to be issued. There seemed to be no further news, and now here they are! The authorities have been quick.” “The need for military funds is urgent. They have to be quick,” said another Dayuan shareholder. “I expect they'll soon be circulating in Zhejiang.” “I wonder how many of these notes they intend to issue. They say anyone wishing to exchange them for silver or cash will be treated as though holding silver, but if there are too many notes and too little actual silver, then...” Sun shook his head and left the rest unsaid.

Liu understood his meaning and became wary. Back at the bank, finding Hu still there, he described his visit to Liu Er and finally mentioned the official notes. Hu examined them carefully. “Business grows harder every day, but the greater the difficulty, the greater the opportunity. Qingsheng, there will be all sorts of possibilities in these notes. Think them through carefully.” “I believe they'll certainly lose their value.” Hu thought this too sweeping. Confidence had to be sustained by everyone. If the notes were not issued excessively, if sound regulations were established and the notes could be used conveniently, then, with support from private banks and remittance houses, they ought to retain a stable value. Otherwise, the abuses that might arise were beyond imagining. What he wanted Liu to think through was the various problems that might develop if confidence in the notes weakened: how to avoid those problems, and even how to profit from them.

“Remember this,” he said. “Everything in the world has two sides. When one side gains, the other loses. That is particularly true of business. Buyer and seller, one taking in and the other paying out, are natural opponents. Sometimes the advantage lies in buying, sometimes in selling. A capable businessman must gain both ways: sell when prices have risen far enough, buy when they have fallen far enough. That is how you gain on both sides.” Liu was intelligent; he merely lacked experience. He understood Hu's guidance and felt he had learned a great deal. But how was one to anticipate the rises and falls? By one's own judgement, of course—but where did that judgement come from? He put the question to Hu, who looked gratified. “An excellent question. However shrewd you are, however perfectly you work your thirteen-column abacus, counting every penny in and out without the slightest error, that isn't the great thing. What matters most is vision. The larger your business, the farther you must look. In a small business, for instance, if hot weather comes early and you think the summer will be long, you stock up on palm-leaf fans ahead of time. That too is vision. But a great business requires a view of the whole situation. If your vision takes in a province, you can do business throughout that province. If it takes in the empire, you can do business throughout the empire. If it takes in foreign countries, you can do business abroad.”

Liu had never heard anything like this. Along with heartfelt admiration, he felt some bewilderment. “Then may I ask, Mr Hu, what is your own view of things at present?” “I look at the whole empire.” Hu usually spoke with easy assurance. Now his expression grew grave, as though he bore a heavy burden on his shoulders. “The Longhairs will achieve nothing lasting. They are bound to be defeated. But it won't happen in two or three years. There is a great deal of fighting still to come. The guiding purpose of my business is to help the imperial armies win.” “Mr Hu,” Liu murmured, frowning slightly, “I still don't quite understand.” “Then I'll put it plainly. I'll undertake any business that helps the imperial armies win, even at a loss. You must understand that it isn't really a loss: it is capital invested. Once the imperial armies win and peace returns, what business won't prosper? By then you will have made a contribution. The government will naturally repay you, and every path will be open to your business. Think of it! How could you fail to rise?” Liu immediately thought of Wang Youling. It was Hu's foresight in investing capital in Wang that had brought him to his present position. With delighted understanding, he said, “I see! I see!”

This understanding changed his view of the official notes. Previously he had assumed that they would depreciate and ought to be avoided as far as possible. Now he believed their first need, on being issued, was help in establishing themselves. As Hu had said, confidence had to be sustained by everyone. Since he had authority to act for Fukang, he resolved then and there to support the notes with all his strength. Two days later, the bankers' guild circulated a notice summoning its members to discuss how the official notes were to be distributed—in practice, how the obligation to take them was to be apportioned. Besides the Board of Revenue silver notes, there were notes denominated in copper cash. The guild's presiding officer for the year brought samples, which passed from hand to hand. Their form resembled that of the silver notes. Across the top stood “Great Qing Treasure Note”; down the centre, “Equivalent to ... Standard Copper Cash.” At the sides were the words, “Currency throughout the realm; equal value in payment and receipt.” The inscription below read, “This note shall circulate in place of standard copper cash. It may also be used, in the prescribed proportions, to pay land and poll taxes, grain levies, and all other taxes and contributions. All treasuries, in the capital and the provinces, shall accept and remit it.” The presiding officer explained, “The authorities have assigned us two hundred thousand taels in silver notes and a hundred thousand strings in cash notes. At the prescribed rate of two thousand cash to one tael, those hundred thousand strings equal fifty thousand taels. The total is therefore two hundred and fifty thousand taels.” He paused. “How should we apportion the distribution among the large and small banks? Let us discuss it together.”

“Since the ministry has issued these notes, the market cannot refuse to use them. But that depends on people trusting them,” said Fat Zhang. “If a customer wants actual silver, a bank cannot insist that he accept paper. I suggest this: let the guild collect the silver and cash notes from the provincial treasury and distribute them fairly among the banks, according to their size. We shall use as many as we can, and settle every ten or fifteen days, paying in actual money for whatever we have put into circulation. The banks will take no profit; we shall perform the service for nothing.” Though the proposal offered no profit, it imposed no risk either, and the bankers promptly voiced their approval. The presiding officer shook his head. “That won't do. The authorities wanted full payment. After lengthy negotiations, they agreed to accept sixty per cent immediately, with the remaining forty per cent paid over two months.” They looked at one another in dismay. Everyone knew that the presiding officer had always defended the trade's interests zealously. If a favourable condition could be obtained, he would fight to the utmost for it. What he could not secure, no one else could. The only question left was how to divide the obligation.

Here, too, opinion split. The larger banks wanted an equitable allocation according to size; the smaller ones wanted the larger houses to subscribe first, so that if the total was covered, nothing need be imposed on them. Argument followed argument, with no resolution. As a newcomer and a junior, Liu had not ventured to speak first. But when all the influential men had spoken without reaching agreement, he felt he should no longer hold back. “I have a view which I should like to submit to my seniors for correction,” he said. “We pay sixty per cent and receive the full value in notes. That amounts to an interest-free government loan of forty per cent. The calculation is not unreasonable. Besides, these notes have only just been issued. We cannot yet know how they will turn out, but we ought to look for a good outcome rather than assume a bad one. Everyone will use them, certainly, but the banks have the closest interest of all. If confidence in the notes fails, we shall be the first to suffer. We must therefore help establish that confidence.” “Well!” Fat Zhang interrupted with his usual frankness. “Who would have expected young Liu to produce such an argument?” “A sound argument!” The presiding officer nodded at Liu with warm approval. “Please continue, my young friend.”

Encouraged, Liu became still more animated. “Fukang has only just opened, and our standing is still modest. But where loyalty to our fellow bankers is concerned, we shall never hold back. Whether the notes are allotted or taken by voluntary subscription, Fukang is willing.” “If we proceed by subscription, how much would Fukang take?” The presiding officer had recognised Liu's attitude and deliberately asked the question, hoping to make him an example. Liu calculated at once. There had been eight large banks; with Fukang there were now nine. The smaller banks still numbered thirty-three. If the two hundred and fifty thousand taels were divided on the basis of one share for a large bank and half a share for a small one, each full share would be approximately ten thousand taels. Quick at mental arithmetic and prompt in decision, he had his answer in an instant. “Fukang will undertake twenty thousand.” “Excellent!” The presiding officer was delighted. “The beginning is the difficult part. Once someone begins, it is no longer difficult. If all the large banks follow Fukang, that will account for a hundred and eighty thousand. Divide the remaining seventy thousand among the smaller houses, and the business is settled.”

“Very good!” Sun Deqing came to Liu's support. “Dayuan will also take twenty thousand.” Fat Zhang supported him too, but differently. By deliberately placing his own contribution below Fukang's, he made its standing shine all the brighter. “Xinhe takes fifteen thousand!” he called. Some subscribed fifteen thousand, others ten thousand. The smaller banks offered two or three thousand each. When the figures were totalled, the two hundred and fifty thousand were not enough to satisfy the subscriptions: Fukang and Dayuan actually had to surrender part of their allocations. The presiding officer, whose surname was Qin, ran a small bank himself. An elderly man of respected character, he enjoyed considerable esteem among his fellows. Liu's readiness to act had enabled him to discharge his responsibility successfully, and he was grateful. The others, too, admired how handsomely Liu had acquitted himself. Fukang's name immediately became prominent both in official circles and among the bankers. News soon reached Hu, who was deeply pleased. “Qingsheng, to tell you frankly, Fukang was so new that I couldn't know how it would turn out. That is why I dared not leave. Now I can go to Huzhou with an easy mind.” “And I'll speak frankly too, Mr Hu. If you hadn't taught me the other day to look farther ahead, I would never have dared commit myself so freely to taking those notes.”

Everything was arranged. Naturally, Hu would travel in the Zhang family's boat. His luggage had already been sent aboard, and they were waiting only for him to embark when unexpected news arrived: Lingui had been transferred. Commissioner Zhou had come expressly from the Sea Transport Bureau to tell him. “Treasurer Lin's brother is a secretary to the Grand Council. He sent a special messenger. The treasurer is to act as provincial treasurer at Jiangning; the imperial edict should arrive soon. But...” Zhou's expression became grave and secretive. “There is one matter in which we must have your help.” Whenever he could help, Hu spared no effort. He answered earnestly, “Tell me.” “Treasurer Lin has a personal deficit of more than twenty thousand taels in the accounts. Ordinarily that would be nothing, but you know Governor Huang's character. We must leave no handle in his grasp. The treasurer therefore hopes you can arrange a loan to cover the deficit first. The Jiangning post is much better than Zhejiang. Once he takes office, he can certainly repay the whole sum within six months. Xueyan...” Zhou lowered his voice still further. “He has approached you only because he knows your courage and loyalty, and how discreetly and reliably you conduct affairs. Everything else goes without saying.”

“When will the money be needed?” “Within ten days.” “Very well. Consider it settled.” Hu agreed so readily that Zhou almost doubted him. After a moment's silence, he asked, “And the interest?” Hu thought, then raised one finger. “One per cent a month?” “How could I dare charge one per cent? Extortionate interest is against the law.” Hu laughed. “If I ask too much, my conscience troubles me. If I ask too little, His Honour may think I want some other favour. So I'll ask only one-tenth of one per cent a month.” “At that rate, won't you be making up the interest yourself?” “Not necessarily. In these troubled times, plenty of wealthy families are willing to leave their silver in a bank without interest, provided their principal is safe.”

“That is another matter,” Zhou said, deeply moved. “Xueyan, frankly, I have never met anyone who stands by a friend as you do. Let us deal openly with one another. You need not stand on ceremony. Treasurer Lin still holds his seal and can fire a few parting shots before he leaves. If there is anything he can do for you, some favour that costs him nothing, speak freely.” After such words, further professions of disinterest would seem artificial and distant. Hu considered briefly. “Nothing occurs to me immediately. But once His Honour takes up his new appointment, there will be public remittances between Jiangning and Zhejiang. If he could favour Fukang by directing that we handle them, so that I might extend the business, I should be deeply grateful.” “A small matter. I can promise you that myself.”

As soon as Zhou left, Hu sent for Liu, explained the affair, and asked him to assemble twenty-five thousand taels for Treasurer Lin. “The money is available, but I fear we cannot spare it for so long,” Liu said. “We have already issued a draft for the twelve thousand due on the official notes. Deposits are still limited. We cannot make up twenty-five thousand unless we use the goodwill deposits from the other banks, and those can be used for no more than a month.” “With a month, what is there to fear? Peel the radish as you eat it, a section at a time. The first revenue collection is not over. Huzhou is still collecting silver, grain, and land and poll taxes. Cash will arrive in ten or fifteen days. Qingsheng, we must keep our business flexible. The skill lies in moving funds from one place to another without letting a gap show. Think of what ‘managing funds’ means: you forecast when money will come in and put it to use beforehand. That is how you stay ahead of other people.” Liu understood the principle, but since he was not the proprietor, his nerve was naturally less robust. Hu's words emboldened him. “In that case, we may as well do it handsomely. Let us send the silver early.”

“Quite right. Go yourself. From now on, you will represent us whenever this sort of thing arises. Take blank drafts and the correspondence seal, and ask Commissioner Zhou how they should be made out. Take several drafts.” “Very well. What about the promissory note?” “Let them write it as they please. Even a simple receipt from Treasurer Lin will do.” This was wholly contrary to banking practice and entailed considerable risk. But Liu already knew that his proprietor was an unusual man, so he said no more. Taking the seal and several blank drafts, he went to see Zhou. Hu gathered his personal necessities, intending to hear Liu's account on his return and then embark for Huzhou immediately.

He waited a long time. Not until dark did Liu return, wearing the triumphant expression of a card player who had collected from all three opponents and won handsomely. From his face, Hu guessed that there was unexpected good news; that the mission itself had succeeded went without saying. To train him in composure, Hu gave him a gentle reminder. “Qingsheng! Take your time in telling me.” Liu was quick enough to recognise that Hu's tone and manner were a mirror, reflecting his own exuberance and impatience. With an embarrassed smile, he sat down, put aside a bundle wrapped in a handkerchief, wiped his perspiration, and began calmly. “I saw Treasurer Lin. He was exceedingly courteous. Everything is settled. His eldest son has signed the promissory note, with Commissioner Zhou as intermediary and guarantor.” He offered the document to Hu. “I needn't see it.” Hu waved it aside. “Did the treasurer have anything to say?” “He said he was deeply obliged to Fukang. He has also given Zhou instructions on two matters. Both are truly unexpected good fortune.”

His excitement returned, understandably enough: the news was genuinely encouraging. Zhou had told him that the guild's report on taking up the official notes had reached the provincial treasury, with special praise for Fukang's enthusiastic subscription. Lingui was pleased that the public business had been concluded so satisfactorily. Moreover, because his relationship with Fukang was now different, he had decided to report to the ministry and request an imperial commendation. Liu thought this would bring great prestige among their fellow bankers. “That is your achievement,” Hu said. “And a commendation would bring more than prestige. It would have a considerable effect on business. Once even the ministry knows Fukang's name, the remittance houses in the capital will regard us differently. We shall be well placed when large public remittances are to be handled.” This was a deeper view, and Liu stored it away. He then turned to the second matter. “This one will help our immediate business enormously. But first, Mr Hu,” he abruptly broke off to ask, “what exactly are ‘supplementary military funds’?”

The term had only recently come into use. Hu had heard Wang Youling and Yang Yongzhi discuss it, and could give a detailed explanation. “The Board of Revenue's annual receipts are limited. It should collect forty million taels a year, but in fact receives fewer than thirty million. The military appropriation covers only the ordinary establishment. Now, with the Longhairs to fight, troops and volunteer forces have been recruited, suddenly adding well over a hundred thousand men. Where is the money to come from? Under the Ming, in such circumstances, an additional levy was imposed on every mu of taxable land. But the Great Qing has promised ‘never to increase taxation.’ The only course is for provinces where there is no fighting and trade remains comparatively stable to make a greater effort and find ways to help meet military expenses. That is what ‘supplementary military funds’ means. They are not sent to the ministry, but directly to the supply offices of the major camps.” “Then that explains it,” Liu said. “Treasurer Lin has ordered that Zhejiang's supplementary remittances to the Jiangnan Grand Camp should be handled by Fukang as far as possible.” “Splendid!” Now even Hu could not conceal his delight. “I've been considering how to extend our business to Shanghai and Jiangsu. Heaven has granted my wish. Excellent! Excellent!” “But, Mr Hu, this means you cannot leave for Huzhou just yet. I have no experience in this field. You will have to handle it personally.” “Very well. Leave it to me. I shall need Fat Zhang's help to get it arranged. First, we must find out whether the Jiangnan camp's supply office is at Suzhou or somewhere else.”

He stood, intending to go straight to Xinhe at Yanqiao. Then he reconsidered. An undertaking of this importance still rested solely on Liu's report. How much money was involved? What would the remittance charges be? Were there any other arrangements beneath the surface? He knew none of these things, and therefore had no basis for discussion. He should at least see Zhou and secure the business before asking a fellow banker for advice. If it came to nothing, he would have furnished others with something to talk about, damaging Fukang's credit. He composed himself and sat down again. “Huzhou must certainly wait a few days. This is good news, but we must be careful. Impatience will not help. An affair like this is also sure to arouse envy among our fellow bankers, so we must be cautious in what we say.” It was a warning to Liu not to let success turn his head. He needed it. Since opening, everything had gone smoothly, and without realising it he had acquired a rather self-important air. Hearing Hu's reminder, he reflected calmly, felt his face grow hot, and lowered his eyes. “You are right, Mr Hu,” he answered earnestly.

Hu was very pleased by his manner. “Qingsheng!” A little moved himself, he patted the young man's shoulder. “Our enterprise still has a long way to go. We have only just begun. This little success is nothing. My worry is that the twenty-four hours in a day are not enough. Give me a good assistant, and you'll see what a business I can build. I mean to extend it to the capital, and abroad. The real achievement is to do what others cannot. Work hard. Whatever I gain, you will share.”

Hu not only felt that the hours of the day were insufficient; he wished he could divide himself in two. At that very moment, two places required his personal and immediate presence before matters could be arranged. One, of course, was Huzhou. Old Zhang needed him to inspect the proposed silk firm and make a decision. In addition, Wang had sent a special messenger with a letter asking what should be done with the taxes collected in the first instalment: should they be remitted, or could some arrangement be made to employ them locally? Wang had received orders to inspect the defences along the Zhejiang-Anhui border and could not wait long. He urged Hu to come to Huzhou at once. The other place was Shanghai. Hu had now seen Zhou. Zhejiang's supplementary military funds had originally been dispatched in actual silver. But with the Jiangnan Grand Camp besieging Jinling and the Jiangbei Grand Camp surrounding Yangzhou, neither land nor water routes could be relied on to remain secure. After discussions with the Jiangnan camp's supply office, it had recently been decided to remit the money either to Shanghai or to Suzhou, with instructions sent whenever the need arose. The supply office had stationed a commissioner in Shanghai and wanted a responsible representative of the Zhejiang bank to meet him there and settle the details. Liu could not handle this. Even if he could, with one of them in Huzhou and the other in Shanghai, no one would remain to manage the Hangzhou bank. After lengthy thought, Hu sighed. “Nothing is more troublesome than a shortage of people. From today, you too must watch for good assistants. At present it is as though food were set before us and we couldn't eat it. Isn't that a pity?”

“What are we to do if we can't eat it?” “What else can we do? Invite someone to help us. These supplementary funds for the Jiangnan camp...” Hu paused. “What sort of man is old Sun at Dayuan?” Liu understood. “You couldn't find a more upright and dependable man. But Fukang's connection with Xinhe is a special one. Why not share the business with them?” “Didn't you want to establish an affiliation with Dayuan? The principle is simple. To enlarge our field of business, we must widen our connections. When another opportunity for joint business arises, we shall find yet another firm. If we go on that way, every bank will have an interest bound up with Fukang. Imagine how strong we shall become.” Hu excelled at drawing on other people's strength, but he always proposed cooperation on terms beneficial to both sides, so others gladly placed their abilities at his disposal. Sun Deqing was a case in point. He was deeply grateful that Fukang would share the Jiangnan remittance business with him. Being asked to represent them in Shanghai seemed a further mark of respect. He became a wholehearted supporter of Fukang and volunteered to convert his twelve-thousand-tael goodwill deposit into a long-term interbank loan. Half the loan to Lingui was thus provided for.

The other half was covered by an unexpected deposit. On the evening before Hu's departure for Huzhou, a military officer arrived carrying a very heavy sack. He asked specifically for “Proprietor Hu.” Liu attended to him himself, offering tea and tobacco. “Please sit down. Our proprietor is leaving for Huzhou and has already gone aboard his boat. You may tell me whatever you wish to say; it will be the same.” “No. I must speak to him in person. Could he be asked to come back, or could I go to his boat?” There was no reason to prevent the meeting if he insisted. In fact, Hu had not yet boarded the boat; that was merely Liu's excuse. “Then I'll ask him to come. May I have your name?” The visitor gave both name and rank. “Luo Shangde, company commander in the Tenth Battalion of the Qiantang naval force.” “Very good. Please wait a moment, Mr Luo. I'll send for him at once.”

Hu was fetched from his house. When he asked the purpose of the visit, Luo opened the sack, revealing a heap of silver: large ingots, round ingots, and broken pieces, all mottled with earth as though freshly dug from the ground. Hu was puzzled. Did the man want it recast into whole new ingots? That would require a visit to a silver-melting shop. While he wondered, Luo drew a bundle of notes from an inner pocket and laid them before him. “The notes amount to eight thousand taels,” he said. “You can weigh the silver presently. There should be a little over three thousand. Proprietor Hu, I want to deposit it here. It doesn't matter whether you pay interest.” “Oh!” Hu was still more surprised. Who would have imagined that a Green Standard officer, paid only a few taels a month, could have savings of more than ten thousand? Such men did not acquire money easily; he ought not to be deprived of a proper return. “Mr Luo, we are honoured by your confidence in our little establishment. We pay the current market rate. But first, may I ask whether this will be a short- or long-term deposit?”

“The term is precisely what I cannot say.” Luo knitted his thick brows and repeatedly stroked his beard with a large hand, as though faced with a formidable problem. “Then let it be a deposit payable on demand.” Hu was always generous in his manner of doing business. “Whenever you need it, bring your passbook and withdraw it.” “I don't need a passbook. I trust you.” The affair grew stranger by the moment. Hu could not refrain from asking, “Mr Luo, how can you deposit more than ten thousand taels without even wanting a passbook?” “Having one or not makes no difference. I know what sort of man you are. Mr Liu Er at the governor's office comes from my home district. I've heard him speak of you. But there is no need to mention my deposit to him.” Two thoughts immediately occurred to Hu. First, Luo trusted him, a complete stranger, more deeply than a fellow townsman whom he had known for years. Second, considering the officer's position, manner, and extraordinary conduct, this might be less a piece of business than a source of trouble.

Hu did not fear trouble. He felt only that Luo's trust was evidence of Fukang's good credit, and became interested both in the man and in his affairs. It was growing late, and a customer deserved hospitality. In a friendly, informal tone, he said, “Mr Luo, you look like a man who enjoys a bowl of wine. Shall we drink while we talk?” The proposal suited Luo perfectly. A Sichuan man, he agreed heartily. “Certainly! I won't pretend to refuse. I'll accept your hospitality. Sorghum liquor for me. The food doesn't matter, but give me plenty of chilli—the hotter the better.” “Then you've come to the right place!” Hu laughed. “I have two bottles of chilli oil so hot they make your throat smoke. I simply can't manage them. Today I've found a connoisseur.” He called an apprentice and sent him to Huangfan'er for dishes. There was already plenty to drink, both rice wine and spirits, standing in whole jars in the dining room. As for the chilli, he added, “A-Mao! Go to my house and ask Mrs Hu to find the two bottles of Pingwang chilli oil someone gave us and send them back with you.”

After the apprentice left, Hu followed proper procedure. He called Liu and had two clerks weigh the silver on a balance and check it in Luo's presence. With the notes, it came to a little over eleven thousand taels. Hu suggested depositing the round sum and letting Luo take back the remainder. He agreed. By the time the money and notes had been put in order, the dishes had arrived. The table was opened out, and the three men sat down for a drink. Soon A-Mao returned with the two bottles of chilli oil. A speciality of Pingwang, near Wujiang, it was made by incorporating red chillies into rapeseed oil and was extraordinarily hot. Neither Hu nor Liu dared attempt it. Luo, however, was entirely in his element and repeatedly cried, “Wonderful!” At last he began to speak of himself. “Proprietor Hu, you must never have encountered a customer like me. To tell the truth, even I feel that my way of doing things is bound to arouse suspicion.” “Not suspicion,” Hu corrected him. “It makes one feel there must be a particular reason.” “Exactly. There is a particular reason.”

By his own account, Luo came from Ba County in Sichuan. His family had been comfortably placed, but from childhood he had neglected every proper occupation. Brothels, gambling, drinking—he loved them all. A thorough wastrel, he had driven both parents to their graves through distress and anger. A marriage had been arranged for him while he was young. His prospective wife's family belonged to the local gentry. Seeing how badly he had turned out, they never mentioned a wedding date, though they made no formal demand to break the engagement. Marriage scarcely concerned Luo. His real worry was finding money to gamble, and he repeatedly borrowed from the family. Finally, his prospective father-in-law sent the matchmaker with a message: Luo had taken fifteen thousand taels from them in all. That debt could be cancelled. If he returned the girl's betrothal document, they would give him another thousand, provided he went elsewhere to earn a living. Otherwise, he would become a beggar in his home district and disgrace his dead parents. The humiliation cut Luo to the bone. In the matchmaker's presence, he tore the betrothal document to pieces and swore that, even if he must labour like an ox or a horse, he would repay the fifteen thousand taels. “A prodigal's reform is beyond the price of gold!” Hu raised his cup to him. “Mr Luo, the only real failure is never to make the effort.”

“Indeed!” Luo drank deeply. “The next day I left Chongqing, found a boat going downriver, and left Sichuan. On board, I thought: I've made my grand declaration, but where am I to find fifteen thousand taels? At Hankou, someone suggested joining the army. Victories brought rewards. If I could win promotion and become an officer, there would be vacant soldiers' places whose pay I could draw. I thought that if I must sell my life, I had better get a decent price. The army seemed best. Just then His Excellency Lin was recruiting...” He meant Lin Zexu. In the fifth month of the twentieth year of Daoguang, British forces had concentrated at Macao, preparing to attack Guangzhou. As governor-general of Guangdong and Guangxi, Lin greatly strengthened the defences. He fortified the Bogue, placing more than two hundred guns along its shores, with sixty warships, and recruited five thousand new soldiers. By this circuitous route Luo entered the naval service. He did not, however, fight in Guangdong. Lin's preparations were so formidable that the British dared not attack directly. With twenty-six warships they changed course for Dinghai, then advanced inland along separate routes. Zhejiang's governor and provincial commander were helpless. The court appointed Yilibu, governor-general of Liangjiang, as imperial commissioner to inspect military affairs in Zhejiang, and ordered Fujian's provincial commander Yu to bring reinforcements. Some of the newly recruited naval troops at Guangzhou were also transferred to Zhejiang. “That is how I came to Hangzhou,” Luo said. “My luck hasn't been bad. In thirteen years I've worked my way up to a sixth-rank post and saved more than ten thousand taels. I'll be honest with you, Proprietor Hu: some of that silver was hard earned, and some came easily.”

The hard-earned part had been saved copper by copper through strict economy. The easy part came from drawing the pay of unfilled places and sharing in criminals' proceeds. Otherwise, he could never have accumulated eleven thousand taels. The officers of the Green Standard were dispirited men: those without money sought it, and those who had it spent it. Luo alone behaved differently. Whenever he obtained silver, he buried it or exchanged it for notes to conceal on his person. He neither visited brothels nor gambled nor lent to others. Some urged him to join enterprises such as smuggling salt, where large profits could be made, but he remained unmoved. His colleagues consequently regarded him as an oddity. “Let them say what they like. I have my own plan. I reckon another three years should bring me up to fifteen thousand. Then I'll go home to Sichuan.” “That will be a proud day!” Hu was moved and thought he might find some opportunity to help Luo earn a little. But then he remembered the man's firm refusal to engage in business. Why offer goodwill only to have it rejected? He held his tongue.

“But now, Proprietor Hu, I'm afraid it may not work out.” “Why?” “Orders have arrived. Our battalion is to go to Jiangsu to fight the Longhairs.” Luo looked depressed. “I don't wish to sound faint-hearted, but the Green Standard troops can't even defeat bandits. How are they to fight the Longhairs? This expedition looks very bad. I have to make arrangements.” “Oh?” Hu asked attentively. “What arrangements?” “For these eleven thousand taels, of course. I have no family here. Mr Liu Er at the governor's office is a decent enough man, but I cannot entrust it to him. He follows His Excellency Huang. Suppose Huang is transferred to a distant province—to become governor of Guizhou, say, or governor-general of Sichuan—or receives a post in the capital. Liu Er would naturally go with him. How would I find him then, thousands of li away?” “That is true. Fukang is established in Hangzhou and will stay here. You can come to withdraw your money whenever you wish.” “Exactly!” Luo took a long, satisfied drink. “Now you understand me.”

“I do, I do.” Hu calculated briefly. “Mr Luo, since you have honoured Fukang with your confidence and treated me as a friend, I'll be equally straightforward. We shall make this a three-year fixed deposit. When you collect it, principal and interest together will amount to fifteen thousand taels. Will that suit you?” “How... how could it not?” Surprise and delight mingled in Luo's face, along with embarrassment at receiving so much. “But that is too much interest.” “That doesn't matter. In business one gains here and loses there; the accounts must be considered as a whole. Your deposit is different. Friendship enters into it. Go and fight with an easy mind. Three years from now, you can take fifteen thousand taels back to Chongqing and repay your debt. You will have other income during those three years; save it for travelling expenses. If it is inconvenient to keep it with you, send it here. I'll credit your account and pay interest on it too.” Hearing this, Luo felt as a soldier might after returning from patrol on a snowy night, washing his feet, and settling into bed: every limb was comforted. He imagined the moment, three years hence, when he would visit his former prospective in-laws with the silver. That would truly be a triumphant season of his life. The thought filled him with contentment.

“Proprietor Hu, no wonder Mr Liu Er cannot praise you enough. There is something very satisfying about your friendship.” “That is my principle!” Hu laughed. “As the saying goes, at home you rely on your parents; abroad, on your friends. I rely on friends even at home, so I must think of their interests. Well, that is settled. Qingsheng, prepare a passbook.” “No, no!” Luo waved his hands. “Your word is enough. There is no need for a passbook. If I carry it and lose it, it will only cause trouble.” “That won't do. Business must follow proper rules. A passbook must be opened. If carrying it is inconvenient, leave it with a friend.” “Then I'll leave it with you.” “Very well.” Hu pointed to Liu. “Leave it with him. This young friend of mine is a man of honour and good faith. His word can be relied on. Set your mind at rest.” “Excellent! Then I entrust everything to you.” Luo rose, made a respectful bow, and took his leave.

Once the guest had gone, Liu could contain himself no longer. Excitement shone in his face and voice as he hurried to express his feelings. “Mr Hu, if business continues like this, we shall have a firm foundation within six months.” “One step at a time.” Hu remained entirely composed. “I expect Luo will tell his colleagues about this when he returns tonight. More deposits from our military gentlemen may follow. Whatever the amount, they are customers. Tell the clerks to treat them all alike, without favouring one over another. Courtesy is especially important. These military gentlemen can be easier to deal with than anyone, but when they become difficult, no one is more troublesome.” “Yes, yes. I understand.”

Hu went aboard that very night. Because of the heat, he had specially arranged with A’zhu's mother to set out after dark and moor below the Beixin customs station by Gongchen Bridge. At daybreak they would clear the barrier and travel as far as possible in the cool morning, then find a shaded place to stop at noon and continue after nightfall. Passengers and boatmen would all be more comfortable. A’zhu and her mother willingly agreed, and even A-Si and the additional hired hand were pleased.

With the rhythmic creak of the sculling oar around them, Hu and A’zhu sat quietly facing each other in the lamplight. She had taken special care over her appearance, wearing loose-legged trousers of pale cotton and a fitted black silk jacket. The contrast of black and white suited her well. Like most boatmen's daughters, she had unbound feet and often went barefoot; tonight she wore embroidered slippers. Hu noticed the embroidery. “Don't look!” she said, drawing her feet back. “I'm looking at your slippers. Let me see them.” Reassured, she showed them to him. “What material are the uppers?” he asked, examining them. “Will you make me a pair too?”

“No!” she answered with a smile. Then she rose, went inside, and returned with a large, thick book. Pressed between its pages were embroidery patterns and silk threads of many colours. Hu chose a design of five bats surrounding the character for longevity and specified soft white satin. “White satin shows the dirt. Blue would be better.” “Never mind. They won't get dirty.” “Another story!” A’zhu said. “How could they stay clean if you drag them over the ground every day?” “Do you think I mean to wear them? I couldn't bear to. I'll put them away, and when I miss you, take them out to look at.” She blushed, pleased despite her embarrassment. With an awkward little smile, she said, “You have such a thick skin!”

Her manner moved Hu, and he wished to embrace her. But the cabin windows stood open on both sides for ventilation, and A-Si might see them from the stern. He restrained himself. A’zhu said nothing either. Treating his slippers as a serious undertaking, she immediately searched the book for threads and matched the colours, concentrating so completely that she forgot he was beside her. “Why work on that now?” Hu asked. “Let's talk.” “Go on. I'm listening.” “Enough, enough.” He closed the book. “I have an interesting story for you.”

He told her Luo Shangde's story, embellishing it and bringing every scene to life. A’zhu listened to every word. “But what about the young lady?” she asked. “Did she too despise poverty and admire wealth? Or did she refuse to marry him because she resented his being such a wastrel?” Hu paused. “I didn't ask him.” “Why not?” The question seemed unreasonable, and he was amused. “Had I known you would be so concerned about the young lady, I certainly would have asked.” “You ought to have asked anyway. He never mentioned her, and you never asked—as though she weren't a person at all.” She pursed her lips. “Nine men out of ten have no conscience.” “That still leaves one who has.” Hu smiled. “I'm not among the nine.” “I'm not so sure.” “Not so sure I'm bad, you mean?” “Shameless!” She drew a finger down her cheek to mock him.

The exchange revived all A’zhu's private anxieties. Her mother had concealed the arrangements to ask Fat Zhang to act as matchmaker, and she was too shy to inquire. At their little gathering aboard Chunhao, she had vaguely perceived that her mother wanted Zhang to approach Hu about the marriage. But what had happened? Alone by moonlight or lamplight, she had turned the question over countless times without discovering the answer. Here was an opportunity, yet she hesitated. First, she could not find the words with which to probe. Second, she feared that her inquiry might reveal there was nothing in it at all. That disappointment would be unbearable. In the present confusion, at least she still had something to hope for. Absorbed in her thoughts, she lowered her brows and blinked repeatedly. Hu could not quite guess what occupied her. He only felt that, after a man's hard day's work, sitting quietly opposite someone in the lamplight was a rare and peaceful pleasure. He too remained silent. Neither knew how much time passed before A’zhu seemed to wake from a dream and looked around, bewildered, as though unsure where she was.

Hu's secretive smile made her uneasy. Had he seen through some private thought? “What are you grinning at, you wicked creature?” “Why!” he laughed. “How have I offended you? I haven't said a word.” “That's just what I resent—you never say anything!” There was depth in the remark. Hu considered it. “What do you want me to say?” “How should I know? The mouth is yours. If you have something to say, say it yourself.” “I have a great deal to say. I don't know which part you would like to hear.” She savoured this answer, and her spirits gradually eased. A great deal to say implied a long time in which to say it. There was no need to hurry; they could take their time.

“Let's discuss business,” Hu said. “What message did your father send?” “He has found two buildings and two men. Nothing can be settled until you inspect them.” “I don't know whether a building is good or bad—or rather, I know about the building itself, but not the location. It ought to be near the land and water transport terminals. As for the men, if both are good, we'll employ both.” “One is surnamed Wang and the other Huang. Both are very capable. Unfortunately, we can only employ one.” “Why?” “They don't get on. Haven't you heard that one mountain cannot hold two tigers?” “Of course I have. Only someone who doesn't know how to employ people fears a struggle between two tigers. In my hands, not merely two but any number would have to behave.”

Given his abilities, A’zhu thought this might be no empty boast. Still, she deliberately teased him. “Boasting costs nothing.” “If you don't believe me, wait and see.” He smiled. “It's two tigresses I fear. I couldn't make them behave.” A’zhu thought the meaning obvious: one tigress was Mrs Hu and the other herself. She longed to protest that she was no tigress, and then to ask whether his wife was fierce. But she could neither say these things nor properly pretend not to understand. Lately she had learned much about human dealings: there were open and indirect ways of speaking, times when one must remain silent, and times when one must answer. This was a moment when an answer was necessary. It should be half serious, half playful, suggesting understanding without admitting too much. After a moment, she smiled. “You are so formidable—and so wicked—that you need a tigress to keep you in order.”

“You keep calling me wicked. What exactly is wicked about me?” “You!” She pointed at his nose. “All your work goes on inside your head.” “Do you mean I'm an ‘underworld scholar’?” That was the Hangzhou expression for a sly, treacherous man, whose distinguishing traits were few words and no outward sign of pleasure or anger. Hu was neither silent nor expressionless. He was cheerful, open, and always smiling. Even A’zhu felt the accusation would be grossly unfair. She shook her head. “No, not that.” “Then am I a fool?” “Certainly not. Ah! Now I have it.” She spoke with conviction. “That is your worst fault. You always talk people into a corner. Whatever they say is wrong, and they don't know how to answer.” Hu was taken aback. No one had ever told him this before. On reflection, he did have a habit of pressing people too hard with his verbal dexterity. It was not a good way to deal with others. He ought to correct it.

“Am I right?” A’zhu asked. “Everyone is right sometimes.” Then he asked earnestly, “A’zhu, do you think I'm willing to admit a fault and mend it? Tell me honestly.” She nodded. “I won't deny your good qualities.” “Then tell me my faults freely. I shall listen.” He reached for her hand, and she let him hold it, blushing. It was late. The banks were deserted and still; only the creak of the oar and the swish of water broke the silence. A’zhu could hear her own heartbeat. At last she could not resist asking, “Where will you stay in Huzhou?” “I think I'll stay at Prefect Wang's yamen.” “Yes,” she said quietly. “That would be proper.”

“I've been thinking,” Hu said, his mind returning to business. “We need a large building: the shop in front, living quarters behind, and plenty of guest rooms. Ideally, there should be a small garden, with the guest rooms opening onto it.” “Must it be so elaborate?” “The more comfortable, the better. Consider it. The quality of the silk is much the same everywhere, and prices are fixed by agreement among the trade, with little variation. A merchant can buy silk anywhere. Why should he insist on buying from you? Something else must attract him. We must make him think, the moment he boards his boat, ‘When I get to Huzhou, I'll stay with the Zhangs. Their house is comfortable. You sleep well, eat well, and are treated like one of the family.’ Make a guest feel at home. Once you have done that, how can the business escape?” The principle was simple, but because A’zhu's own fortunes were involved, his words seemed particularly warm and persuasive. Her mind filled with images of guests made to feel at home. She had seen such scenes many times aboard her family's boat and thought nothing remarkable of them. Now, however, they inspired a longing she could not express.

“I can't promise anything else, but silk merchants who stay with us will at least eat better than elsewhere,” she said modestly. “That goes without saying. Your mother's cooking will leave their jaws hanging...” “Listen to you!” she interrupted with a laugh. “Whatever you say, you add so much sauce and seasoning that you could talk the dead back to life.” Her words sounded reproachful, but she was delighted. Hu knew when to stop and offered no further compliments. “A’zhu, there is no end to the ways one can make people comfortable. Once the silk firm opens, I can help your father with outside affairs, but everything within the house will depend on you and your mother. The inside is even more important than the outside.”

“I understand. But it won't be like the boat. How shall I know what the silk merchants want?” “That is where the difference lies. On a boat, the guest directs things, and whatever he says goes. A merchant from elsewhere who stays at your premises expects you to arrange matters. He won't say much.” “He may say nothing, but he'll know whether he's been treated well.” “Exactly.” Hu nodded emphatically. Encouraged, A’zhu grasped the point. “I'll simply treat him as a visiting relative,” she said with confidence. “Attend to whatever needs attending to. If he doesn't want our attention, he must have his own reasons, and we'll leave him alone.” “Precisely!” Hu tapped the table lightly. “You've found the secret. Some people don't understand. Either they fail to consider their guests, or they carry consideration too far and interfere with every little thing. The guest feels constrained and is afraid to return.”

A’zhu was ordinarily broad-minded, but in talking to Hu she somehow became full of private interpretations. Once again she brought the subject back to herself. “Now I understand. My mother must have been too friendly. You were afraid of being watched and restricted, so you stayed away. Isn't that it?” “You!” Hu pointed at her, but would say no more. Plainly he had a criticism in mind and was withholding it. She pressed him, and still he refused. Half serious and half playful, by turns angry and coaxing, she insisted on an answer. There was no real reason not to give one; Hu simply wished to tease her. He let slip a fragment here and there to provoke further questions. Unaware of his design, she came closer, demanding an explanation, until he caught her in an embrace and kissed her cheek.

Only then did A’zhu realise that she had been tricked, and this time she was genuinely upset. Turning her back to both Hu and the lamp, she lowered her head and remained silent for a long while. At first he thought the behaviour deliberate and chose not to respond. Gradually he sensed that something was wrong. He went over and tried to turn her towards him, but she twisted sharply away. This was clearly no playful sulk. Alarmed, he stepped in front of her, looked closely, and became still more disturbed. “What... what is it?” he stammered. His frightened expression softened her. It also comforted her to see how much she mattered to him. She smiled through her tears, though some awkwardness remained. Now cautious, Hu dared not tease her again. With the same concern, he asked, “What is wrong? You gave me a terrible fright. If something has upset you, or if I've said the wrong thing, tell me.” “Nothing.” She composed her face, wiped her tears, and recovered herself. The little disturbance made Hu quiet, but he continued watching her expression, afraid that a missed response might upset her again.

“It's getting late.” A’zhu's mother called from outside the cabin without entering. Afraid that she might notice something, A’zhu quickly signalled Hu not to mention the tears. He nodded to her and went to meet her mother. “Auntie! Come and sit down.” There was no reason to refuse. Once seated, she asked, “Have you ever been to Huzhou, His Honour Hu?” The formal title jarred on his ear. Without thinking, he answered, “Auntie, call me Xueyan.” The words struck A’zhu deeply. To her, they settled the nature of their relationship. As though she had swallowed a remedy for anxiety, she felt wholly reassured, and watched her mother smilingly to see how she would answer. Her mother was still modest. “I couldn't presume.” Yet she too was beaming. “I had better still...” Still do what? She herself did not know what attitude to take. A’zhu, acutely alert, would not let the opportunity slip. “Call him Xueyan!” she blurted out. Realising that she had been too direct, she added, “Obedience is better than ceremony.”

It was ingenious of her to produce the saying. Though not entirely apt, it provided a forceful reason. Her mother said, “That is true. Then I shall take the liberty.” But though she spoke of taking the liberty, she still did not actually address him by name. A’zhu decided that, having begun, she must finish and fasten the matter securely. “Mother, say it once, then.” This sounded almost like mockery. Slightly embarrassed, her mother gave her a reproachful look. “Who asked you to get so excited?” Hu saw all the subtle expressions passing between mother and daughter and was amused. His own remark had been rather careless, but regret would serve no purpose. Since matters had reached this point, he might as well please A’zhu. With a broad smile, he said, “Auntie, we're all like one family. You should have called me by my name long ago. Isn't that right, A’zhu?” Now it was her turn to be embarrassed. “I don't know!” she said, blushing. “Don't ask me about matters between you and my mother.” To rescue her daughter, her mother finally said, “Xueyan, you're right. We are like one family. We shall depend on your care in future.”

“Naturally.” Wanting to see A’zhu blush, Hu turned to her again. “A’zhu, aren't we one family?” “I don't know!” She was delighted and embarrassed, but also a little annoyed that he should mischievously put her in a position from which she could not escape gracefully. In a fit of pique she decided to leave him, but he anticipated her. As she turned, he called, “A’zhu, don't go. I have something to discuss.” “I'm sleepy. Say it tomorrow.” Yet her feet stayed where they were. “I'll tell you a joke. I guarantee it will wake you up.” “It's still early for bed,” her mother added. “Sit a little longer.” A’zhu sat down again. Hu did not look as though he intended to tell a joke. Indeed, after patting her hand to signal patience, he turned to the woman he addressed as “Auntie.” “A’zhu and I were just discussing why, when there are so many silk firms, merchants should insist on dealing with yours. There are many things to consider.” “Indeed!” Her mother's eyes brightened with interest. “I should like to hear them.” “Let A’zhu explain.” A’zhu's interest revived too. She gave a detailed account, and Hu added points of his own. Her mother listened with relish and naturally had several ideas that had not occurred even to him.

“Xueyan, I must say, you really are capable!” She paused, looked at her daughter, and finally spoke with resolution. “A’zhu is fortunate. She will certainly have happiness to enjoy.” The declaration was so direct that A’zhu blushed to the roots of her ears. Hu made matters worse by looking steadily at her with an almost-smile and reaching for her hand. She could no longer remain seated. “Who wants to enjoy his good fortune?” She sprang up and turned away, swinging her long braid so sharply that it almost struck his face.

“Do you want to share my good fortune or not?” Hu asked, touching her cheek and speaking in a voice only A’zhu could hear. Leaning against him, she could hear her own heartbeat and felt herself blushing. Fortunately, the dim lamplight concealed her embarrassment, allowing her to answer with composure. “Of course I do. If I don't, who will?” “Very well. There will always be good fortune for you to share.” She looked up. “Then let me ask you: what did my mother say to you?” “About what? What did she say?” “Do you need to ask?” “Naturally I do,” he replied with great assurance. “There are so many matters under discussion. How can I know which you mean?”

“You are always pretending to be an innocent lamb,” A’zhu said resentfully. “I could bite you.” “I play the lamb, and you boast.” Hu laughed. “If you dare, I'll admit defeat.” “You think I don't dare?” She took his teasing challenge seriously, until he cried out and she stopped. “Do you admit defeat?” she asked. He responded with another teasing challenge. His subsequent behaviour crossed a boundary; she objected and resisted, and he stopped. Reassured that her objection had been respected, she laughed. “I knew you were up to no good. I was right.”

Hu asked about the secure fastening of her clothes and how she undid it. “I have my own method, of course.” “Show me.” She almost demonstrated, then realised his purpose. “You are full of tricks. I won't fall for that.” Unable to deceive her, he let the matter drop with a smile. “Then tell me this: who taught you?” “A girl who performed riding and acrobatic tricks. She was from Shandong and very pretty. Once her troupe travelled on our boat. She shared my sleeping quarters, and one evening, while we were chatting, she told me that there are all kinds of bad people on the road and you must depend on your own caution. Her trousers were made this way. I copied them and made two pairs for myself.” “Did you learn any boxing from her?” “No. She offered to teach me, but I thought the boat was too small. It wasn't a place to learn boxing.” “What did she mean to teach you?” “Something called grappling. If a man tried to harm me, I could teach him a lesson.” “Just as well, just as well!” Hu patted his chest. “Fortunately you didn't learn it, or I should have to be on my guard every moment I was with you.”

“Do you think I'm so fierce?” A’zhu asked, half in earnest. “What do you think yourself?” She fell silent, uneasy. She had long suspected that Hu regarded her as difficult, and feared that this would damage their future relationship. She wanted to reassure him, but did not know how. Hu, meanwhile, decided to maintain restraint: partly to test his own self-command, and partly to frustrate her expectations for once—not to humble her, but to show that he too could observe propriety. After further teasing, he asked her to pour him a bowl of tea. “It's cold.” “Cold is just what I want.” She rose, turned up the lamp, put her clothes in order, and poured some thoroughly cooled Longjing tea. Taking a deep drink herself, she felt refreshed and calmer. Still embarrassed, though also reassured, she hesitated to turn towards him.

“What is taking so long?” Hu asked. After a moment's thought, she poured his tea, lowered the Meifu oil lamp until its flame was no larger than a bean, and brought him the bowl. As he accepted it, they exchanged more teasing remarks about their earlier embarrassment. A’zhu answered with renewed confidence, and Hu admitted that he too had reason to exercise restraint. “So there are things even you don't dare do,” she said mockingly. “I thought you feared neither heaven nor earth and were capable of any wickedness.”

“Now you have something to taunt me with!” Hu laughed again. When the laughter subsided, he said, “Since we are doing nothing wrong, why keep the lamp so dim? Turn it up, and let's have a proper talk.” She feared that a brighter light would reveal her embarrassment. “If one's conduct is upright, what is there to fear?” “And one's behaviour at bedtime must be upright too.” “Naturally,” she said proudly. “Until the appointed day, don't imagine otherwise.” “The day?” Hu deliberately pretended not to understand. “What day?” He acted so convincingly that she became confused. Was he truly unaware, or merely playing the innocent again? “If you don't know, never mind!” she said, somewhat angry. “I've never met anyone so difficult. One moment you're serious, the next you're pretending. You never let anyone see your true feelings.”

The accusation was serious enough that Hu could no longer answer with a grin. Yet he would not accept it either. “You are being foolish,” he retorted. “Can't you see my true feelings already? Understand this: I spend time with you because I want to be happy. Must it be like waiting on a great official or discussing business—solemn throughout, without half a joke? Never mind me; I think you would find that very dull yourself.” The rebuke actually soothed her. Biting her lip, she considered his words one by one and felt increasingly at ease. She also discovered a method of her own: if Hu liked to play the innocent, she would repay him in his own coin. “Very well. Now I understand your temper.” She smiled again. “I'm not afraid of your deceiving me. You know my temper too. When I'm easy to deal with, I'm easy; when I'm not, you'll discover what I can do. You had better be careful.” Hu smiled without replying. Dealing with women, he thought, was like dealing with customers: he would rather face someone formidable but reasonable than someone who seemed meek and ineffectual, yet could sometimes be impossible to reason with.
