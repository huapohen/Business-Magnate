CHAPTER NINE

As soon as Hu Xueyan arrived in Huzhou, Wang Youling brought him to stay at the prefectural yamen. Their separation had been brief, but their friendship was so deep that both felt a joy beyond words at meeting again. Each had much to tell the other, yet there was no time for a private conversation. Courtesy, as well as his own immediate interests, required Hu first to pay his respects to the two senior secretaries. Private advisers customarily had a little domain of their own: Qin Shoumen and Yang Yongzhi each occupied a courtyard where they both lived and worked. Wang accompanied Hu to Qin’s quarters first. After an affectionate exchange of news, Qin thanked Hu most earnestly. Before the Dragon Boat Festival, Hu had sent Qin’s household a remarkably generous present: two shi of white rice, a carrying load of fresh seasonal fruit and vegetables, and ten strings of copper cash. Mrs Qin had written from Hangzhou to tell her husband, and his manner towards Hu was warmer than ever. “I have given up wine on account of my rheumatism,” he said. “Today I shall make an exception and have a proper drink with you, Brother Xueyan.”

“Excellent!” Wang put in. “Master Qin, where do you think we should give Xueyan his welcoming dinner?” Ordinarily, Wang himself would naturally entertain his guest on the first evening. The question therefore suggested that he had something else in mind. Qin considered it, then smiled. “Do not tell me, sir. Let us each write down a guess and see whether we are thinking along the same lines.” He took a brush and a sheet of paper, turned away, and wrote something down in secret. Wang did likewise. When they unfolded their slips, one read “Then put into practice,” and the other “Wood and easy.” Both men burst out laughing. Hu knew that the written characters for “wood” and “easy” combined to make the surname Yang; he also knew the saying in which “put to use”—Yongzhi—was followed by “then put into practice.” Both slips pointed to Yang Yongzhi. What he could not understand was why drinking at Yang’s quarters should be so amusing. “Let me explain,” Wang said. “Master Yang has something to be very pleased about. He had scarcely been in Huzhou a few days before he took a concubine. The lady looks as though she will bear him sons, and she is capable and good-natured besides. We shall descend upon him this evening.”

For all this talk of descending upon Yang, Wang instructed Li Cheng, the attendant who served his private office, to arrange a shark’s-fin banquet and have it delivered to Yang’s quarters with a jar of good wine. Hu had a further purpose of his own. Just now he needed Yang’s help and would not let slip any opportunity to cultivate the man and do him a kindness. Finding a moment, he drew Wang aside. “Surely Master Yang ought to have a present to mark his taking a concubine?” “I have already given him one,” Wang replied. “You need not bother.” “The courtesy cannot be omitted,” Hu said, “and the gift must be substantial.” Wang thought for a moment, understood his intention, and nodded. “Very well. What do you propose to give?” “Something of real value. A pair of gold bracelets, I thought. I had better go and buy them at once.” “There is no need for all that trouble. I have a pair ready to hand; you may use those. Only”—Wang lowered his voice and pointed towards the inner room—“he must not hear of it!” He meant Qin Shoumen. Hu answered with a look of understanding. They then excused themselves on the pretext of changing their clothes and arranged to meet Qin at Yang’s quarters at six precisely.

At five, however, Li Cheng was already showing Hu into Yang’s rooms. Good fortune lifts a man’s spirits, and Yang’s ruddy face and air of complete satisfaction were enough to make anyone envy him. “Ah, my dear fellow!” He seized Hu’s hand with exceptional warmth. “I cannot claim to think of you through every hour of the day and night, but whenever I have a quiet moment you do come to mind—that is perfectly true. Well, has your bank opened to a roaring trade?” “Very well, thanks to your good wishes.” Hu made a point of studying him closely. “You are looking splendid, Master Yang. I take it that you and your employer suit each other admirably?” “Need you ask? With Mr Xuexuan, I am truly a fish in water.” “Quite so—a fish in water!” Hu laughed. “And I hear you have another sort of fish-and-water happiness as well?” Yang roared with laughter and called into the inner room, “Jinyun! Jinyun, come out here!”

Jinyun was, of course, his new concubine. The curtain lifted, and a young woman of about twenty appeared, her face as round as one of the plump Da A’fu clay figures of Wuxi. She gave the visitor a shy smile. “Jinyun, this is Mr Hu, whom I am always telling you about. Come and greet him.” “Oh—Mr Hu!” Her eyes widened as she looked him up and down; then she gathered her skirts and curtsied. “You do me too much honour.” Hu returned her greeting with a bow and, in the same movement, took a red-paper packet from his sleeve and handed it to Yang. “A trifling present, to add to the lady’s bridal ornaments.” “No, no! There is no such obligation.” Yang protested vigorously. “Unless you consider it too paltry, you must accept it. Besides, it is for the lady, not for you.” After that Yang could hardly refuse. Feeling the packet, he realized that it contained a pair of bracelets, though he could not tell whether they were gold or silver. He asked Jinyun to thank their guest again. “The present is so insignificant,” Hu added, “that you had better not open it just now, or mention it. I should hate to be laughed at.” Yang understood what that meant. Holding the packet, he looked both embarrassed and grateful. “You are far too generous. I scarcely deserve such kindness. But it would be discourteous to resist you further.” He bowed deeply and slipped the packet into his pocket.

They had barely concluded this exchange of bows, protests, and persuasion when Wang and Qin arrived together. Naturally there was more teasing, with Jinyun as its subject, before they sat down to dinner. Hu announced at once that he did not count as a guest and insisted that Wang take the place of honour. Wang, in turn, urged the two secretaries to sit above him. After a lengthy exchange of courtesies, Wang finally took the first seat, Hu the next, and Yang the host’s place. Jinyun was invited to join them. They were all sufficiently close to dispense with ceremony. Jinyun had a delightful disposition, speaking always with a sweet smile and attending to everyone gently and thoughtfully. Qin drank himself thoroughly tipsy. Wang saw his opportunity. He had already mentioned to Yang the proposal that Fukang should act as agent for the prefectural treasury; now was the moment to leave the two men to discuss it in detail. He rose to take his leave. “Stay and have a talk,” he said. “I am rather sleepy and shall go on ahead.”

“I fear Brother Xueyan must be tired too.” Unexpectedly, Yang seemed to have no intention of keeping Hu. Fortunately he followed this with an invitation. “May I ask you to breakfast tomorrow morning? Huzhou is justly proud of its delicacies. Come and try my concubine’s cooking.” “Gladly! I shall certainly come.” “And you too, sir, if you feel inclined,” Yang added to Wang. “Thank you.” Wang naturally had no wish to join them, and in any case had business to attend to. “There has been a killing in the eastern countryside. I must go out early tomorrow to examine the body, so I cannot come.” Hu kept his appointment the next morning. Jinyun’s cooking was indeed excellent. One dish consisted of parcels wrapped in thin bean-curd skins, cooked with vermicelli and served with chilli oil from Pingwang. Even on the Zhang family’s boat Hu had never tasted it. He ate two bowls and praised it without ceasing. When breakfast was over and tea had been brought, they began their discussion.

“My employer has given his instructions. Fukang is to handle the funds of both the Huzhou prefectural treasury and the Wucheng county treasury. That is settled.” Yang continued, “Have you an affiliated bank in Huzhou, or do you intend to open a branch?” “We shall certainly open a branch. For the present, Hengli will collect the funds on our behalf.” “Hengli’s credit is quite good.” Yang rose. “Come into my study.” Though called a study, the room had nothing bookish about it. A five-drawer desk stood at the window, covered in blue cloth. Apart from brushes and an inkstone, it held only an abacus and account books. Beside it stood a very solid wooden cupboard. Yang opened this, took out a document box, and from the box produced a sheet of paper, which he handed to Hu. “I have everything ready for you. Fill in Hengli’s name and obtain a guarantor’s endorsement—merely as a formality—and Hengli can come to collect the money.”

Hu examined the paper. It was a formal petition to undertake the handling of public treasury funds. He had never dealt with such an arrangement before and could not help asking, “Once this has been submitted, is that all that is required?” “Yes. The yamen gives you its written approval, and the matter is settled.” Hu knew that official business invariably entailed expenses. “In that case, Master Yang,” he said earnestly, “please tell me how much I should pay, and to whom, so that I may make the necessary arrangements.” “To speak frankly, another man might spend a thousand taels without finding things so easy. In your case, there is nothing to discuss. Still, for your own sake, if you want things to run smoothly afterwards, you must give the clerks a little something. I shall introduce you to one of them. Invite him out for tea, say a few civil words, and give him a packet containing this amount.” He held up one finger. It could hardly mean a thousand taels; did it mean ten, then, or a hundred? Hu thought it wiser to make certain. “Very well. I shall make it a hundred and twenty taels.” This was an indirect way of asking: if Yang meant ten, he would naturally correct him. “No, no! A hundred is enough. That covers everything. There is no need to throw away any more money elsewhere.”

Yang sent for a clerk from the revenue department, a man in his fifties whose clothes were decidedly impressive. Such men were formally known as clerical functionaries. In yamens large and small, they alone understood the everyday workings of administration, and that knowledge was their capital. The true state of affairs, the crucial points, and the tricks of the business were secrets never divulged to outsiders. Although no written rule made a clerkship hereditary, in practice it passed from father to son.

Prefectural and county yamens had three divisions of runners and six administrative departments. All six departments employed clerks, of whom those in the criminal department put on the most imposing airs and those in the revenue department were the wealthiest. The latter owed their prosperity to the system for collecting land and poll taxes. The Board of Revenue concerned itself with the total assessed, not with the particulars. Who owned how much farmland, where each plot lay, and how it was graded—only the local revenue clerk knew all this exactly. His authority rested on a secret register handed down through his family, known as a fish-scale register. Without it, a man of the greatest ability could not collect the taxes. Possession of that book not only made official business possible but also enabled the clerk to grow rich. For years the collection of taxes had been a hopeless muddle. Those who paid did not necessarily receive receipts, while those who paid nothing might possess proof that they had paid. As long as the required proportion of the assessment reached the authorities, no one above concerned himself with whose payment had been credited to whom; indeed, there was no practical way of untangling it.

A revenue secretary therefore had to deal with the revenue clerks. A formidable clerk might keep the secretary under his thumb; an equally formidable secretary might bring his clerks completely to heel. Generally, however, they lived on good terms, as intimate as members of a family. That was the relationship between Yang and this clerk, whose name was Yu Si. “Old Four,” Yang said familiarly, “this is Mr Hu, a close friend both of Prefect Wang and of mine.” A clerk’s standing was low. Seeing what influential connections this Mr Hu possessed, Yu prepared to kneel in formal homage. Hu was quicker: the moment Yu bent his knees, he stepped forward and supported him. “Brother Yu! A pleasure to meet you.” “Mr Hu, I could never presume to accept that form of address. Please call me Yu Si.” Yang too felt that Hu need not be so modest. “You may call him Old Four as well.” Then he turned to the clerk. “Old Four, take Mr Hu out for a cup of tea. There is a small matter in which he would like your help.” “Certainly, certainly! I shall be delighted to offer Mr Hu some tea.”

Yu took him out into the street. In front of the county yamen stood a very large teahouse called Bilangchun, its frontage three bays wide. The public tables occupied the front; behind them, amid luxuriant trees and flowers, a separate courtyard offered more secluded seating. Instead of taking Hu there, Yu sat at the head of a long table placed lengthways in the centre of the front hall, immediately beneath the eaves. Hu understood at once. This was the “horse-head table,” where only a local leader of the Grain Transport Brotherhood was entitled to sit facing outwards. Hu was not a member, but he knew the rule. He also understood Yu’s purpose: to show everyone that he was entertaining a distinguished guest. Hu appreciated the compliment without acknowledging it in words. Speaking might expose him as an outsider; silence would preserve the proper appearance. To explain what he understood would only make matters awkward. Instead, he showed by his manner that he considered it an honour to have such a friend as Yu.

Yu’s influence was considerable. No sooner had he taken his seat than people began coming over, smiling and offering their greetings. Some called him Fourth Brother, others Fourth Uncle. Only a very few called him Old Four; these must either have entered the brotherhood alongside him or be close friends of equal standing. Whatever their form of address to Yu, all treated Hu with great respect. Yu introduced his visitor to each of them and, in a few cases, made a point of telling Hu who the newcomer was. Hu understood that these were people of consequence, indispensable acquaintances if he was to establish himself in Huzhou. Visitors came and went without a break; many sent over refreshments, until the table was covered. There was plainly no possibility of discussing business. Yu, too, felt that he had done enough to make his friend known. Beckoning to a waiter, he asked, “Is there somewhere at the back? Somewhere quiet.” “I shall see and report back to you, sir.” Before long the waiter returned to say that a table was available. When they followed him inside, another attendant was removing the tea things from a table in a secluded corner. Evidently the waiter had talked its occupants into giving it up. It was a small incident, but Hu found it deeply revealing. Yu’s ability to get things done could hardly have been demonstrated more clearly.

“Mr Hu, please tell me what you require.” “Brother Yu”—Hu reverted to his original form of address—“we are friends; all this formality makes us seem strangers. Call me Xueyan.” “That would hardly be proper. But let us leave such niceties for now. What is it you wish me to do?” Hu explained his errand. “Have you a guarantor?” Yu asked. “I shall ask Hengli to find one.” “There is no need. Give me the petition and leave everything else to me. Tomorrow I shall bring the written approval to your quarters.” Even Hu was surprised by such prompt assurance. He clasped his hands in thanks. “I am deeply obliged. Master Yang told me to prepare a red packet as a small token of appreciation. Now I dare not produce it. To do so would show that I had no idea how to behave.”

Yu nodded gravely and immediately regarded Hu in a new light. His earlier respect had been for an honoured guest of Master Yang and Prefect Wang. Now he saw that Hu was himself a man who knew how to conduct himself in the wider world. As he poured the tea, Yu arranged the pot and cups in a particular pattern. This was a way of asking whether Hu was “inside the threshold”—a member of the brotherhood. An uncomprehending response would identify an outsider; a member would answer with a corresponding gesture. The exchange was known as a “tea-bowl formation.” Hu knew how to make one, but since he was an outsider, he had no reason to do so. Yet Yu had already made his signal, and to pretend not to understand might, if discovered later, look like deliberate deceit. Hu recalled the brotherhood’s rule that a claim of membership could be accepted, but a connection must not be denied. In his case, denial would not mean concealing actual membership. He knew the brotherhood’s customs and, though technically outside it, was effectively linked to its members as a brother. That relationship needed to be made clear.

After a moment’s thought, he asked, “Brother Yu, there is someone I should like to ask you about. I imagine you must know him.” “Who is that?” “Brother You, the fifth, from Songjiang.” “So you are a friend of You Laowu?” Yu looked surprised. “How do you address each other?” “Brother You and I were much as you and I are: friends from our first meeting.” This made clear that Hu was an outsider. He then answered Yu’s specific question. “Brother You is too polite and calls me ‘Uncle,’ though I scarcely deserve it. I knew Old Master Wei first, you see. Out of respect for the old gentleman, You treats me as his friend and places himself a generation below me. In fact, we are like brothers.” Yu understood perfectly. An outsider who fitted so naturally into the brotherhood’s world was a rare find indeed.

“In that case, we are all among our own people. Still, you are an honoured guest of Prefect Wang. I fear I am claiming a friendship above my station.” “Nothing of the kind,” Hu replied. “Each friendship stands on its own. To tell you the truth, I do not generally get on very easily with officials.” Men of the brotherhood could be remarkably open-hearted. Seeing Hu’s attitude, Yu wanted to know him better. He adopted a new form of address. “Mr Hu, the banker—have you come to Huzhou solely on this official business?” He pointed to the petition. “That is one reason.” Hu considered, then decided to tell him the truth. “I also want to help a friend open a silk firm, and I should like to buy some silk myself.” Yu understood immediately. To receive the public funds of Huzhou Prefecture and Wucheng County and employ them locally was a shrewd calculation. “But do you know the silk market?” he asked. “I was hoping for your advice.” “Prices have fallen sharply. It is certainly a good time to buy, but you must beware of being unable to sell.” “Ah,” said Hu. “A different trade is like a mountain barrier. I do not yet understand the reasoning behind those two remarks.”

“That is easily explained.” Huzhou raw silk had one great customer: the Three Southern Bureaus, the imperial weaving establishments at Jiangning, Suzhou, and Hangzhou. All three were of roughly equal size, each operating seven or eight hundred looms and buying a substantial quantity of Huzhou silk every year. Once the war against Hong and Yang began, treasury funds ran short and transport became difficult, reducing production at all three establishments. With the fall of Jiangning, a third of the looms were lost. Suzhou lay close to the fighting, and its bureau was operating only intermittently. Even if Hangzhou remained unaffected, official purchases, taken together, would amount to no more than half their former volume. Moreover, people from Jiangning to Suzhou, as well as from Yangzhou and other places north of the river, were fleeing the war. When they could scarcely fill their stomachs, how were they to buy silks and satins? Raw silk consequently remained unsold and prices plunged. Stock bought now would be difficult to dispose of; once fresh silk began to yellow, it would be harder still to sell.

“Indeed!” Hu laughed. “I know how to turn somersaults through the hole in a copper coin, but of the silk market I know absolutely nothing. I am fortunate to have your advice. Otherwise I might have rushed in and found myself like a man grasping dry flour with wet hands—in a mess I could never get clear of.” “I am no great expert either. But I have a few friends in the trade whom I could bring along to talk to you.” Yu paused, then added, “They would not dare take advantage of your inexperience.” “That is worth more than gold.” Hu clasped his hands in gratitude. “Please arrange a meeting for me.” “Between friends, there is no need for ceremony. I know you are busy, so tell me when you are free. I shall give you a welcoming dinner and invite them at the same time.” “Tomorrow evening, perhaps. And I should like you to ask a couple of men who understand the export trade.”

Something stirred in Yu’s mind. “Mr Hu, how quickly you think!” he said with genuine admiration. “I am impressed.” “Do not praise me yet. We still do not know whether the export trade is active. If it is, surely falling silk prices present an opportunity? Brother Yu, let us undertake this together.” “Gladly!” Yu answered with pleasure. “I shall profit by your good fortune.” “On the contrary, I shall depend on your help.” “We need not exchange compliments among ourselves.” Yu was growing excited. “If we are going to do it, let us give ourselves room and make a substantial venture of it.”

Another man might have taken Yu’s words for empty politeness or deliberate bluff. Hu thought otherwise. Men of the brotherhood prided themselves on making their word as solid as a stone doorstep: a sentence spoken through their teeth could be more reliable than a signed contract witnessed by others. Yu had already made his power and standing quite clear. As for money, even if he were not personally wealthy, he must at least be able to mobilize funds. Why should they not undertake a large business together? Their venture would have two advantages that others lacked: Hu’s own judgment and Yu’s connections. Properly combined, the two could carry all before them.

Hu, too, was excited. He outlined his plan to help Old Zhang open a silk firm, though he said nothing of A’zhu, who was central to the whole affair. Yu already knew Zhang and had travelled on his boat. “Old Zhang, is it? He is an honest fellow. He has a very pretty daughter.” Now that the subject had arisen, Hu could hardly fail to respond without seeming less than frank. Yet an open declaration would be awkward; he could only hint. Smiling, he said, “A’zhu is half the reason for opening this firm.” “Ah!” As the saying went, a worldly fellow had a finely perceptive heart. Yu understood immediately. “You have an excellent eye!” “There is still a small difficulty in that matter, and I may have to ask for your help later. But let us leave that for now. What do you think about the silk firm? Should we run it jointly, or establish another concern?” “Neither is necessary. Since Old Zhang is one of your people, we need not let the benefit go to outsiders. When we begin our export business together, we can have his firm buy the silk for us.” Zhang’s firm did not yet even have a name, and already it had secured a substantial piece of business. Hu, however, knew how to return a kindness. “In that case, I shall ask Zhang to set aside a further share of the profits for us to divide.” “That is a small matter,” Yu replied. “Proceed as you intended, Mr Hu. If you run into anything you cannot manage, come to me. Tomorrow evening, once we have talked to the men I invite, we can discuss our partnership.”

In a single conversation with a man he had never met before, Hu had found an ideal partner. Not far from Bilangchun stood Hengli Bank. Its manager, Zhao Changsheng, had already received Fat Zhang’s letter and knew the standing of his visitor; he welcomed Hu as an honoured guest. Here Hu was on familiar ground. He no longer had to seek instruction at every turn, as he had when discussing silk. A few questions about the bank’s business told him all he needed to know. Hengli was conducted on sound, conventional lines, but it was small and insufficiently developed. Judged by Hu’s own principle of pressing vigorously ahead in business, it might not be able to keep pace with him. Nothing mattered more than flexibility in deploying funds. Hengli’s approach was cautious and practical. It was unlikely to advance whatever sum he required merely on his word or on the strength of a note. If it failed to respond when needed, the consequences could be serious. Was Hengli, then, the right affiliated bank to handle transfers for Fukang’s agency business with the Huzhou and Wucheng treasuries? The question required fresh consideration.

With this thought in mind, Hu avoided the principal subject, but Zhao raised it himself. “Mr Hu, Xinhe has written to say that you are introducing us to the prefectural and county treasuries to receive and disburse funds on their behalf. We are most grateful. May I ask what arrangements are involved?” Hu thought quickly. He had already devised a compromise that would cost him nothing, benefit Hengli, and allow him to do proper justice to his friendship with Fat Zhang. “The position is this,” he answered calmly. “After discussing the matter, Prefect Wang and Master Yang have entrusted the two local treasuries to Fukang. As we have not yet opened a branch in Huzhou, I thought I would leave the local payments to your bank. I do so partly because of my friendship with Zhang at Xinhe and partly out of regard for a fellow banker. There are no conditions attached.” That meant there would be no terms to negotiate: it was a favour from which Hengli would benefit outright. Delighted and grateful, Zhao kept clasping his hands. “Thank you, thank you!”

“Brother Changsheng, give me a figure up to which you are prepared to allow an overdraft. Once I have spoken to the people in the yamen, the matter will be settled. Another day I shall give a dinner and invite Master Yang and the revenue clerk, Old Four Yu, to meet you.” Secretaries in prefectural and county yamens regarded social engagements outside as something to be avoided, lest conspicuous entertaining invite criticism and damage their employers’ official reputations. Zhao also knew very well how influential Yu Si was in Huzhou. Hearing Hu speak as though both men would come at a word, and refer to Yu with the familiarity of an intimate friend, he felt his respect deepen. His manner changed almost unconsciously. “Naturally, Hengli must give the dinner. Mr Hu”—he placed both hands on his knees and leaned forward, speaking with great clarity—“may I first ask what volume of receipts and payments the two treasuries handle?”

“I do not yet know precisely. Ordinarily the local transactions would not be very large. Still, Huzhou is prosperous, and we are dealing with both a prefectural and a county yamen. I should expect a regular turnover of thirty to fifty thousand taels.” Zhao considered. “In that case,” he said, sounding apologetic, “our capital is limited. I propose to set aside a facility of twenty thousand taels, with a separate facility of ten thousand for your bank. Please let me have a specimen of your seal.” “Certainly.” Hu had not intended to seek a separate ten-thousand-tael overdraft facility for Fukang from Hengli, but rejecting a kindly offer would hurt Zhao’s feelings. He therefore continued without a change of tone, “As for dealings between Fukang and your bank, we can make a separate agreement, on whatever terms you think appropriate.” “Yes, yes. I shall follow your wishes.” “Then that is settled.” Hu rose. “I must take my leave.” Zhao pressed him warmly to stay for lunch, but Hu’s plans for Hengli had changed and he was anxious to put the new arrangements in order. He firmly declined, saying that they would have plenty of opportunities to meet in future and need not do everything at once. Having agreed to return the following morning, he left the bank.

He went straight back to Bilangchun, where he was already treated like a regular patron. Several people came forward to greet him. “I have urgent business with Brother Yu,” he said directly. “Where might I find him?” “We know where he is; we know.” A man surnamed Qian called to a young fellow. “Little Monk! Take Mr Hu to Crystal A’qi’s.” Hu thanked him and followed Little Monk westwards out of the teahouse. He wondered who this Crystal A’qi could be; he had better find out beforehand. At his question, Little Monk gave a mischievous smile. “A first-rate piece of goods,” he said. “Fourth Uncle Yu’s old favourite. He has lunch and takes his midday nap there every day.” So she was a local prostitute. A woman who had caught Brother Yu’s eye must be a celebrated beauty. “Why is she called Crystal A’qi?” Hu asked. “Crystal means crystal.” Little Monk laughed. “Surely, Mr Hu, you know that particular feature a woman may have?” Once the allusion was explained, Hu found his own question amusing. He asked no more and followed the young man through a series of turns into a long lane. Near its end, Little Monk stopped. “You had better knock yourself, Mr Hu. I am not going in.”

“Why not?” Little Monk blushed slightly. “Fourth Uncle Yu will not allow me to see Crystal A’qi.” “I see!” Hu clasped his hands. “Thank you for your trouble.” Only when the young man was well away did he knock. A young maid answered the door and, on learning his business, showed him in at once. As soon as he reached the upper floor, he smelled the fragrance of opium. He lifted the curtain to find Yu lying at ease, drawing in smoke and sending it drifting out again. On the other side of the large redwood bed reclined an exceptionally alluring young woman in the full bloom of her twenties. She could only be Crystal A’qi. With the pipe at his lips, Yu merely looked at Hu and beckoned. A’qi, holding the bowl for him, could not rise either; she sent Hu a seductive smile instead. He felt a sudden stir within him. No wonder Yu barred Little Monk from the house! Those eyes could enchant even an old monk of proven spiritual discipline, let alone a “Little Monk.”

Having smoked the pipeful in one draught, Yu seized a small teapot, took a mouthful, and hastily sat up. “How did you find your way here? Come, come—lie down a while.” Before he finished speaking, A’qi was already rising with a graceful smile to yield her place. Hu saw no reason for excessive ceremony. Smiling and nodding in return, he gathered up his robe and climbed onto the opium couch. “A’qi, this is Mr Hu, a distinguished guest.” “Brother Yu,” Hu corrected him, “you should say a good friend.” “Quite right, quite right. A distinguished guest and a good friend.” A’qi offered her greetings and fetched a small stool, sitting beside the bed to prepare another pipe for Yu. “How did you find me?” Yu asked again. “I went to Bilangchun first, and a young fellow brought me here.” Hu deliberately omitted Little Monk’s name. “I suppose you have not eaten? Stay and make do with a meal here. A’qi, go and see about adding a few decent dishes.”

When she had gone to attend to lunch, Hu and Yu spoke quietly across the opium lamp. Hu came directly to his purpose: he wished to withdraw the petition and rewrite it. “What change do you want to make?” “Hengli is rather small. I propose to divide the business. Hengli can handle local receipts and payments, while another bank deals with funds remitted to the provincial capital.” “Which bank have you in mind?” “That is just what I wanted to discuss with you. Is there a bank you know well?” “There is.” Yu went on preparing his opium, apparently absorbed in thought. After a considerable pause, he asked, “You want me to choose one for you?” “Yes.” “If someone else had asked, I could name a reliable bank at once. But since it is you, we must speak differently. Banking is your own trade. You have no need to consult me unless there is something more behind it. We are friends; tell me frankly, and let us see whether I have understood your needs.”

From this, Hu could tell that Yu had already worked out a plan for him. They ought, of course, to discuss it candidly. Yet Wang and Yang were involved, so he must choose his words with care. “Nothing escapes you, Brother Yu. I have a special friendship with Prefect Wang and get on very well with Master Yang. But in official matters, everything must leave you all able to account for yourselves. I must never let a friend suffer for helping me. That is an unalterable principle of my dealings in the world. Would you not agree?” Naturally Yu agreed. Hu’s words served two purposes: to keep Wang and Yang clear of responsibility, and to assure this new friend, already so close, that he would never behave like a bungling fool. Yu understood and nodded repeatedly without speaking. “The advantage to a bank in acting for a public treasury,” Hu continued, “is simply that it can make use of the public funds. But these are unlike a wealthy client’s deposit, which may remain untouched and can safely be lent out at interest. Public money can be employed only for short periods. If no immediate use can be found for it, one ends up working for nothing.” “Yes, yes,” Yu said. “That is much how I see it. Go on.”

“My idea was to buy silk here. If the market were strong, a quick resale would leave a margin to be picked up. That no longer seems possible. Besides, now that we are joining forces, I must change my approach. How? That is what I want your advice on.” “To be frank, I have a bank myself. I am one of three partners, with my brother acting in my place. If I had wanted it to handle the local prefectural and county treasuries, I could have arranged that long ago. I held back only because people would talk. For the same reason, my bank cannot now become your affiliate: I must have no hand in transferring public funds. What I am wondering is this—why not open a Huzhou branch of Fukang?”

That was precisely what Hu wanted, but he had not yet established himself locally and had thought it too early to consider. He hesitated, unsure how to answer. “Are you worried about being unfamiliar with the place and its people?” Yu turned to look at him as he asked. The movement showed how seriously he meant the question. Opening a branch bank was not a matter of announcing that one was open for business, as though setting up a vegetable stall. Since Yu knew the trade, he must have considered the difficulties. Hu decided to hear him out. “The place is unfamiliar; the people are another matter. With you here, Brother Yu, what have I to fear? Now that I know we are fellow bankers, let me ask how you think this branch should be established.” “Your branch will be different from others. It will serve only two purposes: first, handling public treasury business; second, making it convenient to buy silk. Its premises must therefore look sufficiently impressive, but it will not need many staff. If you have your own people, so much the better. Otherwise I shall find them for you. That is the first point.”

“And the second?” “Capital, naturally. Your branch will need access to substantial sums—ten or twenty thousand taels whenever they are required. Yet you will not be making long-term loans, and you cannot keep all that money sitting idle. My suggestion is that you need not transfer any funds from Hangzhou. Beyond what you receive from the prefectural and county treasuries, whatever you require can be supplied from my bank.” This was better than Hu could have hoped. Delighted, he said, “With such help from you, what more could I ask? I shall pay the usual rate for interbank borrowing.” “No, you must not pay that rate,” Yu replied. “It would leave you no advantage. We shall work out another arrangement.” His proposal was flexible: the return would be shared proportionately. If Fukang lent to a customer at one per cent, Yu’s bank would receive half a per cent; if the rate were eight-tenths of one per cent, it would receive four-tenths. In short, the two banks would divide the interest equally. Fukang would earn half the return simply by passing the funds through its hands.

Such an opportunity was rare indeed. On reflection, however, Fukang would not be earning money for nothing. It would have to use its connections and skill to put Yu’s funds to work. Otherwise, however much he possessed, his large sums would breed no smaller ones; money left sitting idle was still dead money. Even so, Hu was unquestionably getting the better of the arrangement. He thanked Yu repeatedly, but also made a promise. “Friends need not exchange empty courtesies, and a man who knows the world will not tolerate dust in his eyes. I shall speak plainly. I have complete confidence in my ability as a banker. I dare say no one else can conduct the business more flexibly. Since you are giving me this opportunity, I shall make certain you have reason to be proud of me.” “Those two remarks are worth something,” Yu answered slowly. “Let me be equally frank. My old comrades and the younger fellows under them can manage almost anything—except business. Among them I have no friend of your exceptional ability. I do know a few capable men outside the brotherhood, but they still fall short of you. Nor have I ever felt the same immediate affinity with them.” This was an expression of complete confidence. Conventional modesty would have been out of place. Hu answered simply, “I understand.”

“Of course you do. I have a pretty sharp eye for a man myself.” Their friendship had reached a point at which further ceremony was unnecessary. A’qi now brought people in to serve lunch. The redwood table by the window was covered with dishes. Host and guest sat facing each other, with A’qi at the side; Hu jokingly addressed her as Fourth Sister-in-Law. “What wine will Mr Hu have?” she asked, pointing to Yu. “He has so little warmth in him that even in the sixth month he drinks spirits infused with tiger bone and quince.” “Not today.” His craving satisfied, Yu sprang from the opium couch and stretched and kicked his legs as though about to give a display of martial arts. Then he cracked the joints of both hands, shrugged his shoulders, and twisted at the waist, plainly feeling wonderfully comfortable. “Well?” A’qi prompted. “What wine?” “Bring the medicinal wine in the foreign bottle.” “Which one?” She hesitated. “The very best?” “Naturally the very best!” Yu gave her a fierce look. Only then did she realize how highly he valued this friend. She hurried to smile apologetically. “Mr Hu, it is not that I am mean. I did not realize—” “Enough, enough!” Yu interrupted. “The more you explain, the worse you make it. Bring the wine.”

It was indeed precious. According to Yu, it had been made from the finest Fen spirits and authentic medicinal ingredients, following a secret prescription from the imperial palace. The prescription alone had cost a hundred taels, paid to an imperial physician; a single batch of the medicine cost more than two hundred. The wine had been steeping for three years, and Yu had still been unwilling to drink it. “It is not a question of money,” he explained. “Some of the ingredients cannot be bought at any price.” “He said he would not open it until his fiftieth birthday,” A’qi said with a smile. “Thanks to you, Mr Hu, I shall have a taste of this treasure today. I wonder what is so wonderful about it.” “What is wonderful? You will find out tonight!” Yu and Hu exchanged a laugh. A’qi might be accustomed to such suggestive banter, but she could hardly appear entirely unmoved. She rolled her eyes at Yu and scolded him. “No ivory ever grows in a dog’s mouth!” After a little more joking, they settled to sample the rare medicinal wine. Hu naturally pronounced it excellent, whereupon Yu offered to copy out the prescription for him. Once these courtesies were over, they needed to return to business. There was so much to discuss that Hu scarcely knew where to begin; he held his cup and considered.

Yu mistook his silence for reserve. Pointing to A’qi, he said, “She may have no other virtues, but she has two. First, she keeps her mouth shut: whatever she hears, she never repeats a word outside. Second, her heart is perfectly open and sincere—you can see straight through it. That is why she is called ‘Crystal.’” He gave her a sidelong smile. There was something unmistakably intimate in it, and A’qi seemed genuinely annoyed. “You wretch!” she muttered. “What nonsense about crystal! Talking such rubbish in front of a guest!” Yu had a weakness for being scolded by her; the more she reproached him, the more he laughed. She, for her part, was satisfied once she had said her piece. Turning to Hu with a pleasant expression, she said, “Do not laugh at me, Mr Hu. The old fellow cannot get through a day without provoking me into scolding him.” “That is what makes it enjoyable,” Hu replied with a smile. “If I were Brother Yu, I too would need a scolding from you every day to feel quite comfortable.” She laughed sweetly, her eyes bright and fluid as quicksilver, full of warmth and invitation. Hu felt another stir of attraction, but caught himself at once. Such conduct was among the gravest offences in the brotherhood’s world. He quickly composed his thoughts and looked away.

“Let us begin with the bank,” Yu said, meeting his eyes. “Mine is called Jucheng. It too is in front of the county yamen, not far from Hengli.” “Brother Yu, what would you think of my putting up a sign for the Fukang branch at Jucheng?” “It could be done, but it would not be a good arrangement. First, outsiders would take the two concerns for one. Second, sooner or later you must have premises of your own. When you move, customers accustomed to coming to us will find it inconvenient.” Hu knew both points perfectly well. The difficulty was his shortage of help: one man could not attend to so many things. He had intended to borrow a place to establish himself, put up Fukang’s sign, and see how the silk business developed before deciding what to do next. Since Yu disapproved, he had to abandon the idea. “I know you must be short of people,” Yu continued. “I can help you there, though only by supplying men to run errands. You will still have to find a manager yourself.”

“Not necessarily.” Hu described how he had come to employ Liu Qingsheng. “I like young men with quick minds. It does not matter if they know little about banking; I can teach them.” “I cannot think of such a person offhand.” Yu turned to A’qi. “Can you think of anyone?” “There is one, but you would not like the suggestion. I had better keep quiet.” “There is no harm in mentioning him.” “Young and quick-witted? There is Little Monk.” Before Yu could respond, something stirred in Hu’s mind, and his eyes lifted involuntarily. Yu was far too shrewd to miss it. “What?” he asked. “Do you know him?” “He was the one who brought me here just now,” Hu answered without losing his composure. “Oh!” A’qi asked in surprise. “Why did he not come in?”

Her question revealed that she knew nothing of Yu’s prohibition. If Hu answered truthfully and exposed the little secret, the two might quarrel. Even if Yu could keep A’qi in order, forbidding a young man to visit was hardly a creditable act. Hu decided to conceal it for him. “I did invite him in,” he said. “But a friend was waiting for him at Bilangchun. He had made time specially to show me the way and had to hurry back once he had brought me here.” The fabrication was so watertight that even Yu believed it. His expression visibly relaxed. “Little Monk certainly has a good head on him,” he said. “Only—” “Only what?” A’qi broke in. “Nothing could be better than recommending him to Mr Hu. He is forever calling you ‘Fourth Uncle’ so affectionately. When an opportunity comes, should you not give a younger man a helping hand?”

Pressed in front of a guest, and faced with such an unimpeachable argument, Yu could hardly refuse outright. He turned to Hu. “See the fellow first. If you like him, employ him; otherwise I shall find you someone else.” Hu already thought rather well of Little Monk. He was not material for a bank manager, but he would be excellent at dealing with people outside. Still, that hidden and awkward source of jealousy required careful consideration. Hu nodded. “Very well. Let me talk to him first.” “There is someone on your side I should like to talk to as well,” Yu said suddenly. “What sort of man is Old Zhang?” “Honest and steady. His business ability is probably limited. When we join forces, you had better put in someone of your own to take overall charge.” “No, no!” Yu shook his head vigorously. “There is no need to upset an arrangement already made. Zhang should remain in charge; I shall find the men to work under him. I wanted to talk to him only to see what sort of fellow he was, so that I could choose someone whose temperament suited his. Now that you have told me, I understand. We can leave that until after tomorrow evening. For now, let us deal with your bank. I shall hold back the petition; it can be processed at any time, so there is no hurry. Your first step is to find staff and premises. Presently I shall take you to the bathhouse for a soak. It is an excellent place to find whoever you need.”

They put aside their cups and finished the meal, then went to a bathhouse called Yiyuan. Yu visited every day and had his own fixed place; the whole row was reserved for him to entertain friends. As at Bilangchun, people came up to greet him as soon as he sat down. This time he introduced everyone to Hu, whatever their standing. Only after a considerable round of greetings were they left in peace. “Where will Little Monk be just now?” he asked casually of those nearby. “Does anyone know?” “Where else? At the Wang family’s gambling house, of course,” someone answered. Hu understood that Yu intended to send for the young man. “A moment, Brother Yu,” he intervened. “What is it?” Hu considered. “I wonder whether he would be willing to come to Hangzhou with me.” “Eh?” Yu was puzzled. “Why do you want to take him to Hangzhou?” “I need someone there to go out and attend to things for me.” “He has never set foot outside Huzhou Prefecture. In the provincial capital he would be completely at sea. What use would he be?” Hu had not expected opposition, and Yu’s objection was so reasonable that he was momentarily at a loss.

That moment’s hesitation betrayed him. Yu’s mind worked quickly too. Reviewing everything Hu had said since A’qi first mentioned Little Monk, he concluded that something had been left unsaid or was not entirely true. Had he no wish to cultivate this friendship, he could have ignored it. But they had taken to each other at once and intended to become close; he could not simply let the matter pass. “Little Monk is a very slippery fellow,” he said in a warning tone. “You must not believe everything he tells you.” A man of experience needed only a hint. Hu knew that Yu had detected some unreported remark by Little Monk. Worldly men were apt to be suspicious; unless he cleared up this misunderstanding, the consequences could be serious. Yet an explanation would be difficult to frame. What if this were another Wulong Courtyard affair, a tale of betrayal like that in which Song Jiang’s woman, Yan Poxi, took a lover? Exposing it could cost Yu his dignity. On reflection, Hu thought it unlikely. A’qi was open-hearted, unlike Yan Poxi; Yu would never be as forbearing as Song Jiang; and Little Monk hardly seemed bold enough. Had there been an affair, he would never have admitted so readily that Yu barred him from A’qi’s door. Still, there was no doubt that A’qi looked favourably on the young man. It would not be presumptuous to mention the matter now and put Yu on his guard.

“Brother Yu, let me tell you frankly. I rather like Little Monk. But he told me you would not let him visit A’qi’s house, so I cannot employ him in Huzhou. You and I have a long friendship ahead of us. I cannot put someone you dislike under your nose. If I take him to Hangzhou, that difficulty disappears.” Only now did Yu appreciate the depth of Hu’s consideration. Above all, he had refrained from revealing the prohibition in front of A’qi. Here was a man who thought of every detail where a friend was concerned. Seen in this light, his proposal to take Little Monk to Hangzhou was probably also for Yu’s benefit, to spare A’qi the restless thoughts that the sight of this slick young rogue might stir. Moved by such solicitude, Yu felt all the more obliged to dissuade him. “It is true that I dislike Little Monk. But dislike him or not, I still have to look after him. He is too slippery—an expert at eating, amusement, whoring, and gambling. If you take him with you, he will cause you trouble.”

“None of those habits matters,” Hu said. “There is only one thing I want to ask you: has Little Monk ever betrayed his own people for an outsider?” “He would not dare! If he did, even if he escaped the punishment of three knife-thrusts making six holes, Huzhou would certainly have no place for him.” “Then I shall take him after all. I only hope he is willing. People are always more comfortable where they know their surroundings.” “That is no obstacle.” Yu shook his head. “If you really want him, he must agree whether he likes it or not. Besides, what more could he ask than to serve a man of your standing, one of his uncles’ generation? Everything I said just now was for your sake.” “I know, I know. I am not afraid of his tricks. Even if I could not keep him in order myself, with you behind me, would he dare disobey?” The compliment was perfectly judged, and Yu felt thoroughly pleased. Better still, this would lure the tiger away from its mountain and remove a persistent irritation from his heart—all on A’qi’s own recommendation. What could be more satisfactory?

“There is no hurry about this either,” Yu said at ease. “It only needs to be settled before you return to the provincial capital. When we have a quiet moment, I shall send for Little Monk and let you talk to him properly. If you really like him, say nothing to him yourself. Tell me what you intend—what work you would give him in Hangzhou—and I shall speak to him.” “Excellent, excellent!” Hu’s wish to employ Little Monk had been half for Yu’s sake in the first place; he was happy to leave the arrangements to him. “I entrust the matter to you, then.” Their chief purpose in lingering at Yiyuan had been to discuss Little Monk away from A’qi. Now that it was settled, there was no need to remain. Hu dressed and took his leave, eager to meet Old Zhang.
