CHAPTER TEN

Half a month later, Chen Shilong returned to Huzhou in the same boat. He had not brought Wanxiang, but he had given her the hundred-tael banknote. She, too, had heard that Wang had been appointed prefect of Huzhou. She was willing to come for a visit, only somewhat later. Chen was anxious to return and report, and could not wait. Since the money to help her settle her affairs would have to be given anyway, he had decided to make a generous gesture and give it to her in advance. “Well done! We needn't concern ourselves with that now. What did You Laowu say?” “He said he wouldn't write a reply. If you want to ship silk to Shanghai, it would be best to do so before the end of the seventh month.” “Before the end of the seventh month?” Hu repeated earnestly. “Yes. He was very clear about that. He also said that once the goods passed Jiaxing, they would be in his territory, and he could guarantee that nothing would go wrong.” “Hmm. I see.” Hu reflected, drawing a great deal from those two simple answers.

“Mr Hu,” Chen continued, “I also found out quite a lot about the Small Swords Society.” “Oh!” Hu was rather surprised. “How did you find out?” He had warned Chen not to put any further questions to You Laowu. He very much feared that the young man had been running his mouth and making inquiries among people who had no business knowing. Such careless talk was a fault he must be firmly warned to correct. Sensing Hu's displeasure, Chen hastened to explain. “I listened carefully while other customers were talking in a teahouse.” What he had heard was this: some years earlier, men wearing red headscarves had risen near Shanghai. The authorities called it the Redhead Rebellion. One of their leaders, Liu Lichuan, was originally from Guangdong. He did business in Shanghai, cultivated official connections, and had considerable dealings with foreign merchants.

The next day, Hu had quietly explained the matter to Chen. “This is how things stand. We want to send our silk to Shanghai for sale to foreign firms. What worries me is trouble from the Small Swords Society. With bad luck, our goods could be caught right in the middle of it. You Laowu may know what is going on inside the society. All I want is his advice on how to avoid trouble and get through safely. Do you understand?” “I do.” “Then think how you should put it to him.” After a moment's consideration, Chen answered, “I thought I would say: ‘Uncle You, Mr Hu and my Uncle Yu have sent me to ask after you and pay their respects to the old master. Mr Hu has several boatloads of silk he would like to send up, but he is afraid the route may be unsettled. He has specially sent me to ask whether there is any danger. Whether he ships the silk or not will depend entirely on your word.’” Hu considered, then nodded. “Good! Say exactly that.” “But you'll have to give me a letter of introduction, Mr Hu. Otherwise, how will he know who I am?” “Naturally. And you'll take some modest gifts as well.” Modest in name only, the gifts cost a considerable sum because there were so many of them. The famous Zhu Laoda sesame sweets alone filled two great baskets. There were foods, clothing, and useful articles: scarcely a Huzhou speciality had been omitted. They filled a boat, which sailed straight for Songjiang.

“The things on this list are for You Laowu himself; those on this one are for the old master; those on this one are for our friends at Tongyu. And here is another list. Ask You Laowu to send someone to take you there.” Chen accepted it and smiled at the words “Wanxiang, Meijia Lane.” “Don't laugh!” Hu said. “She's not my sweetheart. And you needn't ask whose she is. When you see her, ask just one thing: would she like to visit Huzhou? If she doesn't wish to, leave it at that. If she does, bring her back in your boat. Here are a hundred taels. Tell her they're from me.” “Very well. I understand.” Recently, with Hong Xiuquan having established his capital at Jinling, Liu Lichuan had made contact with him and was preparing a major undertaking. Rumours abounded in Shanghai. Some said Liu had already allied himself with Zhou Lichun of Qingpu; others that Jiading, Taicang, and other places were unsettled; still others that the foreign merchants in the settlement would all support him. Though only rumours, this information was extremely useful to Hu. A new concern now troubled him: might You Laowu also be in touch with Liu Lichuan? The question mattered enormously. He must discuss it with Yu Si.

After repeating Chen's report, Hu offered his interpretation. “You Laowu has given us a deadline. He guarantees our safety before the end of the seventh month. Does that mean something will happen in the eighth?” “Of course. Once the eighth month comes, he won't guarantee it.” “Then he knows what Liu Lichuan and the Small Swords Society intend. Doesn't that mean he means to rebel too?” For the first time before Yu, Hu looked worried. “Rebellion is no joking matter!” Yu thought for a long while. “No. From what we know of Liu, he probably belongs to the Hongmen. The Grain Transport Brotherhood and the Hongmen keep out of each other's affairs. Besides, You Laowu has his old master living comfortably in Songjiang and members of the brotherhood scattered everywhere under him. Even if he wanted to act, there are too many restraints for him to risk anything rash. But in the brotherhoods one gives notice in advance. Liu may intend to cause trouble in the eighth month, and You Laowu knows it. As for joining him, in my opinion, certainly not.” This was an experienced and penetrating analysis. Hu's doubts vanished. “Then we mustn't miss our opportunity!” he said excitedly. “Think, Brother Yu: if the Small Swords Society rises, Shanghai's transport routes may be cut, but the foreign settlement certainly won't be affected. Silk from elsewhere won't get through, yet the foreign firms will still have to trade. Won't the price of silk soar?” “True enough,” Yu answered thoughtfully. “But if nothing happens, our gamble will come to nothing.”

“You can hardly call it nothing. We'll still have the goods.” “If you want to do it, we'll do it,” Yu said readily. “Today is the twentieth of the sixth month. We have forty days—plenty of time!” “Brother Yu!” Hu exclaimed. “I've just realised something else.” Since You Laowu was a good friend, he would naturally consider Hu's interests. If he had joined Liu Lichuan's scheme, the rising's outcome would be uncertain and his own safety beyond his control. He would hardly undertake someone else's business. He might simply have said that he could not guarantee the route, indirectly warning Hu not to ship. His willingness to guarantee safety before the end of the seventh month therefore showed that he was outside the affair and confident he would not be drawn into it. Yu entirely agreed. “I'd do the same. If I were involved in a great undertaking of that kind, I honestly couldn't spare a thought for a friend's incidental business.” “And You Laowu understands trade. If the settlement were in danger and foreign firms might stop buying silk, he would certainly have told me. On that reasoning, we can go ahead with confidence.” “Exactly. I'll take responsibility for arranging the funds. You handle the rest.” They discussed the details and decided to buy as much silk as possible and have it in Shanghai by the twentieth of the seventh month. The profits would be divided into three shares: one each for Hu and Yu, and a third to be distributed among those who needed rewarding, with the details to be settled later.

Leaving A'qi's house, Hu returned to the Dajing Silk Firm. During the fortnight Chen had been away on his Shanghai journey, Hu had opened both businesses. He had paid to take over the silk firm's ministry licence, and changing its name would have been inconvenient, so it remained Dajing. He had also acquired the use of a very respectable house through a property pledge. At the front was a broad hall, five bays wide, serving as the shop; behind were two courtyards, one large and one small. The larger contained rooms for visiting silk dealers, the smaller Hu's own quarters. Two rooms were also set aside for Old Zhang and his wife when they needed to stay. On Chen's recommendation, Dajing had hired the man surnamed Huang as the manager in charge of its day-to-day business. His full name was Huang Yi, and he proved quite capable. Old Zhang consequently became a proprietor who could leave the actual running of the business to others. They consulted together when something arose, but Hu generally followed Huang's advice.

“Mr Hu,” Huang said after hearing the announcement that they were to buy heavily, “the lean fifth and sixth months are normally the time to bargain silk prices down. If we're buying, we mustn't let word get out. Once it does, prices will shoot up.” “Then what should we do?” “Send more men into the countryside and buy quietly. It will just take a little longer.” “Time is precisely the trouble. We must be quick. And silk for foreign firms mustn't be doctored with sizing. It must be exactly what it claims to be. That means we must first sort and prepare it ourselves, then pack it and load it. With only a month to get it to Shanghai, we're already pressed for time.”

Huang hesitated. From experience he knew that, if they put up a red notice promising to buy any quantity that met their standard, prices were bound to rise sharply, leaving them badly out of pocket. He offered two alternatives. First, Hu could use his connections at the yamen to have all outstanding taxes pressed for collection. Once the tax collectors appeared at the door, payment could not be avoided. Households with silk would be forced to sell their new silk to pay the government. “No, no!” Hu shook his head vigorously. “That's too ruthless. The people would curse me to death, and I'd never keep my footing in Huzhou. Besides, we must protect His Honour Wang's reputation.” “Then there's the second way,” Huang continued. “The imperial weaving establishment isn't buying silk at present, and our competitors' trade is slack. We can find out who has stock and buy it from them.” “That would do. But make absolutely sure their silk is suitable for the foreign market.” Dajing became extraordinarily busy. Cartloads of silk came in; packet after packet of silver went out. More women skilled in handling Huzhou silk were hired to sort and prepare it. Short of hands, they enlisted both A’zhu and her mother. Every day the two worked until after midnight before going home, and sometimes they stayed at the firm. Hu had three places to visit daily: the county yamen, A'qi's house, and Fukang's branch. He went out early and returned to Dajing only at night, then began issuing instructions. He was too busy even to exchange a word with A’zhu.

As the weather grew hotter and the work increased, A’zhu never complained of hardship. Her one grievance was that she could find no opportunity to be with Hu. Twenty days seemed to pass in a moment, and the seventh day of the seventh month was approaching. Several days beforehand she had resolved that, on this festival when the two heavenly lovers were reunited, she would have a proper conversation with him. On the day itself, she worked with unusual energy and told the cook early that supper was to be served late. Normally there was another spell of work after supper. She had arranged with the silk workers that they would push on, finish their tasks, and then eat, allowing them to go home early. By the time supper was over, darkness had just fully fallen. Everything was put away, and it was time to leave. A’zhu told her mother that home was too hot and she would stay at the firm to enjoy the evening air. Her mother understood the excuse but did not expose it. “After sweating all day, won't you go home for a bath and come back?” she merely asked. But she would sweat again on the walk back. “I'll wash here. Let Aizhen stay with me.” Aizhen was their maid. After washing, A’zhu sat in the cool air, gazing at the distant Milky Way and waiting for Hu. By ten o'clock even Aizhen was nodding. Then someone arrived: Chen Shilong, whom Hu had sent to Hangzhou on business five days before.

“When did you get back?” “Just now,” Chen answered. “I didn't know you were here. I've brought the things.” “What things?” “All sorts of food and household things: dress materials, face powder, torreya nuts, walnut sweets, and vegetables. Some Mr Hu asked me to buy, and some I bought myself.” “What did you buy yourself?” “A sandalwood fan. For you.” “Spending money you shouldn't again!” A’zhu reproached him. “I could use a finely woven palm-leaf fan. Why buy sandalwood?” She was speaking against her own wishes: in fact, she had been longing for just such a fan. Chen felt rebuffed. “Then I've made a mistake.” He stared at her blankly. A’zhu felt guilty, but did not want to explain further. “Have you eaten?” she asked. “I don't feel like a meal. What I'd really like is a bowl of chilled mung-bean soup.” “There's red-date and lily-bulb soup.” She could easily have sent Aizhen for it, but fetched it herself. When he had gulped it down, she asked, “Would you like more?” “If I have any more, there may be none left for Mr Hu.” “It doesn't matter. He won't eat much.” She gave up her own portion to satisfy Chen's appetite. He was halfway through his second bowl when Hu returned, and hurriedly rose to greet him.

Wanting to speak to Chen, Hu had no attention to spare for A’zhu. The moment he sat down he asked about Hangzhou. “Here's Old Liu's reply.” Chen handed him Liu Qingsheng's letter. It concerned Fukang's handling of the public funds of both the Huzhou prefecture and the county. Hu had drawn all the money into his silk purchases, but the provincial treasury was pressing urgently for the sums due to it. Chen's special journey to Hangzhou with a letter to Liu had been intended to resolve this difficulty. Through Liu Er, Liu Qingsheng had approached the clerk responsible for the treasury's accounts, who had agreed to a delay until the end of the month, when everything must be remitted in full. “Old Liu says time passes quickly, and asks you to make preparations early. He's trying to find a way in Hangzhou too, though it's hard to say whether he'll succeed.” “What is he trying?” “He didn't tell me, but I know a little. First, he's consulting other banks. If they have remittances payable in Huzhou, he'd like them transferred to Fukang, which would issue the drafts—” “What!” Hu started. “That's rash of him. Drafts issued in Hangzhou must be honoured here. He has to tell me before doing anything of the kind. Otherwise, won't we end up refusing payment?” “He's still making inquiries. When something definite emerges, he'll naturally write.” Chen paused, then added, “He's also talking to Xinhe. If we can't send the money in time, he wants them to advance it.” “Do you know what Fat Zhang at Xinhe says?” “I hear their own funds are very tight.”

Hu fell silent and calculated. The deadline at the month's end could not possibly be extended again. If the Small Swords Society did rise, the Jiangnan Grand Camp would both lose revenue from the Shanghai area and have to send troops to suppress it. Its demands on Zhejiang for military funds would then become desperately urgent. If the province could not meet them, the treasurer would report to the governor, the governor would memorialise the throne, and responsibility would be investigated. Wang would be gravely implicated. “We must find a way to remit everything before the month's end,” Hu said. “Shilong, write a letter for me.” It was again addressed to Liu Qingsheng. He was to approach the other banks in advance for short-term loans repayable at the end of the eighth month and write immediately with the amount he could obtain. Any shortfall would be raised in Huzhou. As for drafts issued by Fukang in Hangzhou and payable by Fukang in Huzhou, that arrangement should be suspended until all the necessary funds had been secured. “Mr Hu,” Chen said, holding his brush, “may I ask something?” “Go ahead.” “Will the loans due at the end of the eighth month be repaid from the silk sold in Shanghai?” “Exactly. If the silk can't be sold immediately, I have another way: we can borrow against it in Shanghai. Naturally, I'd rather not. Once we do that, people will see that our resources are limited. It will be harder to work another trick after that.” Chen understood a great deal from these words. Anyone could perform tricks, but the skill varied. The real art lay in keeping the mechanism from being exposed.

He thought as he wrote. Once the letter was finished, they discussed the silk business. Shipment was approaching, and Hu wanted Chen to escort the cargo again. Chen readily agreed. They went through the details of transport by water, right up to the goods' arrival at a Shanghai warehouse. Then they considered whether to establish a firm there. One subject led to another, and deep into the night they were still absorbed in discussion. A’zhu had been left entirely out. At first she could wait patiently, but her resentment of Hu's seeming inability to see her grew steadily. Several times she wanted to get up and leave, yet the wicker chair seemed to have a hook that held her clothes fast. She kept thinking that, when she had the chance, she would give him a proper scolding. At one o'clock Hu stretched. “We can talk tomorrow. I'm really tired.” “I'll come first thing in the morning,” Chen said. “The things from Hangzhou are still on the boat.” “No hurry, no hurry. Get some proper rest yourself. Tomorrow afternoon will do.” Only then did Hu notice A’zhu. “Why! Are you still here?” She longed to retort, “Have you only just realised?” but held back the words. “It's late. Shilong can see you home.” That was more than she could bear. After all her waiting, this was what she got: an offer to send her home. Had she sat there all that time merely waiting for Chen to escort her? Furious that Hu had given her no thought at all, she turned and stumbled away, leaving in a true burst of anger.

Hu and Chen both stared, then immediately recognised that something was wrong and hurried after her. “A’zhu! A’zhu!” “Miss Zhang!” At their combined calls she stopped. Quick to understand, Hu merely told Chen, “You go on by yourself.” “Very well.” Chen took leave of A’zhu as though nothing had happened. “Until tomorrow, Miss Zhang.” She could not ignore him and answered, “Until tomorrow.” Then she returned to her wicker chair. “This weather is too hot,” Hu said, following her with an apologetic smile. “The best thing would be something cooling to clear the heat.” She had expected him to ask why she was so angry, which would have given her an opening for her complaints. Instead he said this, leaving her unable to vent her temper. She could only punish him with silence. “Oh, red-date and lily-bulb soup! Excellent!” Pointing to Chen's empty bowl, he asked, “Could you fetch me a bowl too? It must be delicious.” She wanted to say there was none left, but feared that might turn their disagreement into a real quarrel. In the end, she fetched a bowl and put it in his hands. Yet she felt more aggrieved than ever. Her eyes grew hot, and two tears fell.

“What's this?” Hu could no longer pretend not to understand. “Why cry when nothing's wrong? If someone's offended you, tell me. Though I can't imagine anyone daring to do that.” Pleasant words, but words were all he offered. What was he really thinking? Sulking would achieve nothing. She must have a serious talk with him. “Do you know that I stayed here especially to wait for you?” she asked, wiping her tears. “Good heavens!” Hu made a great show of astonishment and tapped his forehead. “I'm so busy I can hardly think. Imagine my not realising you were waiting for me! I'm sorry, I'm sorry.” He took her hand and held it between his, leaving her uncertain whether to laugh or cry, love him or resent him. Hardest of all was that she could find no way to explain the feelings that had troubled her for so long. She had always thought that a man so quick-witted and so good at reading people must understand what she wanted. If he had given no sign, surely he was deliberately pretending otherwise. Yet sometimes she comforted herself with the thought that he was simply too busy to consider such things. Now that they were to talk seriously, she must first discover whether he truly had had no time to think or had thought about it and made other plans. Even that was hard to put into words. She sat with her head tilted, absorbed in her thoughts, letting his irrelevant chatter pass unheard.

“Well?” Hu nudged her. “Have you gone dumb, or deaf?” “Neither. I simply don't feel like talking. And if I did, I wouldn't know where to begin.” Her tone was calm, but the words were serious, as Hu naturally recognised. He had indeed been pretending not to understand, and lately he had formed other ideas, making him all the more cautious. “What is it that's so difficult?” he asked. “The difficulty is that I can't say it myself.” It was a good reply. Though indirect, it was as clear as an outright statement. Hu could no longer evade it. “Oh, so that's it. To tell the truth, you can't bring yourself to say it, and I can't find the time to act. Do you think I haven't considered it? I've thought it over seventeen or eighteen times. I absolutely stand by what I asked Fat Zhang to tell your mother. But I need time to arrange things. You've seen and heard how matters are just now. I never expected this visit to Huzhou to bring me a friend like Yu Si. Trading with foreign firms and opening Fukang's branch were both unplanned. You heard just now how tight my funds are in Hangzhou. They need me there to put things right, yet I can't even get away.” This speech soothed A’zhu like a dose of medicine that relieved a choking pressure. A smile came to her face. “Look at you! I say one thing, and out comes a whole torrent. Nobody can argue with you.” “It's wrong if I say nothing and wrong if I speak! You're terribly hard to please. All right, all right. Let's talk about something else.”

Their conversation naturally remained within the affairs of the Dajing Silk Firm. What interested A’zhu most was where Hu would be going, and she hinted that she too would like to visit Shanghai. He replied that in such heat it was better to stay put than travel. Besides, Old Zhang would certainly be going, and she ought to remain in Huzhou to help her mother look after the business. This was so reasonable that she said nothing. “Tell me,” he suddenly asked, “what do you think of Chen Shilong?” In what respect? She wanted to say something good about Chen, but did not know what to say. “He's very capable,” she answered broadly. “I mean his character. Would you call him the simple, honest sort, or the slick sort?” Simple and honest would mean useless; slick would mean untrustworthy. A’zhu felt that the question could not be answered on those terms. She shook her head. “Neither.” “Neither simple nor slick. Just ordinary. Is that it?” Ordinary was hardly a compliment either. Unwilling to do Chen an injustice, she again said, “No.” “Not this and not that. Then tell me what kind of person he is.” Half unable to answer, half angered by his pressing manner, she snapped, “I don't know! What has Chen Shilong to do with me? Please stop asking.” Her voice was sharp and quick.

Her face flushed with anger and her breathing quickened. Hu kept watching her with a smile, making her still more uncomfortable. She sat fuming for some time. When she noticed his gaze, she turned away, held her palm-leaf fan before her, and muttered, “Smirking rogue!” “All right, all right. It's all my fault. The night's getting cool. Come and sit inside.” This reminded her how late it was. Should she go home? If so, she must leave at once, and Hu would have to escort her. Being seen in the street together would be awkward, and she did not want to ask him for the favour. Yet staying seemed worse. She had slept here before, but always with her mother, which left no grounds for gossip. Now she and Hu would be alone, and that was different. “Are you really going to ignore me?” Hu continued. “Then I'll sit out here with you all night. But if you catch cold and fall ill tomorrow, you'll be the one to suffer.” His affectionate tone softened her anger. “I've never seen anyone like you,” she said. “You're as slippery as a loach!”

She meant that his attitude towards her was impossible to pin down. Hu had no answer, but he was growing impatient. There was much to do first thing tomorrow; he needed to get to bed and enjoy a good night's sleep. How could he help chafing at this waste of time? After a moment's thought, he could only hint, “Sit here a little longer, then. I'll go and sponge myself down.” Naturally, bed would follow. Hearing his impatience, she became anxious too and, without further deliberation, stood up. “I'm going home.” “Home?” That would mean finding someone to escort her. Of course, he could hardly refuse to go himself, but the journey there and back would take time and tire him, and he really did not want to stir. He tried to persuade her. “Why bother? Get whatever sleep you can here. It'll be daylight soon.” Still hesitating, A’zhu glanced at the dozing Aizhen and felt relieved. With her maid to accompany her, she need not fear gossip.

After a few more words they went to their separate rooms, but neither could sleep. Hu thought that the affair with A’zhu had become a real dilemma. With her temperament, she would prefer to be together all day, laughing and talking. Married to a straightforward young man, with just the two of them, she would surely be happy. With his own disposition, however, he would inevitably have several wives and concubines in time. A’zhu would be jealous. Why invite endless domestic turmoil? His thoughts returned to Chen. She did not seem to dislike him; she simply had her heart set on joining the Hu household and could think of nobody else. If Hu gradually let her grow distant while giving Chen every opportunity to draw close, the two movements together might produce a marriage.

Such a marriage would be an excellent thing. A’zhu's parents would certainly welcome the son-in-law, and the young couple would be entirely satisfied. Remembering the source of their happiness, they would credit Hu with it all. At the very least, Chen would devote himself wholeheartedly to helping Hu's business. With this settled in his mind, Hu fell peacefully asleep. Rising early the next morning, he reckoned that he had two major matters to attend to. First, Wang was going to the provincial capital to report on his administration and had invited Hu to travel with him. Hu must obtain a definite answer about the arrangements. Second, he must ask Yu where they could raise funds to cover the public money due at the provincial treasury by the month's end.

“You've come at just the right time!” Wang said on seeing him. “I was about to send for you. I have two things to discuss. First, I want a donation from you.” This came without explanation, and Hu did not understand. Whatever the donation was for, however, there was no reason to refuse. He answered generously, “Name the amount, Mr Xuexuan. Just say the word.” “I want to increase the academy's study allowances. What do you think?” Poor scholars often depended on the small awards given for the academy's monthly exercises. Nominally these paid for lamp oil for night study; in reality they fed their families. Hu understood such arrangements. “An excellent cause! I approve. Would two hundred taels be enough?” “You certainly give generously!” Wang laughed. “Two hundred taels in all: a hundred for the academy's allowances and a hundred for the foundling home, so that it can buy a few more mu of land.” “Agreed. Where should I pay it?”

“There's no hurry. Now for the second matter.” Wang continued, “The county militia arrangements have been settled. The situation is growing more urgent, and protecting the district and its people cannot wait. I must go to the provincial capital at once and settle the matter there so that we can begin. I intend to leave tomorrow. Can you be ready?” “How could I be ready tomorrow?” Hu considered. “Three days from now is the earliest I could leave.” “Then come and see me as soon as you arrive. And one more thing: what is happening about the money due to the province? I must have an answer if my superiors ask.” That was difficult. If Wang stayed in Huzhou, payment at the month's end would be acceptable. But once he went to the capital, the treasurer would ask why he had not brought the money with him. Wang would find that hard to answer, and Hu must think for him. “We agreed to remit it all by the month's end, but you can't go empty-handed. Could you delay three days and travel with me? In those three days I'll put together fifty thousand taels in cash for you. You'd arrive looking much better.” Wang considered. “That will do.” Once it was settled, Hu, eager to raise the money, went straight to Yu and explained. Their fortunes were bound together, and Yu had already pledged to take responsibility for the funds. He agreed at once: the full sum would be ready by the day they left.

Returning to Dajing, Hu summoned Huang Yi and Old Zhang. He told them he would be leaving in three days and asked whether all the goods could be prepared, loaded, and sent off with him. “There isn't time,” Huang answered. “I worked it out carefully this morning. We need five days.” “Today is the eighth of the seventh month. Five more days brings us to the thirteenth. You can still reach Shanghai before the twentieth.” An idea came to Hu. “I've already arranged things with His Honour Wang and can't break my word. We'll leave on the eleventh. You follow afterwards, and I'll wait in Hangzhou.” Then he turned to Old Zhang. “A’zhu wants to see Shanghai. Let her go along.” “Certainly!” Old Zhang warmly agreed. “She's worked hard lately and has grown thinner. Let her have a look around Shanghai.”

“There's something else.” Hu suddenly had another inspiration. “We must do good works!” Both Huang and Old Zhang stared, wondering what had prompted the announcement. What good works, and for whom? Naturally, Hu explained. The idea had come from Wang. “The first thing business needs is a peaceful market. Peace brings prosperity. Our good works will help maintain that peace.” Fond of proverbs, he found an apt one. “‘Hunger and cold turn men's thoughts to theft.’ And who suffers? The wealthy. So when business makes us money, we must do some good. This year we'll distribute rice vouchers, give away padded clothes, and provide coffins for those who can't afford them.” “Oh, that kind of good work,” Huang replied. “Those things are usually done in winter, towards the year's end. There's plenty of time.” “There are good works to do in hot weather too. The fierce late heat of autumn is still to come. Giving out tea and medicines is useful help.” Hu was decisive, especially over such a small matter. “Old Huang, once we've decided, let's act. Start today.” Huang knew his temperament well: work must be done quickly and properly, and money would be freely provided. That made it easy. The same day, a wooden stand appeared outside Dajing. On it stood two tea vats, each large enough for a full carrying load of water. Bamboo tubes, cut diagonally and fitted with handles, served as cups. The tea contained medicinal ingredients intended to clear internal heat and counter toxins. A fresh sheet of plum-red paper on the door announced: “Biwen Pills and Zhuge Marching Powder given free by this firm. Please inquire within.”

Dajing promptly became a busy place. In a single afternoon, more than two hundred bottles of Zhuge Marching Powder and a hundred packets of Biwen Pills were given away. Huang was deeply troubled. That evening he complained to Hu that, first, they might not be able to keep it up and, second, so many people coming for medicine would interfere with trade. “We've nearly finished buying the silk, so business won't be much affected. There may be many people, but the cost is really small. Naturally, the first day draws a crowd. It'll be better in a day or two. People who've already received medicine will be embarrassed to ask again. Besides, medicine isn't copper cash: more isn't always better. Don't worry.” “I have an idea,” Chen put in. “We should have the medicines specially prepared, in smaller quantities per package. The wrappers should be printed in red: ‘Presented by the Dajing Silk Firm.’ The little porcelain bottles for the Marching Powder should be specially fired too, with Dajing's name on them.” “That would take a great deal of arranging. It's too late this year. We'd have to leave it till next year.” Huang plainly did not want additional trouble. Hu said nothing then, but afterwards called Chen aside. “Shilong, you have a good head. To tell the truth, you're the only one who understands what I mean by giving out tea and medicines. Good works won't go unrewarded. I'm using them to make a name for myself, though I couldn't say so aloud. You guessed it. Very clever.”

Chen was excited by this encouragement. “I've learned a great deal from you too, Mr Hu,” he answered earnestly. “Take your time. Stay with me long enough, and I guarantee you'll get somewhere. Now there's something else. When A’zhu goes to Hangzhou this time, look after her. She has a child's disposition and likes company and excitement. There's nothing to do on a boat, so keep her company.” “I understand.” Did he? Hu doubted it. He would need another hint or two. “Shilong,” he asked casually, “what do you make of A’zhu and me?” How could Chen answer that? He merely smiled, showing a row of white teeth and looking touchingly boyish. “There's no need to feel awkward. Say what you think.” Pressed beyond escape, Chen answered, “Aren't you very fond of Miss Zhang? Everyone says you're going to establish a household here in Huzhou as well.” “Yes, I would like a home in Huzhou. Coming and going, I'd have somewhere comfortable to stay and eat. But nothing has happened between A’zhu and me.” The two parts of this statement did not quite fit together. Chen was puzzled. “Do you have some other plan?” “It's too early to say. We'll see when I come again.”

“Then what about Miss Zhang?” Chen asked after thinking, troubled on her behalf and feeling she was being unfairly treated. “Her whole heart is set on you.” “I know. That's why I have to take things slowly. Fortunately, as I said, we've behaved properly. Nothing has happened, so there shouldn't be any great difficulty.” This meant Hu had decided not to take A’zhu. But why? Chen was astonished. “Mr Hu, there's something I simply can't help asking.” He blinked. “What's wrong with Miss Zhang? To be honest, you could search with a lantern and not find another like her.” Those few words showed how much he admired her. Hu thought his good deed would succeed and was likely to end happily. He was pleased, but showed nothing. Instead he sighed. “Ah, you don't understand my feelings. If A’zhu weren't so exceptional, I might simply establish another household and give it no further thought. It's precisely because she's so rare that I can't bring myself to do it.” “Can't bring yourself?” It grew more mysterious with every word. Chen asked bluntly, “Why?”

“First, however much we call it two wives of equal standing, others would still see her as a concubine. That would be unfair to her. Second, you see how I live, constantly moving from place to place. I couldn't bear to leave her lonely here in Huzhou.” “Mr Hu!” Chen exclaimed. “You really are a good man.” “Not necessarily. But enough of that. Shilong,” Hu lowered his voice, “I truly regard you as my own younger brother. There's nothing I wouldn't discuss with you. You're clever enough to understand my intentions. Don't repeat what I've just said to A’zhu or her parents. Now that you know what I mean, you should know how to act.” Chen understood at last and was overjoyed. So Hu was stepping aside for him! No wonder he kept stressing that nothing had happened between himself and A’zhu: he meant that he was not handing on some burden he had tired of carrying. This kindness deserved real gratitude. But since Hu preferred not to state it openly, Chen need not make a fuss of thanking him. They understood one another without words.

One point still needed clarification. “Mr Hu, when Miss Zhang speaks to me about you, what should I say?” The question showed that Chen had understood. Hu answered, “You can casually bring up two things from time to time. First, my wife has a fierce temper. Second, I'm busy and won't stay in any one place. In a word, however you put it, you must gradually help her forget me.” “Very well. I know what to do.” With this unspoken understanding established, Hu began that very day to find every opportunity for Chen to be close to the Zhang family. Messages, errands, anything connected with Old Zhang—all went through Chen. Meanwhile Hu moved into the prefectural yamen, using Wang's official business as his excuse, and stayed out of sight all day. The following day was the tenth. By noon there was still no sign of him, and A’zhu's mother grew impatient. “Shilong, what's come over your Mr Hu? He's leaving tomorrow. Things need to be settled. Surely we should all meet before he goes.” “He really is busy. Fortunately, everything has been arranged. We sail on the thirteenth. If there's anything else, we can ask him in Hangzhou.” True enough, but surely they ought at least to give him a farewell dinner. She had been planning it for two days, assuming without saying so that he would eat his last evening meal at home. Now it seemed she must make an explicit arrangement. “Shilong, do me a favour. Please go at once and tell him he must come back to dinner tonight, whatever happens.”

Chen naturally went, but did not return to the Zhang house until four in the afternoon. By then A’zhu and her mother had been worrying and speculating for a long time. “Why have you only just come back?” A’zhu demanded reproachfully. “I was anxious too,” he answered calmly. “Mr Hu was discussing official business in His Honour Wang's office and told me to wait. I waited a couple of hours. Afraid you would be worrying, I thought I'd come back first to let you know. Just as I was leaving, he came out. We'd scarcely exchanged three sentences when Wang sent an attendant to ask him back. Mr Hu said he'd be out immediately and told me not to leave. Who'd have thought it would take another hour?” “Then I blamed you unfairly.” A’zhu smiled apologetically. “He says he'll certainly come, but doesn't know when. Probably seven at the earliest.” “Seven it is,” her mother said. “We'd wait till midnight if necessary. Only there are two dishes that won't taste right if they're kept too long.” “I'll go to the silk firm, then. There's plenty to do.” “You must come to dinner too.” Still uneasy, she added, “Better go to the yamen and wait, then come back with him.” Chen agreed and had just stepped outside when A’zhu called after him, “Wait! I'll come with you.”

They walked together from the Zhang house to Dajing. Chen had many friends and greeted people all along the way. Some looked curiously at A’zhu, and he invariably introduced her with great seriousness: “This is Miss Zhang.” After two or three such introductions, she complained again. “Stop calling me ‘Miss’ all the time. What young lady of a grand family goes wandering about the streets?” “Then what should I call you?” She did not answer. Being called Miss Zhang at home was quite gratifying, but hearing it before strangers embarrassed her. Yet she did not want him to use her childhood name either. There was really no harm in it, but once he became accustomed to that, changing later would be difficult. From Miss Zhang to Mrs Hu, or his mentor's wife, would be a natural progression. At the thought of her future position, her cheeks warmed. Afraid Chen might notice, she stole a glance at him. He too was watching. Their eyes met; he was untroubled, but she hastily looked away and blushed more deeply.

Between her agitation and the heat of the westering sun, beads of sweat appeared on her forehead. In her hurried departure she had forgotten her handkerchief. A movement of her hand was enough for Chen to see what she needed. He quietly took a white Hangzhou-silk handkerchief from his sleeve and slipped it to her. It looked spotless, almost unused, and as she needed it she accepted without ceremony. As she wiped her face, she noticed an unfamiliar scent. For a moment she was lost in thoughts and feelings she could not quite explain. She kept the handkerchief in her hand, occasionally using it to wipe away the sweat, and returned it only when they reached Dajing's entrance.

The silk firm was piled high with bales of Qili silk. Huang and Old Zhang were counting them and working out the total accounts. Chen and A’zhu arrived at just the right time to help with the silk stacked in the guest rooms at the rear. Chen counted, and A’zhu kept the record. They were still not finished at dusk when she reminded him, “You ought to go to the yamen. We can count the rest tonight.” Seeing that the work would indeed take longer, Chen stopped and hurried to the prefectural yamen, then accompanied Hu to the Zhang house. Hu had only just taken off his outer robe, sat down, and received a cup of tea when Old Zhang brought a sheet of figures. “We haven't finished the exact count, but we have an estimate: about eight hundred and fifty bales. Including freight, each bale will cost around two hundred and eighty foreign silver dollars. We've already put two hundred thousand taels into this shipment.” Hu asked Chen, “Eight hundred and fifty bales at two hundred and eighty dollars each—what does that come to?” “Two hundred and thirty-eight thousand,” Chen answered promptly. Hu paused. “Correct.” Then he asked Old Zhang, “Do you know whether prices paid by the foreign firms have changed these last few days?” “No real change.” “Still around three hundred dollars. That gives us twenty per bale, only seventeen thousand five hundred in all.” “That's a considerable sum! More than seventeen thousand foreign dollars on a single deal!”

Honest Old Zhang was easily satisfied. Hu felt that there was little point in discussing the matter deeply with him. After a moment's thought, he merely said, “Whatever happens, Dajing's commission is yours to earn. Old Zhang, you're the proprietor of Dajing in any case. You can sell that boat of yours.” Old Zhang was bewildered. Why say that? Chen, however, understood: Hu was making clear that, even if they did not become relatives, he would continue to help Old Zhang. If sincere, that was truly generous. “There's no need to sell the boat,” Old Zhang answered. “It makes your journeys easier.” “Very well. But you needn't concern yourself with the boat any longer. Put all your energy into the silk firm. It's a pity Shilong can't help you.” “Why not?” Old Zhang grew alarmed. “Without him, and with you away from Huzhou, I'm afraid I can't manage alone. To tell the truth, I can't keep Mr Huang in hand.”

Old Zhang was anxious, but Hu remained perfectly calm. Such matters were no difficulty to him. Besides, a new idea had occurred to him and needed a little thought, so he merely smiled without answering. Honest Old Zhang took this for disbelief. He had personally experienced Huang's domineering ways, but to describe them behind the man's back seemed disloyal. Yet silence would leave Hu unconvinced. What could he do? Find a witness. “Shilong knows what Mr Huang is like.” Looking at Chen, he said, “Please explain to Mr Hu.” “No need!” Hu waved this aside. “I can see for myself. To be honest, everything on this visit to Huzhou has gone well except this gentleman: I haven't yet brought him into line. Naturally, you can't control him, but someone can. Don't worry. For the moment there's nothing urgent. We'll discuss it when you return from Shanghai.” “What will happen then?” “Then...” Hu glanced at Chen. “I'll have an excellent arrangement, one that will satisfy you completely.”

While they talked, A’zhu laid out bowls and chopsticks and listened carefully. She thought the best arrangement would be to dispense with Huang and have Chen help instead. But she had also heard that Hu intended Chen to learn a foreign language and eventually represent the firm in Shanghai, dealing exclusively with foreigners. That was an important position too, and Hu might not be willing to change his plans. What other satisfactory arrangement could there be? Her idea happened to be exactly the same as Hu's, but he said nothing because the time was not yet ripe. They now sat down together to eat. Hu occupied the place of honour, Old Zhang sat to his left, and Chen to his right. The seats at the lower end were for A’zhu and her mother. Her mother was still in the kitchen, and A’zhu took the right-hand seat, next to Chen. “Come and fetch the dishes!” her mother called from the kitchen, Aizhen having been sent out on an errand. Chen was the first to rise. Quite naturally, A’zhu caught his arm. “Stay where you are. I'll go.” He followed her nevertheless. They went back and forth together, and whatever he said along the way kept making her laugh. Drinking with Old Zhang, Hu watched them out of the corner of his eye and was quietly amused.

After dinner and a short rest, Hu rose to leave again, saying he still had something to discuss with Yu. Disappointment showed on the faces of both women. They seemed to have a great deal to say to him, yet on reflection neither could find a single matter so urgent that it must be raised now. They could only let him go. “We'll meet in Hangzhou,” was his entire farewell. At the door, A’zhu's mother caught up and called him back. “When will you come to Huzhou again?” “How can I say just now?” She glanced behind her. A’zhu was not nearby. “Keep that matter in mind. It must be settled this year.” “Yes, yes,” Hu replied. “Before this year is out, there will certainly be a splendid wedding. I'll definitely come for it.” If he was to be the groom, of course he must come. Why say so? His words struck her as odd, but never in her dreams did she imagine that Hu was no longer her prospective son-in-law.
