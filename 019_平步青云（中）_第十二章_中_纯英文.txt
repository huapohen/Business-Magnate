HU XUEYAN

INSTALLMENT 019
A Swift Rise to Prominence, Volume II — Chapter Twelve, middle part

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

The female Zhang Fei had a warm heart and was particularly fond of A’zhu. Seeing the unhappiness that flickered across the girl's face, she could not help asking, “What's the matter? Tell me what's troubling you.” How could A’zhu confide in anyone about this, especially her? “Nothing, nothing!” She did her best to sound carefree. “I couldn't be more comfortable here. What could trouble me?” “There's something I don't understand.” The Seventh Lady spoke her mind without much thought for the consequences. “Since your Mr Hu has arrived, why hasn't he come to see you?” A’zhu was deeply embarrassed. Alarmed, Mrs You hurried to stop her. “There you go again! A true headstrong Zhang Fei.” “Why? What's wrong with asking?” Mrs You was no fool either. Seeing how matters stood, she suddenly decided to use the female Zhang Fei to her advantage. She sighed deliberately. “Every household has its own difficult scripture to recite. We must try to persuade our Sister Zhang to understand Mr Hu's difficulties.” To speak of understanding and persuasion implied that Hu was at fault. While A’zhu was still considering those words, the Seventh Lady could contain herself no longer. Taking her hand and looking straight at her, she said, “Of course something's troubling you! You've been badly treated. However busy he is, he can't spare a moment to see you? Tell that to the ghosts! Men are contemptible creatures. When they want you, you're an empress. Once they change their minds, you can kowtow to them and get a fist in return. I've seen plenty of that sort.”

“My dear lady, my dear lady!” Mrs You sounded almost as though she were pleading for mercy. “Will you spare me? What is all this shouting about?” “Very well!” The Seventh Lady lowered her voice but spoke still faster and more urgently. Holding A’zhu with one hand, she pointed at her sister-in-law with the other. “Sister Zhang couldn't have made it clearer. He agreed to establish a household in each place. Why hasn't it been done long ago? Now he comes to Songjiang and won't even see her. What is that supposed to mean?” Turning to A’zhu, she continued, “I thought there was something wrong from the beginning and felt it unfair to you. At first I wondered whether I'd misjudged him. Now it's plain enough: a devoted woman and a faithless man!” “Headstrong Zhang Fei, headstrong Zhang Fei! You really are...” Mrs You left the sentence unfinished. Listening beside them, A’zhu felt acutely uncomfortable, though she could not immediately tell whether the cause was the Seventh Lady or Hu. Ashamed and angry, she shifted quietly until her face was in shadow, hidden from the two women.

They would not allow even that. Both turned towards her. Taking her other hand, Mrs You comforted her. “Sister Zhang, don't listen to her. That's her nature: the moment she opens her mouth, she offends someone.” A’zhu now had to be gracious. “Seventh Sister means well by me. I don't blame her.” “That's a fair-minded thing to say!” The Seventh Lady looked still more indignant on her behalf. “This concerns your whole life. Now that we've brought it into the open, let's think it through properly.” She asked Mrs You, “What exactly is Mr Hu thinking, behaving like this?” “Nine out of ten of your questions are impossible to answer,” Mrs You replied with a smile. “Still...” She deliberately stopped and watched A’zhu carefully, trying to read her feelings. That was not easy. A’zhu considered their concern unnecessary and maintained her composure as a way of refusing their kindness.

The Seventh Lady hardly understood this signal; even if she had, it would not have restrained her tongue. Assuming a gentler manner, she said, “Sister Zhang, you're such a fine young woman. Why accept an inferior place?” Hearing her express the same view as her brother, Mrs You saw a chance to speak through her. When A’zhu remained silent, she supplied the next line like a performer in a duet. “What do you mean, an inferior place?” “Bowing and scraping as a lesser wife—isn't that accepting an inferior place?” The speaker meant no particular wound, but her listener felt one. The words touched A’zhu's hidden sore, and composure became impossible. “Besides, it might be tolerable if the wife were good-tempered. Otherwise, lowering yourself like that is simply pressing your warm face against someone's cold backside.” Another of her coarse expressions! Mrs You had repeatedly warned her that their guest was a young lady and such language must be restrained. Yet nature would have its way. Just now, however, she needed the Seventh Lady as her supporting player and could not stop to scold. She merely sighed. “Yes, that's precisely Mr Hu's difficulty.” There was a meaning behind this that A’zhu must discover, but someone else asked for her. “What? Is the woman at home a tigress?” “From what Mr Hu says, she's very fierce.” “Then he's at fault! With a jar of vinegar at home, why deceive our Sister Zhang?” “I must say something fair on his behalf,” Mrs You answered earnestly. “He intended to settle matters with his wife before holding the wedding. When she wouldn't agree, he had to retreat. That's hardly deception.”

“What?” A’zhu exclaimed. “Fifth Sister-in-Law, how do you know?” “Her brother—Brother Five—told me everything.” Mrs You indicated the Seventh Lady. “Mr Hu really does have difficulties. He couldn't bring himself to tell you, but keeping them to himself was no solution either, so he spoke to a close friend. Don't worry, Sister Zhang. We'll think of a way.” What way? The meaning was unclear, and A’zhu's mind was too tangled to examine it. Her sole wish was to speak to Hu face to face. “There's always a way. We needn't stand on ceremony with a heartless man. But...” The Seventh Lady lowered her voice. “Tell me honestly. You've travelled back and forth together on the boat and stayed in the same place in Huzhou. Have you and he ever...” Before she could finish, A’zhu cried out in embarrassment and alarm. “No!” Afraid they might not believe her, she spoke with absolute firmness. “Absolutely not! I'm not that sort of girl.” “I know, I know.” The Seventh Lady sounded greatly relieved. “If he hasn't taken advantage of you, things are easier still.” “Exactly,” Mrs You agreed. “This isn't such a difficult affair yet. It all depends on you.” Again there was a deeper meaning for A’zhu to consider, but the Seventh Lady talked too much to give her time.

“It's fortunate you found out early,” she said. “Out of ten men, eleven want something new. If he's treating you like this before he's even won you over, imagine what he'll do once he's had his way. He'll cast you aside, and then even your tears will do no good.” She considered this restrained language, but A’zhu, who understood relations between men and women only imperfectly, flushed and lowered her head, not knowing where to look. Coarse though the female Zhang Fei's words were, they revealed a side of the matter she had never grasped. She had heard that men tired of the familiar and sought the new, but never had it been explained so plainly. She remembered how Hu had sometimes overstepped the bounds, pressing further and further, and how she had stood firm when it mattered. She had been right to do so.

This sense of relief eased her embarrassment. Nor did her whole heart now seem bound so immovably to Hu. She raised her face, smoothed her hair, and sipped the honeysuckle tea intended to clear heat and toxins. Calmly she asked, “Fifth Sister-in-Law, Seventh Sister, you say you'll think of a way for me. What way?” Mrs You had been waiting for the question. They had reached a crucial point, and she must choose her words carefully. Pressing the Seventh Lady's hand to keep her from interrupting, she asked in return, “That depends on your own wishes. The main decision must be yours. If you want to go east, we'll find a road east. If you want to go west, we'll see whether that road is open.” A’zhu understood. There were two paths: continue with Hu, or wipe away all the sweet words and solemn promises of the past. Understanding them was one thing; making the choice on the spot was beyond her.

“In my view, such things require willingness on both sides. Since he has said this, it wouldn't be right to force him. Leaving everything else aside, you must at least preserve your own dignity.” “Exactly!” The Seventh Lady could hold back no longer. “Fifth Sister-in-Law is absolutely right. You're a delicate flower, not stale fish left on the market stall that we must force on him before it spoils!” The words struck A’zhu like mustard, sending resentment up into her nose until she nearly wept. She was a delicate flower, yet Hu treated her like unwanted fish, behaving strangely and scarcely deigning to notice her. It hurt to think of it. Through clenched teeth she said, “Who would be so cheap as to force herself on him?” “Good. You've seen it clearly,” the Seventh Lady answered. “To be honest, even having equal standing in two households was a terrible concession for you. And still he talks of difficulties! A man like that can keep his whole family to himself, thank you!”

Half the task was accomplished. Why continue abusing Hu and merely create an enemy? Mrs You defended him. “Seventh Sister, you're going too far. Mr Hu is as good a man as one could find. There are things beyond his power, and he doesn't wish to waste Sister Zhang's youth. Don't misjudge him.” One virtue of the Seventh Lady was her readiness to admit an error. Hu had had many opportunities to win A’zhu, yet nothing had happened between them. He had also done a great deal for the Zhang family. A man like that was indeed uncommon. She smiled. “On reflection, you're right. After all his trouble and effort, he gets nothing—and is scolded into the bargain. It really is unfair.” A’zhu's unsettled heart had been rising and falling with the two women's words. Now she remembered all Hu's kindness and could hardly bear to give him up. Yet her defiant words had already been spoken. A sail once lowered was difficult to raise again. Regret stirred within her, but she could not express the pain. Two tears rolled down her cheeks.

“Why!” the Seventh Lady asked in astonishment. “What are you crying for?” “Don't be sad,” Mrs You urged. “You nearly took the wrong road and have turned back in time. You ought to be happy.” How could she be happy? The Seventh Lady had said Hu's efforts had come to nothing. Hadn't hers too? His disappointment was of his own choosing; she had no choice in hers. In the stillness of the night, all the past happiness and future plans would become reflections in a mirror, moonlight on water. How was she to live with that? As A’zhu sat lost in thought, Mrs You took the opportunity to signal to her sister-in-law that enough had been said. But the Seventh Lady did not understand. When Mrs You rose to pour tea, she followed and asked quietly, “Have you something to tell me?” There had been nothing, but since she asked, they might as well talk. “A word or two. Only I'm afraid your tongue will run away with you. Half the affair is settled, but unless the other half succeeds, none of it does.” “What do you mean?” “Mr Hu intends...” Mrs You glanced at A’zhu and drew the Seventh Lady outside the pavilion before lowering her voice. “He also wants to arrange a marriage for our Sister Zhang.”

“To whom?” “That young fellow surnamed Chen.” “Him!” The Seventh Lady cried out in delighted surprise. “Softly, softly!” Mrs You reproached her. “A true headstrong Zhang Fei! You never consider anything.” “He's a good young man.” The Seventh Lady dropped her voice almost to a whisper. Her warm heart was overflowing with joy for A’zhu. “That's the right match! Exactly right!” “Don't rejoice yet. We don't know how things will turn out.” “I'll persuade her. I'll make her agree. She's told me she thinks very well of him.” “Oh?” Mrs You listened closely. “What did she say?” “It's really funny. She told me he was capable and kind, and that she meant to find him a good wife one day. Little did she know: as the old joke goes, a girl raised as a future bride starts matchmaking, but cannot secure her own fate!” At this she burst into laughter, bending and stamping until she looked quite foolish. Even A’zhu was amused. “What are you laughing at?” “You!” Her laughter, only just subsiding, broke out again. “Silly creature!” Mrs You gave her a look, but could not help laughing too. Their mysterious behaviour only increased A’zhu's suspicions. She kept asking what she had done to amuse them so much. Quick-witted Mrs You invented a story and put her off.

They turned to other subjects, ate a late-night snack, and found that it was already midnight. Mrs You was tired from managing the household all day. She did not do the actual drudgery herself, but You Laowu entertained many guests and supported plenty of people who lived at his expense. Every day they fed four or five tables of people. Directing the servants in their reception was work enough. By now she could hardly stay awake. “What about you two?” she asked. “It's cool now. You should go to bed too.” “I'm not sleepy yet. I'd like to sit a little longer,” A’zhu replied. In truth, her troubles would keep her awake even in bed. “I'm not sleepy either,” the Seventh Lady said. “Now that it's cool, we can enjoy sitting out.” Mrs You reflected that, left together, they would certainly return to Hu and Chen. She feared the Seventh Lady lacked the composure to proceed slowly and might ruin a promising affair through haste. She hesitated uneasily. “Go to your room,” the Seventh Lady reassured her, guessing her concern. “We'll sit a little longer, then go to bed.” “Anything more can wait until tomorrow.” Mrs You gave her another pointed hint. “Things settle better when allowed time. I'm always telling you, but you never seem willing to listen.” “I know, I know. Don't worry.” This exchange plainly concealed much that had not been said. As soon as Mrs You left, A’zhu asked, “What did she mean about things settling better when allowed time?”

“Your affair, of course.” The Seventh Lady considered. “After all our talk, what do you intend to do? It isn't that he doesn't want you. A girl like you needn't fear that nobody will. But Mr Hu is afraid to eat the steamed bun already at his lips, and you can't force it into his mouth.” A’zhu found the first half pleasant enough, but the ending made her frown. “Seventh Sister, your comparisons are always so strange that I don't know how to answer.” “Why? I speak the truth. Whatever I think, I say. There's not a scrap of pretence in me.” “I know you're sincere with people. But...” How was she to explain? Many things could be thought but not spoken. A’zhu understood this, yet could not make the principle clear. She could only smile helplessly. “But what?” The Seventh Lady began to understand. “You blame me for being too blunt and saying things you don't like to hear?” That was partly right, but A’zhu could not admit it. “No, no, I don't blame you at all. Everyone here—Brother Five, Fifth Sister-in-Law, and you—has been as kind as possible. Since we're like family, of course we should speak frankly.”

“Good!” Once again the Seventh Lady could not restrain herself. “You know my nature. I take other people's affairs as seriously as my own, especially yours. Now let's make a long story short. What exactly do you mean to do about Mr Hu?” A’zhu wanted to evade the question but could not. After thinking, she could only offer an excuse. “My mother arranged this, Seventh Sister. I'll have to ask her.” “That's a strange thing to say! Have you no opinion of your own?” “I can't disregard my parents.” “Oh, oh! What a dutiful daughter!” The sarcasm embarrassed A’zhu. “Seventh Sister,” she appealed, “let me think. I'll talk to you tomorrow morning.” From years of observing her own household, the Seventh Lady knew very well how to read an expression. Seeing A’zhu's face, she realised that pressing further would be tactless. “Take your time, take your time. Once you've decided what you want, I'll certainly help.” “Thank you.” A’zhu took her hand. “It's fortunate I'm staying with you. Anywhere else, I wouldn't even have someone to tell my troubles to.”

Much of this was intended to strengthen their friendship. In fact, she already felt the misery of having nobody she could confide in. The Seventh Lady's warmth was almost hot enough to burn one's hands. Mrs You was smooth and worldly, apparently taking Hu's side for reasons of interest. That left only Chen. They had almost reached the point where they could talk of anything, but whether she should discuss this particular matter with him was doubtful. Even if she did, he worked for Hu and depended on him for advancement. Would he help her stand against him? That was doubtful too.

Her thoughts twisted and turned, always returning to Hu. What might happen later was secondary. What weighed on her now, what she could not dismiss and must ask him, was why he had changed his heart. Throughout the night, therefore, she planned how to find him the next day and what to say. In imagination she quarrelled with that heartless man face to face, feeling both furious and satisfied: furious because he could give no reason, satisfied because she had scolded him without mercy. When the imaginary scolding ended, however, caution returned. Whatever happened, Hu was enormously important to her parents. Where else could they find someone so generous? Even if he endured her abuse, other people would accuse her of repaying kindness with hostility. The thought discouraged her and made her feel still more wronged. She truly had a grievance she must swallow because there was nowhere to complain.

After a poor night's sleep, she could not sleep late either. In the heat, everyone took advantage of the cool early hours to work, and as a guest she could hardly remain in bed alone. The You household, however, did not stand on ceremony with her. When she had washed and come out, breakfast was on the table and both women were already eating. After greeting her, the Seventh Lady studied her face. “Your eyes have sunk in. You must have been awake all night. Come, eat something and then go back to sleep.” A’zhu said nothing, merely looking unhappily at the food. Songjiang produced rice, and even breakfast was fried rice. She had no appetite for it and wanted only a bowl of soup. “Go on without me. I'm not hungry.” Mrs You put down her chopsticks, felt A’zhu's forehead, and compared it with her own. Frowning, she said, “You're a little feverish. We'd better have a physician see you.” “No, no!” A’zhu felt she was not ill. “I'm perfectly well. Why call a physician? What an idea, Fifth Sister-in-Law!” “Then take a little medicine first.” The You household kept a great many prepared medicines, brought back by grain boats travelling north and south from famous shops in the capital such as Tongrentang and Xi Heniantang. They boiled a piece of shenqu, a medicinal ferment, with mature ginger and red dates and gave her the strong decoction. She perspired a little and felt much better, but her mind was weary, her eyes heavy and sore. All she wanted was a good sleep.

Yet something remained unsettled. She wanted to see her father but feared meeting Hu. Everything she had planned in the night had been overturned. She could not understand what frightened her: a quarrel with Hu that might bring trouble on her parents, a shock she could not bear, or even the thought of making Hu uncomfortable face to face? Tired but unable to find peace, she was unbearably restless. How she regretted this journey! At least beside her mother, a fit of temper or a good cry would offer some release. Here there was nobody to ease her unhappiness, and she had to force herself to maintain the proper manner of a guest. It was misery. Again she resented Hu. He himself had told her father to let her visit Shanghai. What was the meaning of bringing her along and then abandoning her? Leave everything else aside: she could ask him just that. If he could give no satisfactory answer, she would use it to mock him sharply and release some of the anger pent up since yesterday. The thought revived her without her noticing. While the Seventh Lady was absent, she told Mrs You, “Fifth Sister-in-Law, I'd like to see my father. Please send someone with me.” “Of course. But you're not well, and there's no harm in waiting. We're going to Shanghai in a few days. You can meet him then.” “I'd better go. Otherwise he'll worry.” Given that reason, Mrs You could hardly dissuade her. She was looking for someone to accompany A’zhu to Old Zhang's boat when Chen arrived.

“Just in time!” Mrs You said to him solemnly. “Take good care of her on the way to her father. Keep to the shade. She's feverish.” They left one behind the other and walked beneath the eaves, out of the sun. Chen was most attentive, looking back every few steps and repeatedly telling her to take care. Their manner was neither that of brother and sister nor of husband and wife, and people stared. A’zhu felt embarrassed and reproached him inwardly. It was broad daylight and an easy road. Why call out all the way as though deliberately trying to attract attention? At the end of the lane, the view opened onto a quiet riverside path. Looking towards the boats moored beneath the willows, A’zhu suddenly stopped. “Hey!” Chen turned at the sound. “Who are you calling?” “There's nobody else here. What a ridiculous question!” “Oh, you mean me. Have you something to say?” “Naturally. Why else would I stop you?” She considered. “Have you heard anything?” “What sort of thing? From whom?” “Are you pretending not to understand, or what?” She was growing angry. “Oh!” At last Chen understood. “You mean Mr Hu. He says a great deal. What particular subject do you mean?” “Me, of course!”

“Nothing about that. He only said he must get to Shanghai to arrange business and would fetch you in a few days. That certainly isn't quite right.” The criticism comforted her a little. “Even you know he's in the wrong!” she said with a bitter smile. “He agreed to let me visit Shanghai, then left me stranded halfway. Isn't that deliberate ill-treatment?” The word brought back his inexplicable change of heart. Anger surged through her; her eyes grew hot, and she could contain herself no longer. Stamping her foot, she cried, “He planned it! He meant to hurt me! He meant to leave me halfway! He has no conscience!” Chen was alarmed and hurt. He had made so much effort all the way from Huzhou, yet her heart still belonged entirely to Hu. On second thoughts, however, his failing courage revived. The worst thing was for a woman's grievance to remain pent up, brooded over day and night until it sank into her very bones. Then there would be no turning her heart. An outburst like this would empty it of its burden, leaving room for him to enter. He therefore remained silent and merely offered a large white handkerchief for her tears. Somehow that small gesture made an impression on her and stirred a memory: when they had gone into town together in Huzhou, he had always offered just such a handkerchief to wipe away the sweat. The mind could not attend to two things at once. As her thoughts wandered, her tears unconsciously stopped. Feeling both embarrassed and rather sorry for herself, she dried her eyes, blew her nose, and began walking again.

“Wait!” This time Chen stopped her. When she turned, he asked, “What will you say when your father asks why you've been crying?” She considered. “I won't say anything. There's nothing to say.” “You may keep silent, but if he asks me, I can't pretend to be dumb.” “You...” She gave her instructions. “Just say I'm homesick.” “Very well. Let's go.” On the boat, Old Zhang did indeed ask in surprise, and while A’zhu remained silent, Chen gave the answer she had supplied. “Then you must have been upset by something. Staying in another household...” “What has it to do with them?” A’zhu interrupted. “The You family couldn't be kinder. Don't blame them unfairly, Father.” “Then what's upset you? You wouldn't suddenly become homesick for no reason.” “I think,” Chen said, “perhaps it's because Mr Hu won't take Miss Zhang to Shanghai.” “You mustn't blame him for that. He explained it to me. Once he gets there, he'll have a thousand business matters to attend to. He won't have time to look after you, and he won't feel easy leaving you unattended. When things are a little more settled, he'll fetch you and give you a proper outing for a couple of days. Every word of that is reasonable. You're a sensible girl. Can't you understand?”

A’zhu listened with an inward sneer. When he had finished, she said bitterly, “What a clever tongue he has! He could lie a man into his grave and never answer for it. You're the only one who still believes him.” “What?” Old Zhang was astonished. When she would not answer, he turned to Chen. “What does A’zhu mean?” Chen could hardly tell him plainly, but simply pleading ignorance would not do either. Better to withdraw and let father and daughter speak privately. “Ask Miss Zhang herself,” he said. Then he left the cabin, crossed the gangplank, and stood in the shade of the willows. “A’zhu,” Old Zhang asked gravely inside the boat, “what exactly has happened? Tell me.” How? Say that the man no longer wanted her? That seemed to degrade herself, and she would not utter it. Say he had changed his heart? It was too vague. If her father insisted on getting to the bottom of it, she would still have no answer. She was in a difficult position. “Tell me!” he urged. After thinking for a long time, she could only say, “I wish I'd never come on this journey.”

Unable to understand her, Old Zhang grew impatient. “Can't you speak plainly? What's it all about?” “Don't ask me. Why don't you ask him?” Him could only mean Hu. Old Zhang became uneasy. “Have you quarrelled?” he asked with a frown. “I haven't even seen him. How could we quarrel?” She gave a bitter little laugh. “If I did see him, there certainly would be a quarrel!” “Why? What's he done wrong?” Old Zhang reproached his daughter. “You must learn to control your temper. Always getting angry only harms your health.” His first questions almost made her flare up again, but the last sentence softened her. Family was family, after all. With parents like hers, she had been born under a fortunate star. The thought broadened her outlook. What she had been unable either to face or to let go now seemed manageable. “It's nothing so very serious!” Unconsciously infected by the Seventh Lady's manner, she drew herself up with manly defiance and spoke loudly. “From now on, he goes his way and I go mine. I won't even quarrel. Nothing would come of it. When he speaks, I'll just smile. Let him lie in bed at night and search his conscience, and see whether he feels comfortable!” Why this sudden complete break? Old Zhang was surprised but remained calm. He had never much approved of giving her to Hu, so this development did not seem entirely bad. Still, he must understand it. Her manner suggested that Hu had made a definite statement, yet she said she had not even seen him. “Has he sent you a message through someone?” “Naturally! How else would I know what wicked thoughts he's hiding?”

“Don't start abusing people,” Old Zhang rebuked her. “Whatever happens, he's a good man.” “Of course you and Mother think he's good. Without him, where would you be today?” This was an exceptionally rude thing to say to her father. Old Zhang was somewhat uncompromising in matters of principle and could not endure the accusation that he coveted wealth and influence. His expression changed drastically. A’zhu had spoken for the pleasure of saying it, without considering the consequences. Looking up, she was startled by his face. Only then did she realise what she had done. She hurried to smile and explain, but was too late. “Do you think I'm selling my daughter?” His voice was as cold and hard as iron. “I don't want to be proprietor of any silk firm. There's no need to go to Shanghai. We'll return to Huzhou today.” A’zhu had not expected such anger. She knew he was stubborn and would carry out what he said. Overwhelmed by panic, regret, and anger at herself, she burst into tears. The sight hurt Old Zhang deeply, but he could not immediately relax his stern expression. He continued to scold angrily. “What are you crying for? If you must cry, do it at home!” Now she felt the added injustice of being rebuked and cried harder. Her sobs carried to the bank on the breeze. Hearing them, Chen had to come and see what had happened.

Seeing A’zhu wipe her eyes on her sleeve, Chen again offered his handkerchief. “That's twice you've cried today,” he joked. She had been unable to find anything to say and felt wretched beyond words. Here was someone on whom to vent it. Flinging the handkerchief back at him, she gave him a scornful look. “What's it to you? I could cry ten times, and it would still be none of your business!” Her manner as she took her temper out on Chen was that of a spoiled but innocent little girl. Old Zhang could not help being amused, and his resolve softened. Wasn't getting angry with her merely putting himself on the level of a child? Nevertheless, familiar as Chen was—almost one of the family—he remained an outsider and deserved courtesy. His daughter had been rude, and her father must make amends. “Shilong, don't mind her. Wild and silly! The older she grows, the less sense she has.” “You needn't say that, Mr Zhang.” Chen smiled. “If I hadn't come, how would Miss Zhang's tears have stopped?” A’zhu glanced sideways at him and curled her lip. “Thank you so much!” “Enough of that.” Old Zhang grew serious again and returned to the business they must discuss. “Shilong,” he said slowly and earnestly, “you know about our A’zhu. Now I hear Mr Hu has other plans. What exactly is happening? Ask her, and she only cries. You must know. Tell me.”

“I really don't know much,” Chen answered carefully. “But in Hangzhou I heard Mr Hu say something. It seems he and Mrs Hu had a very serious quarrel about this.” “Then...” A’zhu suddenly turned towards him and demanded loudly, “Why didn't you tell me earlier? If you had, I could have asked him long ago. It needn't have come to strangers we've known only a few days passing the message. Isn't that deliberately humiliating our family?” She sounded entirely justified, but it was only her side of the matter. Chen had no obligation to repeat everything he heard. Yet in the present situation she alone could lose her temper; if anyone contradicted her, it would become a quarrel. Chen also had his own purpose and was still less willing to argue. Instead he nodded apologetically. “You must understand. I couldn't go spreading such things.” “Of course! You call him ‘Mr Hu’ and have become his pupil, so naturally you side with your master.” “Enough!” Old Zhang interrupted impatiently. “Chatter, chatter, nothing but quarrelling! At this rate we could talk till dark and get nowhere.” Rebuked, A’zhu knew she had no good answer and dared not speak. But her embarrassment made it impossible to remain. “Talk among yourselves. It's nothing to do with me!” She turned away and went to sit in the rear cabin, listening quietly.

Old Zhang ignored her and told Chen, “I'm in a difficult position. You see things clearly, Shilong, and I want your advice. I'm thinking of taking A’zhu back to Huzhou...” Before he could finish, Chen asked in alarm, “Why? Are you angry with Mr Hu?” “No, no, certainly not!” Old Zhang denied it vigorously. “I was defending him to A’zhu just now. But however poor a man is, he must keep his self-respect. To tell the truth, her mother has entertained some foolish hopes. I've never really felt that I was a silk-firm proprietor. Before, when we expected to become relatives, it didn't matter. Now things have changed. He needn't look after me any longer, and I shouldn't continue accepting his help. Isn't that right?” “No!” Chen answered decisively. “Your view is entirely mistaken.” “Entirely mistaken?” Old Zhang was unconvinced. “Then explain.”

“First, Mr Hu isn't that sort of man. Whatever changes, his desire to help others won't change. Second, in running the silk firm, you're not receiving his help: you're helping him.” “That sounds pleasant, but it isn't true. A man with his abilities has no need of my help.” “The more capable a man is, the more help he needs. An emperor needs eunuchs; a gentleman needs attendants. Only a beggar needs nobody to look after him. Perhaps that isn't the best comparison, but a businessman certainly needs staff. You know Mr Hu's scale of operations. The business he'll build will be enormous. Without people to help, even the greatest ability could achieve nothing single-handed. Take this silk purchase: without you and your wife in Huzhou, and Miss Zhu's help too, could it have gone so smoothly?” Chen spoke with greater emphasis. “Mr Zhang, you mustn't think of yourself as living on someone's favours. It was good fortune that brought us all together. Mr Hu depends on our help, and he won't treat us badly. To speak plainly, even if we merely work for him, we earn our own living through ability and effort. We need feel indebted to nobody.” Old Zhang's thoughts moved slowly and sometimes in the narrow, obstinate fashion people called a one-track mind. Once he had driven himself into a corner, it was hard to get out. Though he recognised the sense in Chen's argument, he could not relinquish his shame at receiving favours. He only shook his head and repeated, “That's not how it is.”

Listening in the rear cabin, A’zhu grew anxious. Every word Chen said appealed to her, and she had another reason of her own. Even if she broke with Hu, the business could continue. Keeping the two matters separate was the best way to prevent gossip. Otherwise people would certainly say that, when Zhang's daughter failed to marry Hu, Zhang lost his position as a silk-firm proprietor too. How humiliating that would sound! She also thought of her mother, whose whole desire had been to leave the boat and establish a home ashore. At last the good fortune Chen spoke of had made it possible. If everything now ended like trying to scoop the moon's reflection out of the water with a bamboo basket, how bitterly disappointed she would be! For these two reasons, A’zhu had to intervene. Entering the middle cabin, she demanded angrily, “Father, are you doing this just to spite me?” Old Zhang stared. “Who would stoop to quarrelling with you?” he replied crossly. “If it isn't spite, why insist on going back to Huzhou? He's made everything perfectly clear.” She pointed to Chen. “What's your reason for refusing to listen?” Old Zhang remained silent and considered. If he insisted on his own way, he would certainly quarrel with his wife, and A’zhu would support her. He could not outargue them, so he must swallow his own feelings. “All right, all right. I'll listen.” A’zhu smiled triumphantly, though she felt some remorse towards her father. But she had been spoiled too long. Instead of saying anything conciliatory, she laughed with disrespectful familiarity. “You won't behave until I give you a scolding!”

“I suppose I'm afraid of you.” Old Zhang smiled bitterly. “You think your arguments are sound, but I know the truth of the matter.” “You've a one-track mind. You always think differently from everyone else.” “A man must know himself,” he answered gravely. “Earning my food through hard work—that I can say. Building up property through ability—I haven't that ability.” “Why worry?” Chen interposed without hesitation. “You have me!” “Did you hear?” A’zhu said with satisfaction. “Everyone wants to help you, and you won't let them. No wonder Mother always says you're... you're as stubborn as an ox! It's really like playing music to an ox.” She covered her mouth and laughed. Watching her, Chen felt his attraction to her grow. Her laughter had its charm, and so did her tears. She was far more interesting than those stiff, quiet, proper girls.

A’zhu went into the rear cabin and fetched a wooden basin. Into it she put the white cotton jacket and trousers her father had changed out of, intending to wash them at the riverside steps. Apart from her refusal to lose an argument, she was a dutiful daughter. Old Zhang, however, would not accept this display of devotion. “Leave those there! I can wash them myself,” he called. “The sun is getting stronger. Hurry back to the You house.” He jerked his chin towards Chen, indicating that he should take her away. A’zhu wondered why her father was so eager to send her off. But after quarrelling with him for so long, she could not bear to oppose him again. She set down the basin and lightly bit her lip, trying to think whether anything needed saying before they parted. “Go on,” Old Zhang urged. “We can talk when you come to Shanghai in a few days.” “Father!” At last she thought of something. “You haven't forgotten what Mother wants you to buy?” “Even if I have, it doesn't matter. We'll discuss it when you reach Shanghai.” Chen accompanied A’zhu ashore and back towards the You house. As they walked, she voiced her puzzlement. He knew why Old Zhang had hurried her away: Hu was due shortly, and her father feared a quarrel if they met. Chen had not meant to tell her, but did so as a test.

She said nothing, merely walking with her head lowered along the quiet riverside. This surprised him. He had expected some response: perhaps she would say her father worried needlessly and she would not quarrel, perhaps complain of Hu's conduct. Her silence left him unable to guess her thoughts. “It's so hot!” she suddenly said, stopping and turning to Chen. “Then rest here a moment.” His suggestion followed naturally, and he pointed beneath a tree with dense shade. There lay a great smooth, flat stone, clearly a resting place for generations of passers-by. It was large enough for two to sit together, but propriety between the sexes obliged Chen to remain standing. One seated and one standing, they looked towards the river, where five or six boys of about ten were splashing about naked. Neither really saw them. Both were searching for something to say. “Oh!” A’zhu suddenly remembered a question. “Why did you call me Miss Zhu just now?” Chen paused, then recalled it. “Take the A' off Miss A’zhu, and you have Miss Zhu. What's wrong with that?” She was very pleased with the form of address. “I thought you'd changed my surname,” she said, smiling. Her charming smile gave him fresh encouragement. The feelings he had stored up for days must now be expressed. Even if it was too soon, he could no longer hold back.

“If I were changing your surname, it wouldn't be to Zhu,” he answered, half in jest. At first A’zhu missed the implication. When she thought it through, she understood. What a rogue! His Mr Hu had barely begun to retreat, and already he was entertaining ideas. She tried to look severe, but immediately discovered that she could not be angry with him. The stern expression relaxed almost as soon as it appeared. Her face had changed from cloud to sunshine faster than the weather in the plum rains, leaving Chen puzzled. But a change for the better was a good sign in any case, and he grew bolder. “A’zhu!” he called, watching her expression. She looked startled and briefly annoyed, then quickly grew calm. In the tone of idle conversation she said, “First it was Miss Zhang, then Miss Zhu, and now you're using my name. You're becoming less respectful by the minute!” “Before, you were in line to become my teacher's wife. I had to call you Miss...” Before he could finish, she demanded, “And now?” “Now it's different, naturally. We're equals. Why shouldn't I call you by your name?” There was some reason in his words, but she still felt uncomfortable. Neither speaking nor smiling, she picked up a handful of pebbles and tossed them into the river one by one, watching each ripple appear, expand, and disappear. Suddenly she felt that everything in life was like that. A small incident could create great distress, but leave it alone and in time it would naturally be forgotten.

“Equals, then,” she said. “I've no wish to become your elder.” Her words arose from her own reflections, but they gave Chen immense relief. To him, his first tentative approach had succeeded. He might as well press on. From today, he meant to turn her heart towards himself. “A’zhu, I want to ask you something. If you find it awkward to answer, you needn't say anything. I'll understand.” Curious by nature, she found this intriguing, though she remained on her guard. She could tell the question would be difficult to answer. Like a child tempted to play with fire, she wanted to reach out but hesitated, unsure what to do. After a little delay, Chen took her silence as permission and put his question. “A’zhu, tell me honestly: do you like me or not?” So that was it! She was astonished. Her head seemed to ring, her face burned, and she broke into a sweat. Answering was impossible. She would have liked to jump into the river simply to escape his gaze. He was looking straight at her. She could only turn away, thinking how shameless he was—and how impossible. If she said nothing, he would surely take it to mean she liked him. Yet she was unwilling to say she did not. Trapped between the two, she grew resentful and stood up. “You! I don't want to talk to you.”

“Not wanting to talk means saying nothing. I understand!” “What do you understand?” Her expression hardened. “Don't make wild guesses.” “I'm not guessing wildly. I understand everything in your heart.” Had she truly felt nothing for him, she could have ignored this. She knew her own mind, and his guesses would not matter. Yet she could not remain indifferent. “How could you understand what's in my heart? You certainly don't!” “Then shall I tell you?” “Go on! You'll be wrong.” “You don't like me at all.” She had expected him to say she did like him. This was so unexpected that she stood speechless. “Well? Was I wrong?” “I can't exactly say you were wrong.” “Then,” Chen immediately pressed, “you do like me?” He had tangled her in his words until she no longer knew what she was saying. However much she resented his teasing, she would not state plainly that she did not like him. “I'm never speaking to you again!” she declared in vexation. “You're even harder to deal with than your teacher!” “No.” Chen's voice was firm. “Neither Mr Hu nor I is hard to deal with.” Sometimes A’zhu listened to a person's tone rather than the meaning of the words. His strong, assured voice gave her a feeling that he could be trusted. Forgetting what she herself had said, she actually came to believe that he was not difficult after all.
