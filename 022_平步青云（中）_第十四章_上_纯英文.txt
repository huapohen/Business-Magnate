CHAPTER FOURTEEN

The important man they were to meet was a Cantonese interpreter named Gu. He had many foreign friends and was particularly familiar with the British. Since Hong Xiuquan had established his kingdom at Jiangning, the British in Shanghai had been trying all manner of schemes. It was these schemes that Hu and You wanted to learn about. You Laowu had extensive connections in Shanghai, and through a succession of inquiries he had discovered that a British firm was already trading with Hong’s army. Two British warships had sailed from Shanghai to Xiaguan. At first the rebels had assumed that the Qing forces had invited them to help fight, and had taken elaborate precautions. But the British had brought an interpreter ashore, and his opening words made it clear that they had come expressly to trade. Their merchandise consisted of firearms and gunpowder, to be exchanged for other goods. The firm had made a handsome profit. The interpreter who had accompanied them was this very man, Gu Yingchun.

This gave Hu Xueyan another idea. He told You that they ought to make Gu’s acquaintance: there would be business to do in both precious objects and arms. By now You followed Hu’s advice in everything. He arranged an introduction through a mutual acquaintance, and the previous day they had met over a banquet with courtesans. It had been agreed then that You would return the hospitality today, with the entire party invited again. In reality, there was only one guest who mattered: Gu Yingchun. The dinner was to be held at Yiqing House on Baoshan Street, an establishment of the prestigious changsan class. You was a favored patron of Yiqing’s Second Miss, the woman who ran the house, so Hu too enjoyed the pleasure of being received as one of the household. They had scarcely taken their seats in the main room and removed their long gowns when an attendant called out that a guest had arrived. Second Miss herself lifted the curtain, revealing Gu Yingchun coming briskly up the steps. “Young Master Gu, you really do honor your friends!” she said with a graceful smile. “The very first to arrive.” “When Brother You gives a dinner, I can hardly be late,” Gu replied. “And when he gives it at your house, I must certainly come early.” “Then I owe this honor to Fifth Master You. Thank you, thank you!”

“Much obliged,” You said, clasping his hands in thanks. Then he gestured toward the inner room. “Would you like to lie down for a while?” “I had my fill of opium before coming, but I wouldn’t mind lying down.” At this, Second Miss called, “Light the lamp!” She took Gu’s serge gown and led him inside. The inner room was her own bedchamber. Its furniture was all of rosewood, except for a large imported brass bed, whose snowy gauze curtains were drawn high. An exquisite set of opium implements had already been laid out on it. After a token show of reluctance, Gu reclined in the place of honor. You offered the opposite place to Hu, and Hu offered it back to You. These were formalities. Hu was the guest, and he had questions for Gu; naturally, he was the one to keep him company.

After two pipefuls and some idle conversation, Second Miss went out to attend to the dinner, while You had other guests to entertain. Hu and Gu were left alone. Hu had already seen that Gu was a man well accustomed to society and would be easy to approach. He began in the tone of one seeking guidance. “Brother Yingchun, I count myself fortunate to have found an old hand to show me the way in the foreign settlement. I shall often have to ask your advice.” “You flatter me,” Gu said with a smile. “I have admired Brother You’s reputation for a long time. He speaks very highly of you, which tells me that you must be a friend worth having. We ought to see more of each other.” “Indeed! All men within the four seas are brothers. And now that the maritime prohibitions have been lifted, it will be harder still to deal with the foreigners unless we Chinese stick together.” “Exactly!” Gu tapped the opium tray with a finger. “Brother Xueyan, that is a thoroughly sensible remark. The truth is that we Chinese ruin one another and let the foreigners reap the benefit.”

There was something in that remark. Hu decided that, with a little care, he could draw Gu out. “These days everyone talks about ‘foreign affairs.’ All the fashionable figures in official circles discuss foreign affairs, and managing them is the most sought-after occupation of all. I have met plenty of those gentlemen, but this is the first time I have heard anyone say what you have just said.” “Hmph!” Gu gave a contemptuous smile at Hu’s mention of these fashionable figures. “They discuss foreign affairs shut up in their own rooms, knowing nothing of the world outside. One sort calls foreigners barbarians and treats them as savages who eat raw flesh and drink blood. The other can scarcely hear the word ‘foreigner’ without wanting to fall on his knees and cry, ‘Your Excellency!’ Discussing and managing foreign affairs in that spirit brings nothing but humiliation upon ourselves.” “You put it very clearly,” Hu said, steering the talk back to its original point. “Both extremes end in our own people ruining one another.” “Precisely!” Gu gestured over the tray with his opium pick. “Those who hate foreigners obstruct everything; those who fear them do nothing but curry favor. Meanwhile, we undermine and push out our own people. Foreigners are quick to see an opening, and they never let one slip. People of that sort are especially detestable.”

His indignation convinced Hu that he had himself been pushed aside and was speaking from experience. Only a mediocrity escaped envy; those who suffered from rivalry and exclusion were generally capable men. Gu spoke coherently, and his views showed considerable foresight. Hu felt that he could judge the man from his conversation. He was short of helpers, particularly of men with this kind of ability. How useful it would be to bring Gu into his service! The thought had scarcely occurred to him before he resolved to act on it, though he must not be too hasty. After a moment’s reflection, he raised a subject he felt certain would interest Gu. “Brother Yingchun!” He suddenly sat up, took a few almonds from a dish and chewed vigorously, which seemed to give his words added force. “There is something I cannot accept. If we ruin one another and let the foreigners gain at our expense, why can’t we unite and win the advantage back?” Gu looked up, poking repeatedly at the tray with his opium pick. At length he said, “Brother Xueyan, no one has ever said that to me before. The last time we took those two warships to Xiaguan to sell arms, the price had been settled and the bargain was almost concluded when a bastard who spoke their language came to see the foreigners. He told them directly that Hong’s army desperately needed guns and powder and had gold, silver and jewels in abundance. That was enough. The foreigners went back on their word, reopened negotiations and more than doubled the price. What a windfall they made! I have resented it ever since. If I could win that advantage back from them, I would do it even without a profit for myself. But you say our own people must unite. How are we to accomplish that? I should like your advice.”

“Now you have put me on the spot,” Hu said. “Things have to be worked out through discussion. I know too little about the foreigners yet to offer a definite answer. Still, if we are to unite, we must make a beginning. Take you, me and Brother You: the three of us could deal with one another in complete sincerity and consult over everything. Whoever has the best idea, we follow it as readily as if it were our own. Then we bring more people together, step by step. The foreigners need not deal with us, but if they do, they will have to listen to us.” “Excellent!” Gu sat up too. “When three men are of one mind, their strength can cut through metal. Let it begin with you, me and Brother You. I’ll give up my job as comprador at the foreign firm.” Comprador was a foreign term for the man who managed a foreign firm’s business dealings. His position stood well above that of an interpreter responsible merely for translating. Hu saw no need for Gu to resign. “Brother Yingchun, between ourselves we should speak frankly. You ought to keep your position at the firm. Otherwise our dealings with foreigners would become inconvenient, and we would lose touch with what was happening among them.” Gu had spoken on impulse. Even without Hu’s reminder, a moment’s reflection would have made him change his mind. Naturally, he agreed at once and repeatedly.

“I was thinking,” Hu said with satisfaction, “how true your saying is about three men of one mind cutting through metal. Each of us has his own field: you have the foreign firms, Brother You the brotherhood world, and I have a few connections in official circles. Put those three together and we ought to get somewhere!” Gu considered this and saw that it was so. Encouraged by Hu, he replied enthusiastically, “Indeed! What a fortunate combination. Bringing those three fields together is no easy matter. Let’s have a proper talk tomorrow, think of something different from everyone else and do some business on a grand scale.” Their understanding made the evening’s entertainment especially enjoyable. You was a generous host, and his guests were all men of the world. Each summoned two or three courtesans; the women came and went amid a clamor of strings and pipes. After nine, someone ordered another round of entertainment, and the festivities continued past midnight. When Hu and You returned to Yuji Silk Warehouse, the Seventh Lady and A’zhu, tired by the day’s events, were already asleep. Old Zhang always went to bed and rose early. Only Chen Shilong was waiting, with a pot of good tea ready for them.

“Brother You, are you sleepy?” Hu asked eagerly. “Not at all. What do you want to talk about?” “A great many things.” Hu turned to Chen. “Shilong, you should listen too. I found you a teacher of the foreigners’ language today.” You understood at once. “You mean Gu Yingchun! How did your talk go?” “It couldn’t have gone better.” Hu recounted their conversation on the opium couch from beginning to end. You was a reserved man who seldom showed his feelings, but even so, his features visibly brightened. The gloom of the past few days seemed quietly to have lifted. “You have a quick mind,” he said slowly and earnestly. “Think what sort of business we could do together with him.”

“There is one opportunity before us already, only…” Hu let the last word linger. “Why, what is so difficult that you can’t say it?” You asked in surprise. “I don’t know whether you have any friendship with ‘Mao, Metal and Knife.’” This was a coded way of writing the surname Liu and referred to Liu Lichuan. You understood immediately. “Nothing worth mentioning.” “Here is what I was thinking. The British are in business, after all. If they can sell guns and cannon to the Taiping forces, they can surely sell them to the government army. I heard at dinner that both the governor-general of Liangjiang and the governor of Jiangsu are worrying over Liu. Their reports play the affair down as a local disturbance, but in reality they mean to send in a large force and crush him in one battle. In that case, guns and powder will be essential. Could we establish a supply office for them in advance, so that when their troops arrive they can go straight into action? If you approve, I’ll go to Suzhou at once. Governor Xu Naizhao of Jiangsu is a fellow Hangzhou man. I can certainly find a way to see him.”

“The idea is good enough. But I can’t take part.” “Because of ‘Ji in a Box’?” Hu asked. The character ji enclosed in a frame made the surname Zhou: he meant Zhou Lichun. You nodded. “Exactly. But you have no ties to him. You could do it.” “Then let it drop. First, whatever we do, we do together. Second, people know how close we are. If it would put you in a difficult position, my doing it would cause the same difficulty.” You was deeply relieved to hear this. In his heart he had wanted Hu to abandon the scheme, but a man of the brotherhood did not cut off another man’s livelihood. It was plainly a profitable venture; he could not ask Hu to give it up. That was why he had said, against his own wishes, “You could do it.”

“There is another course,” Hu continued. “Zhejiang is organizing militia forces. In Huzhou, a gentleman named Zhao Jingxian has taken the lead. He is an exceptionally capable, broad-minded man, and is on good terms with Prefect Wang both publicly and privately. A word from me should settle it.” “Good! Let’s undertake that arms business together.” “There is just one difficulty, and it will depend on you to solve it. British warships cannot reach Huzhou, so delivery would have to be made in Shanghai. The journey from Shanghai to Huzhou might be dangerous. What if the cargo were seized?” “The only dangerous stretch is between Shanghai and Jiaxing. Once it enters Zhejiang, government troops can escort it. Who would dare attack it then? As for the first stretch, I’ll guarantee its safety. Our grain-transport brothers are all idle now, with men and boats ready to hand. Giving them a chance to earn some freight money would be the best possible arrangement.” “Then as far as the two of us are concerned, it is settled. Let’s get on with it. Think again: is there anything else you need me to arrange?” You considered carefully. “Have the Zhejiang authorities send an official letter to our grain transport circuit intendant here, saying that they wish to use the Songjiang grain boats to carry arms. That will put my dealings with the authorities in order.”

“That can certainly be done.” Hu turned to Chen. “I’m afraid I must send you on another journey.” “To Hangzhou or Huzhou?” “Hangzhou first. If Prefect Wang has already returned to his post, go on to Huzhou. You must find him wherever he is. And of course,” Hu added suddenly, “you can take A’zhu home at the same time.” “Very well. When do I leave?” “Within two or three days at most. As soon as I’ve made the arrangements here and written the letter.”

The arrangements were to be made with Gu Yingchun. The next day the three met again in Second Miss’s bedchamber at Yiqing House. Hu explained what they had discussed and asked whether the British would sell guns, cannon and powder to their side. “Why wouldn’t they? They are businessmen. If the price is right, they will sell anything. Tell me what you want, and I can negotiate.” This stumped Hu. “I know nothing about such things. So long as the goods are good, that is enough.” “Quality isn’t the only consideration. There is quantity too. I must have approximate figures before I can negotiate. How many foreign guns, for instance?” “A thousand, surely.” “A thousand!” Gu laughed. “Do you imagine that is a small number? Five hundred foreign guns would be quite a good supply for a militia force. And will you need instructors? Not everyone knows how to fire them. In inexperienced hands they are easily damaged. How will you repair them? All this must be considered beforehand.” Hu clasped his hands. “Brother Yingchun, you know far more about it than I do. Why don’t you draw up a memorandum yourself? That would settle everything.”

Gu readily agreed and began at once. Second Miss personally brought over the brush and inkstone used to write invitations to courtesans and ground a rich supply of ink. There was no blank paper, but since this was only a draft, the backs of the invitation slips would serve. Gu composed with his brush while indulging his opium habit. The prepared pellets were put into the pipe, with a rubber tube attached to the mouthpiece and reaching his lips. After sixteen pipefuls, he was in excellent spirits. His prose flowed freely, without a correction, and he handed the finished draft to Hu. Hu could not compose such a document himself, but he could read it—and judge it shrewdly. A memorandum of this kind had above all to be concise. The best would move a high official with a few sentences. Gu wrote fluently, but fluency had carried him into unnecessary elaboration. He explained the merits of foreign guns and gunpowder from their beginnings. The exposition was good, but somewhat laborious to read. Wang Youling and Zhao Jingxian would certainly finish it, Hu thought. Whether Huang Zonghan would have the patience was another question.

“Excellent!” Hu said, keeping his expression unchanged as he passed the memorandum to You. “There’s no need for me to read it,” You said with a laugh. “I wouldn’t get anything out of it.” “Brother Xueyan,” Gu put in, “this was written in haste. If anything is unsuitable, please say so.” “It is very good. Only, Brother Yingchun, you cannot talk to outsiders in the language of an expert. They won’t understand. I think you may have to sacrifice some of the opening section.” “I understand.” Gu nodded. “So much talk of foreign affairs nowadays consists of people shutting themselves away and spinning their own tales to deceive others. To be frank, very few understand the principles behind the manufacture of guns and cannon. I can shorten those passages. But cutting is one thing and adding another. Where might I usefully say more?” “It is very good already. There are still places where you could say less.” Recognizing this as politeness, Gu replied, “When I undertake something, I mean to do it properly. Besides, we are among friends. Please speak plainly.” “Then let me offer this for your consideration. First, you say, ‘During the Daoguang reign, Britain and France attacked us, and our forces suffered defeat; the essential cause was that swords and spears could not withstand firearms.’ That goes straight to the point, but it could be emphasized further.” “Yes! I had the same thought.”

“There is another point, Brother Yingchun. Could you add a passage along these lines?” Hu proposed saying that the supply of British guns and powder arriving in Shanghai was limited. What was sold to government forces would no longer be available to Hong’s army or other parties elsewhere. Every additional gun bought by the government would therefore mean one fewer in hostile hands, a difference that should count double. In other words, the government would obtain the benefit of two guns for the price of one. “That is a clever calculation,” Gu said, “but unfortunately it does not fit the facts. British arms arrive in one shipment after another, without interruption. Selling to one customer does not prevent them from selling to another.” “Of course. I know that, and you know it better still. But officials do not, and the emperor and the grand councillors in Beijing know even less. All we need is an argument they will like to hear.” Gu looked at You and smiled. You spoke bluntly. “Brother Yingchun, my young uncle here is an expert at these devices. Follow his advice and you won’t go wrong.” “Very well,” Gu said. “I understand. If there is nothing else, I will take this home today, revise it and make a fair copy. I’ll bring it back with a quotation from the firm.”

“Wait a moment,” You interrupted. “How will the quotation be drawn up?” “The usual arrangement is a twenty-percent rebate,” Gu answered. “If you want the price padded, I can discuss that too.” His tone made it plain that he did not favor inflating the quoted price. Hu agreed. They must avoid earning the foreigners’ contempt, and they needed to establish their credit with the Zhejiang authorities. They ought not to invent further devices beyond the twenty-percent rebate. “Quite right,” You said sincerely. “I only mentioned it in case you had overlooked the matter. Since you have considered it, whatever you decide will be all right.”

“But besides guns,” Gu continued, “there are cannon. Should we urge Zhejiang to buy those too?” “Let that wait. There is a man named Gong in Zhejiang who makes cannon.” Gong Zhenlin was a native of Fujian and had once served as deputy magistrate of Jiaxing. By the closing years of the Daoguang reign, he was directing Zhejiang’s cannon works. Since the middle of the Ming dynasty, the so-called Red-Robed General cannon had been copied by casting iron in sand molds. Gong had devised iron molds for casting cannon and had written an illustrated account of them. He had also written an Illustrated Account of a New Pivoted Gun Carriage, making significant improvements in cannon-making technology. His son, Gong Zhitang, had learned his craft from him. Both father and son were highly valued by Zhejiang’s senior officials. “Naturally, our locally made cannon firing clusters of shot cannot match the Western guns with shells that burst on impact. But we mustn’t say so! The men at the cannon works would think we were trying to take away their livelihood. They would find fault with everything, and we might end up unable to conclude even the purchase of guns.” “Brother Xueyan,” Gu said with mingled admiration and feeling, “you understand human affairs thoroughly. You have laid bare all the faults of official life.” “Business and official life are alike in that respect. People in the same trade envy one another. If only they could put envy aside, anything could be accomplished.”

Both Gu and You thought this well said, and their feelings toward one another grew warmer still. Over leisurely drinks at Yiqing House they discussed many plans for expanding their business. It was midnight before they parted, agreeing to meet there again the following afternoon. Gu left, while You stayed with Second Miss. Since there was more to discuss, Hu borrowed a bed at the house without engaging a companion. You had news acquired between their parting at noon and their meeting again at Yiqing House. Liu Lichuan had reportedly established contact with the British. The British intended to build a wall around the foreign settlement and allow neither government troops to be stationed inside nor government forces to pass through. Yet they planned to open Chenjia Wooden Bridge so that Liu could receive supplies of arms and food. “If that is so, Shanghai won’t be recovered for another six months or a year. Can we carry on our silk business? We had better consider it now.”

“We must think carefully,” Hu said. “Shanghai’s customs revenue is the lifeblood of Liangjiang. They cannot let the British have their own way indefinitely. There must be a way of dealing with them.” “I have heard something about that too,” You replied. “Governor-General Yiliang has long been preparing to prohibit trade between the interior and the foreign settlement because the foreigners are helping the rebels. Once supplies are cut off, what prospects will we have in Shanghai?” “There are two sides to that. Cutting off supplies will raise prices, which helps us. But without goods, even the highest prices are useless to us. We can carry on business as usual if we can avoid the disadvantageous side.” “How can we avoid it? We can’t!” There was one way: smuggling. With You’s influence over the waterways and his responsive network, it would not be difficult. But Hu did not dare undertake something illegal. Everything was going well at present; there was no need to break the law. The thought passed through his mind, and he swallowed the words before uttering them. “Young Uncle, I can see only one course,” You said. “Raise as much ready cash as possible, buy goods already in Shanghai, hold them for a while and then sell. As for other business besides arms, we can discuss that too. Best of all would be something our grain-transport brothers could work at together. It would give everyone a hard-earned living and keep them from getting into trouble through idleness.” Hu heard the concealed anxiety behind these words about the brotherhood’s worsening livelihood. They were now close friends, sharing good fortune and bad; he ought to help You bear his worries. He asked about the difficulties facing the Songjiang brotherhood and what might be done. The discussion grew so absorbing that they did not go to bed until dawn.

Hu slept until nearly noon, when one of the senior female attendants at Yiqing House woke him to say that he had a visitor. It was Chen Shilong, bringing a letter delivered by a special messenger from Wang Youling. Hu opened it and read carefully. The affair of the grain-tax disturbances in Xincheng had been brought to a successful conclusion. Ji Heling had fulfilled their expectations: working with local gentlemen, he had devised a plan to capture the principal offenders, who had been sent to Hangzhou for trial and punishment. After the good news came the question of the winter grain shipment. Because Shanghai had fallen, Zhejiang’s tribute rice would now be shipped out to sea through Liuhe. The extra complications meant starting a month early, and the collection of the grain tax would also have to be advanced. Wang needed to hurry back to Huzhou. Shanghai’s fall had caused alarm throughout Zhejiang, and militia organization was being pressed forward everywhere, Huzhou included. Although Zhao Jingxian was directing the work, the local official was responsible for the defense of his territory and could not leave it unattended. Yet Wang could not extricate himself from his duties at the Sea Transport Bureau and could not be in two places at once. He hoped that Hu would return to Zhejiang at all costs: there were many things they needed to discuss in person. Hu finished the letter pleased, troubled and puzzled. He was pleased by the success at Xincheng, troubled because he too had more than he could attend to, and puzzled because there was no mention of the reward Ji Heling deserved. What should he do? He collected his thoughts and decided to go back, but not empty-handed. There were two things he could first arrange for Wang. Having made up his mind, he told Chen, “Go back and pack. We leave tomorrow. A’zhu will come with us.”

He hurried through his washing and went to consult You. At the mention of sending the tribute rice out through Liuhe, You frowned. “That will cause a great deal of trouble.” “Why?” “Liuhe lies north of Jiading.” “Ah!” Hu exclaimed. Sending the grain boats to Liuhe by way of Qingpu and Jiading would be impossible. “Then which way can they go?” “They must make a detour.” Dipping a finger in clear tea, You traced the route on the table. “North from Jiaxing, through Wujiang, Kunshan and Taicang to Liuhe.” “That is a considerable detour. Is Taicang near Jiading?” “Yes, forty or fifty li northwest of it.” You gave Hu a significant look: they must beware of Zhou Lichun seizing the grain. Hu understood. An idea struck him, and he smiled. “Brother You, here is business for you. Turn your connections into money! How would you like the contract to carry Zhejiang’s winter tribute grain over the final stretch to Liuhe?”

It was a natural and excellent proposal. Yet it had come so easily that You could scarcely believe such good fortune existed; for a moment he did not know what to say. “Well?” Hu urged. “I am confident about this. I can settle it myself. One word from you, and it is arranged.” “I don’t know whether the other side will honor my connections,” You said hesitantly. That was the decisive point, the very basis of turning friendship into money. “Brother You,” Hu replied, “I can advise you on everything else. But only you can judge that. I cannot speak for you.” “Yes.” You nodded solemnly. “That is for me to decide. If I undertake it, I must shoulder the responsibility. I cannot do anything foolish that would involve you and Prefect Wang in trouble. To be safe, I can only agree provisionally. There is still time. I’ll send someone to sound out our friend Zhou. If his response is unfavorable, I’ll notify you at once, and we will treat the whole proposal as though it had never been made. What do you think?” “Whatever you say. Let us assume it will succeed and first discuss the arrangements.” They agreed that, once Zhejiang’s grain boats reached Wujiang, You would take over the cargo and carry it onward to the sea outlet at Liuhe. Since the contract involved risk, the freight rate could not follow the ordinary scale. Hu promised to argue for the best possible terms: every extra payment would help.

With the first matter settled, Hu turned to the second, which required a visit to Gu. From his assessment of the situation, the Zhejiang authorities would certainly buy foreign guns. Indeed, the alarm caused by Shanghai’s fall and the urgent need to strengthen defenses would make their demand especially pressing. He could therefore proceed on two fronts: take the memorandum back and urge a large official purchase, while also bringing guns ready for use to Hangzhou. If the militia did not buy them, he could persuade Wang to purchase them for his personal guard detachment. When Hu reached Gu’s house, he found him dressed and ready for their appointment at Yiqing House. After hearing Hu’s purpose, Gu considered for a moment and asked, “How many do you want?” “Two hundred to begin with. I have ten thousand taels with me.” “There are two hundred in stock. How will you transport them? You need official papers to move arms, or they are certain to be detained at a checkpoint.” “Yes. I have discussed it with Brother You. Getting them from Shanghai to Songjiang will cause no trouble. As soon as I reach Hangzhou, I’ll obtain the official documents and send to meet the shipment. That will avoid any delay.” “Good! I’ll take you to see the foreigner now, and we can negotiate the price in person.” He took Hu by the arm and started out.

“Wait, wait!” Hu said with a nervous smile. “This is my first time dealing with foreigners.” “What are you afraid of?” Gu interrupted. “Foreigners are human too. They are not wild savages. They are quite civilized.” “I don’t mean whether they are savage or civilized. What foreign rules of conduct should I know? Tell me beforehand so I don’t make a fool of myself.” “There is scarcely time to explain everything. We clasp our hands and bow; they shake hands—the right hand. Take off your hat indoors. They show respect to women, so stand when you see a foreign lady. Mrs Hudson is very hospitable and likes to ask Chinese visitors all sorts of questions. Their customs do not insist on a strict separation between men and women. Don’t be surprised.” “That sounds pleasant,” Hu said, laughing. “Like the Seventh Lady in the You household.” “Who do you mean?” “An irrelevant joke. Never mind me.” Hu waved it aside. “Let’s go.”

Two sedan chairs took them to a small Western house at Nicheng Bridge. They alighted, sent in their visiting cards and were shown into the drawing room. The servant, dressed in a long blue cotton gown, offered neither tea nor tobacco; he simply closed the door and went away. Before long an inner door opened, and Hudson, his face covered in a yellow beard, strode in. Hu had resolved to copy Gu exactly. When Gu rose, he rose; when Gu shook hands, he shook hands. But when conversation began, Hu could do nothing except watch their expressions. Those expressions were far from reassuring. The foreigner kept shrugging and spreading his hands, while Gu looked increasingly angry. Their voices gradually rose: plainly they were arguing. “Outrageous!” Gu turned to Hu in fury. “He told me clearly that business was business: if a customer had money, he would sell anything he was able to sell. Now he goes back on it. He says he has an agreement with the Long-Haired rebels and cannot sell to government troops after selling to them. I asked why he hadn’t said so before. He claims his consul only recently informed him of it. He says they are subject to their authorities just as we are to ours and cannot act as they please. Isn’t that infuriating?” “Wait,” Hu said. “What does he mean by an agreement? Something like a treaty?” “Roughly speaking, yes.” “Then it makes no sense. Our government made a commercial treaty with Britain, opening five ports to trade. Yet we cannot trade with him, while rebels whom our government is fighting can! What sort of reasoning is that?”

Gu was delighted. “Exactly! That is a very good argument. Let me put it to him.” He turned back to negotiate. Hu could not understand the words, but he could hear the tone and read the expressions. Gu spoke with the confidence of a man who had right on his side; Hudson seemed at a loss for an answer. At last the foreigner nodded, and Gu turned to Hu with a smile. “We have done it! He has agreed to take the matter up with his consul. I suspect there may be no agreement at all. Our order is small and the rebels’ orders are large; he is merely afraid of losing the greater profit for the smaller.” “Tell him that our business is small now but will be large in the future. He should take the long view. If he cultivates our friendship today, we can continue dealing with him tomorrow.” Gu translated. Hudson nodded repeatedly and kept looking at Hu, plainly approving of what he had said. “Brother Xueyan, he finds your remarks very interesting and would like to make a friend of you. He wants to invite you for a drink. What do you say?” “Of course. We should get better acquainted. Let us be the hosts.” “There is no need. Let him entertain us this time. We can return the invitation once the business is settled.”

When Gu conveyed their acceptance, Hudson went to a corner and pulled a silk bell cord in the bright yellow reserved for imperial use in China. A jangling sounded outside, and the servant returned. Hu learned something: foreigners summoned servants by ringing a bell instead of drawing out a loud cry of “Come!” Hudson ordered the carriage, took the reins himself and drove them to a Western drinking establishment. As Hu entered, he felt dizzy. Mirrors on every side multiplied the people, lamps, tables and chairs without end. He quickly grasped a chair and steadied himself. “Let’s sit here,” Gu called to Hudson, and each drew up a chair. Hu sat down too. Looking up, he caught the reflection of a beautiful figure in a mirror. He turned and saw a blonde, blue-eyed woman with a snow-white complexion, lovely features and a slender waist. Her smile revealed white teeth as she asked a question. Hudson gave her an order, and she turned away. Hu could hardly stare openly after her, so he watched her in the mirror. He had seen few Western women, and now found it difficult to take his eyes from the reflection. Her vigorous yet graceful stride reminded him of the swaying walk of young Manchu women in their flowerpot-soled shoes. Each had its own charm; both seemed more attractive to him than Han women with tiny bound feet, whose ideal was to walk without disturbing the folds of their skirts. While he was thinking this, the waitress reappeared in the mirror carrying a silver tray. On it were a bottle of liquor resembling Bamboo Leaf Green in color, three crystal glasses and a bottle of cold water. Once she had set them down, Hudson placed three silver dollars on the tray.

“This liquor isn’t cheap,” Hu remarked. “A silver dollar is worth seven qian and two fen. Three dollars come to more than two taels and one qian.” “Yes, freight is expensive,” Gu replied. He helped Hudson pour the liquor and add cold water; then they raised their glasses to one another. “What?” Hu asked. “We drink now? Wine without dishes?” “A novice in foreign ways!” Gu teased him with a new expression current in the settlement. “Foreigners drink without food.” “Well, that is a first for me.” Hu took a sip. The flavor was not bad, but diluted with water it lacked strength. He put down the glass. “Let’s talk business,” Gu said. After speaking with Hudson, he turned back. “He asks when you need the goods.” “They must be dispatched within three days at most.” “Then the price will be different. He has a shipment already promised to a rebel named Luo at Zhenjiang. He can let you have it first, but it will be thirty taels a gun. If you can wait half a month, another shipment will arrive from Britain, and those would be only twenty taels each.” “Thirty it is. The goods must be good.”

Gu nodded and spoke to Hudson again. With him interpreting between them, they soon settled every detail: two hundred guns and ten thousand rounds of ammunition, at a total price of eleven thousand taels. After a twenty-percent rebate, the sum payable was eight thousand eight hundred. Hudson would also send a gunsmith with the shipment to Zhejiang to attend to the weapons, for a fee of two hundred taels. “I have the money with me. Shall I pay now?” “No need,” Gu replied. “We can complete the formalities at his firm tomorrow.” “Then I’ll leave it to you.” Hu took out his banknotes and handed them over. “Here are ten thousand taels. The surplus is yours.” “There is no need for that!” Gu hastily waved the suggestion aside. “We are all in this together. The rebate goes into our common accounts. We can settle our shares later.” “Quite right. Then consider the remaining thousand taels a deposit you are holding for me.”

Gu nodded, pointed to the notes and spoke to Hudson, whose face broke into a broad smile. He said a great deal very quickly. According to Gu, Hudson found doing business with Hu so agreeable that he wished to present him with one of the latest breech-loading, seven-shot guns as an additional mark of esteem. “Please thank him for me,” Hu said. “And ask whether he could sell me a few of those—what are they called?—seven-shot breech-loaders. I should like to take some home as gifts.” This was less easy. Hudson had spent several years in China and knew how fond many people were of small favors. He was keeping a few fine guns for cultivating useful relationships and did not want to sell them. In the end, however, he yielded and agreed to spare two for Hu, at one hundred and fifty taels apiece, which he said was exactly the cost price. Each would come with a hundred rounds of ammunition, also given free.

To celebrate this additional little transaction, Hudson proposed opening a bottle of champagne. Gu doubted that Hu would care for a light wine with a somewhat sour taste. Champagne was expensive, but putting Hudson to expense was the lesser concern. If Hu tasted it, frowned, shook his head and refused another sip, that would be distinctly rude. Better to decline. He therefore apologized on Hu’s behalf, saying that Hu was unaccustomed to foreign liquor and could not accept the kind offer. Hudson asked in return whether Hu was unable to drink. On learning that he had a considerable capacity, Hudson was astonished. The bottle on the table came from Scotland, he said; it was not only a celebrated brand but had been aged for a long time. Why would Hu not drink it? He knew many Chinese, and all who enjoyed liquor appreciated Scotch. What made Hu different? If Hu did not mind, he would very much like to know. Gu meant to give an evasive answer and let the matter pass. But Hu saw Hudson repeatedly indicating the bottle and his glass while talking at length. Guessing the subject, he asked about it, and Gu naturally told him the truth. “In eating and drinking, one man likes radishes and another likes greens. It is like women: to a lover, his beloved is as beautiful as Xi Shi. There is no explaining tastes.” Gu translated this, and Hudson nodded emphatically. Saying that tastes could not be explained was itself an explanation. There were many kinds of foreign liquor, he added. If Hu did not like British liquor, perhaps he would enjoy French brandy. He beckoned to the waitress and named a particular celebrated brand.

Brandy required a different glass: short-stemmed, broad-mouthed and round-bowled. Only a little was poured, without water. Through Gu, Hudson taught Hu how to drink it. He should cradle the glass in both hands and swirl it gently; as warmth from his palms entered the liquor, its fragrance would rise and its mellow flavor become more apparent. Hu followed these directions and found them justified. Hudson told Gu how pleased he was to have found a liquor Hu enjoyed. He then described brandy-making in great detail, from harvesting grapes to bottling the finished drink. Finally, he pointed to a word on the label and pronounced it: “Cognac.” When choosing brandy, he said, one should always look for this word. It was a place-name, the finest liquor-producing district in France. “I understand!” Hu told Gu. “Just like our yellow wine: it has to be Shaoxing to be authentic.” “Exactly.”

“Now…” Hudson went on to tell Gu that his firm had just obtained the agency for this French liquor, and he hoped Hu would introduce customers. “So he is promoting his merchandise!” Hu laughed. “No wonder he is so enthusiastic. But I don’t understand: what does an agency mean?” “He has the exclusive right to sell it,” Gu explained. “He is the general distributor of this liquor in China and takes a commission on the sales. That is what an agency means.” Hu understood at once. Such a business, earning its return from arranging sales, depended entirely on reputation and credit. A firm needed a respected name, sound credit, the ability to sell the goods and collect payment, and accounts settled punctually without a penny left owing. Only then would another company grant it an agency. He thought he might very well do the same himself. But that was for the future, beyond any immediate prospect, so he did not pursue the subject.

The brandy tasted excellent, but Hu was still unused to drinking without food and took little. To acknowledge Hudson’s hospitality, and to supply himself with gifts, he decided to buy five cases to take home. Hudson was a shrewd businessman too and immediately offered a generous discount. His aim was to promote the product. Hangzhou was Zhejiang’s provincial capital, with every kind of government office except that of a governor-general. It was also the starting point of the canal and a flourishing commercial center. Hudson saw considerable potential in the port and hoped Hu would help sell there the various foreign goods for which his firm held agencies. When Gu explained this, Hu naturally welcomed the proposal. But he spoke frankly to Gu: he already had far too much official and commercial business on his hands to give Hudson a definite answer. How should they put that? “Then simply turn him down.” Hu felt, however, that this was an opportunity too valuable to discard. Better to delay in such a way that Hudson would keep the opportunity for him instead of approaching someone else. An idea occurred to him. “Tell him this: ordinarily I could agree at once to organize matters for him in Hangzhou. But conditions are unsettled. The route from Shanghai to Hangzhou may be cut, interrupting supplies and leaving us to pay expenses for nothing. As soon as the situation becomes a little more stable, I will get to work for him.” Hudson thought this a prudent judgment and agreed to wait. Meanwhile, he asked Hu to investigate the Hangzhou market for foreign goods and make preliminary preparations. He promised Hu the first opportunity to take up any future agency or distribution arrangement.

Their business discussions ended to everyone’s satisfaction. Gu and Hu took their leave and walked back to Yiqing House. They talked on the way, but no longer about business. “Tell me,” Hu asked, “what exactly is the position of that foreign woman in the drinking place?” “She sells liquor. What else?” Gu laughed. “What did you imagine she sold?” Hu smiled without answering. After a while he said regretfully, “It is a pity I don’t understand their language. Otherwise it would surely be interesting to talk with her.” “I wouldn’t have expected you to take a fancy to a foreign woman,” Gu said, laughing too. “A woman is a woman. Chinese or foreign makes no difference, so long as she is attractive.” “Give it time,” Gu replied. “Once you have lived in Shanghai longer, you will have opportunities to make friends with foreign women.”
