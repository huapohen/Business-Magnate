CHAPTER EIGHTEEN

They left Yiyuan and entered their sedan chairs. Chen Shilong gave the bearers a place-name Hu had never heard. After winding through several long lanes, they reached a quiet, out-of-the-way spot near the city wall. Hu alighted before a neat stone-gated house. Both black lacquered doors stood open, giving a view straight through to a brightly lit hall full of moving figures. Palace lanterns hung beneath the eaves, and red candles burned inside. There seemed to be some celebration. “Where are we?” Hu asked. “At a house of mine,” Yu replied. An idea struck Hu. “Brother Yu, is it your birthday? Why didn’t you tell me?” Yu smiled and nodded. “Go in and you will see.” Inside were the two secretaries, Yang and Qin, Huang Yi, Old Zhang, and friends from the banks whom Hu knew. As they entered, everyone clasped hands and offered congratulations. Hu assumed these were meant for Yu and had nothing to do with him. He stepped quietly aside to inspect the house, thinking that, if it was spacious and favorably situated, he might discuss buying it or taking possession under a redeemable property pledge and establish a home in Huzhou. Then he noticed something odd. Two red candles shone on the central table, which was draped in red. It was certainly a celebration, but a birthday should have cakes, longevity peaches, candles and noodles, with an image of the venerable God of Longevity. There were no peaches or noodles. Instead, a five-colored silk tapestry depicted the Two Immortals of Harmony. This was a wedding celebration. “Why!” Hu rubbed the back of his head. “This is like trying to reach the head of a twelve-foot guardian deity—I can make neither head nor tail of it! What is going on?”

The room burst into laughter. Through it came a woman: A’zhu’s mother, her hair smoothly dressed and adorned with a red flower, wearing a satin jacket and gauze skirt. Hu had never seen her so splendidly attired and stared again. “Mr Hu!” she said, smiling. “Congratulations!” At last he understood. Turning to the company, he apologized. “Forgive me! So it was me you were congratulating. How discourteous I have been!” He clasped his hands repeatedly, provoking another burst of laughter. Then a new question occurred to him. He caught Yu by the arm. “Where is Sister-in-Law Yu?” “Probably inside with the bride.” “Exactly!” A’zhu’s mother smiled delightedly. “Such a fine young woman! You are fortunate, Mr Hu. Come and see her.” Everyone surged toward the inner rooms to look at Hu’s new concubine. Only Hu himself hesitated. What a strange affair: everyone knew he was taking a concubine except the man concerned! Now that the secret was out, he must at least learn the details, or he would make a fool of himself at every turn.

He fell back a step or two and detained Chen. “Tell me what has happened.” “Uncle Four has arranged everything. All you need do is be the bridegroom.” Those few words explained it. Yu had organized the whole scene. Encouraging Hu to visit Nanxun had been a stratagem to lure the tiger away from its mountain, giving Yu two days to arrange the marriage. Influential as he was in Huzhou, the time had been desperately short. It was almost like cooking raw rice in the time it took to drink a cup of tea—hard to imagine. Liu Bucai was also difficult to deal with. To bring him to terms in two days, Yu had probably used threats and inducements, persuasion and pressure together. Who knew how much effort it had cost? Hu was deeply moved by such generosity. Money was a small matter. What was rare was the thought and effort behind it. There was real savor in friendship carried to such lengths.

“Hey! What are you doing?” Yu called back. This brought Hu to himself. Looking up, he saw A’qi coming joyfully from a room on the east side, dressed in a red jacket and skirt, heavily made up and glittering with pearls and jade. Yu was particularly happy and began by teasing her. “Well!” He looked her up and down. “You look just like the bride yourself.” Ignoring him, she addressed Hu by a new title. “Brother Hu! How will you thank me?” she asked triumphantly. “I am deeply obliged.” Hu clasped his hands. “I shall burn incense morning and evening and pray that you soon bear a fine son.” This auspicious wish made her smile still more broadly. Assuming the role of hostess, she attended to every guest with easy social skill, until Yu grew impatient. “Enough! The proper ceremony comes first. Bring her out to pay her respects.” When a concubine entered a household, custom required only that she kowtow to its master and mistress. The mistress was absent, leaving Hu alone. He thought the ceremony quite unnecessary, but the guests unanimously insisted that propriety must be observed and pressed him into the principal armchair. The eastern curtain lifted. A’qi, acting as attendant to the bride, brought Furong out. She bowed to the floor and softly addressed him as “Master.” Both Furong and Hu felt embarrassed, while the guests were delighted. Secretaries Yang and Qin, in particular, appraised her appearance without restraint. A’zhu’s mother came to the rescue by urging everyone to take their places at dinner.

There was only one banquet table, set in the hall, and all those seated at it were men. Finger-guessing drinking games and other revelry continued until the second watch. The guests then took their leave, except for Yu and Chen. “Come inside,” Yu said, taking Hu by the arm. “See your new home. A’qi arranged it all. I hope it pleases you.” “Wait, Brother Yu! You have taken such trouble that I don’t know how to thank you. How much have you advanced for me altogether?” “Why discuss accounts now? We can do that tomorrow.” “Very well. But there is something I don’t understand. What about her uncle?” “Liu Bucai?” Yu paused. “How could he have the face to come?” An uncle could scarcely attend a wedding feast at which his niece became a concubine. Hu reflected that Liu evidently retained some sense of dignity. “He has not come today,” Yu added, “but he will appear sooner or later. He is troublesome. I’ll tell you about him tomorrow.” Hu wanted to explain his plan to win Liu over as an assistant, but Yu would not let him linger and kept urging him onward. He had to put the uncle aside and go to see the niece.

The bridal chamber dazzled him. Everywhere he looked were brilliant reds and greens. In the freshly papered room, the furniture, utensils, bed curtains and bedding were all new. To Hu, of course, the newest thing of all was Furong herself. She was eating with A’zhu’s mother and A’qi. At the sound of footsteps she rose, looking rather at a loss. Hu felt awkward too. “Please sit. Go on eating. I am only looking round.” Searching for something to say, he noticed a sheet of tiger-patterned paper mounted in red silk on the wall. Huang Yi had written the calligraphy. Hu had little literary learning, but could make out its phrasing because he knew the poem: it was the verse on his fortune slip. Only two characters in the final line had changed. Instead of “Where does the beauty gather lotus blooms?” Huang had written, “Upon the river the beauty gathers lotus blooms.” Hu smiled and looked back at Chen, who smiled too. Plainly this was a trick the two of them had devised. The others did not understand their amusement. Impatient A’qi asked first, and Chen laughingly told how Hu had drawn the slip and been disappointed by the words “where does.” Everyone found the story entertaining, especially Furong. As she listened, she repeatedly raised her eyes to look at Hu. Each glance seemed to him as bright as a flash of light. “Then there is no more to say!” A’qi told her. “You were born to join the Hu family.” “Indeed, a match ordained beforehand,” Yu agreed. “I have never arranged anything so smoothly.”

“Even so, we owe it to you both,” Hu said. “Let me borrow another’s flowers to make an offering and drink a toast to Brother Yu and his wife.” Quick-handed A’zhu’s mother immediately brought two cups, one for A’qi and one for Yu. “Wait! That is not the way,” A’qi directed. “You had better all sit down first.” She arranged the seats herself. Hu and Furong took the two places of honor; A’zhu’s mother and Chen faced one another on the east and west. “Old man,” A’qi told Yu, “we will sit at the lower end and act as hosts.” Everyone sat except Furong, who hesitated as though afraid that sitting beside her master would overstep her rank. Hu took her hand without hesitation, and she blushed and sat. “First drink the bridal cross-cup toast, then thank your matchmakers together.” From this beginning A’qi devised one amusement after another. Laughter never ceased, and she herself became drunk. Hu had drunk a good deal too, but his mind was clear. He feared that A’qi might disgrace herself. If she said something improper to Chen, the damage might be irreparable. He kept signaling to A’zhu’s mother to intervene.

“We ought to leave and let the newlyweds retire early,” A’zhu’s mother said. Turning round, she asked, “Why, where is Shilong?” Chen had seen the danger and slipped away long before. Hu grew anxious that pursuing the question would draw A’qi’s attention. He hurriedly changed the subject. “Sister-in-Law Yu has had quite a lot. Help her lie down for a while.” Before he finished, A’qi vomited all over the floor, leaving the brand-new bridal chamber in a dreadful mess. Yu sighed repeatedly in exasperation. Hu naturally took no offense. Furong was even more attentive: forgetting her embarrassment and reserve, and heedless of her splendid clothes, she personally cared for A’qi while directing a newly engaged maid and the young maid she had brought with her to clean up. Once she had been sick, A’qi sobered. She was terribly ashamed and apologized over and over. Yu scolded her for making an exhibition of herself, while the others intervened to soothe them. The commotion was considerable. Eventually the visitors departed. Furong saw them to the inner gate, and Hu accompanied them to the street. Before Yu entered his chair, Hu took his hand. “Brother Yu, you have set me a problem. I fear I shall have to stay in Huzhou a few more days.” Yu smiled without answering. Chen spoke instead. “Mr Hu, if there is business in Hangzhou, I can go for you.” “Certainly!” A’zhu’s mother agreed. “Send Shilong wherever he is needed.” “Let me think. We will discuss it tomorrow.”

Hu returned and closed the main gate himself. In the hall the wedding candles still burned, their glowing red light giving everything a dreamlike air. “It really is like a dream,” he murmured, sitting in a new chair. Looking at the armrest, he recognized the wood known in Guangdong as suanzhi rosewood. The design was Cantonese too. Finding a whole new suite of such furniture would be difficult even in Hangzhou, let alone Huzhou. Yu’s painstaking care deeply moved him. From Yu he thought of A’qi, then of Old Zhang and his wife, daughter and son-in-law-to-be, of Huang Yi and the two secretaries, and finally of the day’s gathering. His feelings rose. There were, after all, more good people than bad in the world. Today alone proved it. They had not merely brought about his happiness; they had taken elaborate pains to send him away beforehand so that it would come as an unexpected delight. This was affection, not favor calculated from advantage. While he sat absorbed, savoring the thought, someone called softly, “Master.” He turned. Furong stood there holding a covered teacup. She had removed her evening makeup; her lips were red and her teeth white, and her glossy black hair was dressed in a new court-style bun. Smiling shyly before him, with a different depth of feeling in her eyes, she stirred an excitement he had never known. He swallowed twice to moisten his dry throat before he could speak.

“Thank you.” He accepted the cup with one hand and gently held her left arm with the other. “Let us sit outside a little longer before going in,” Furong said. “I have burned some incense in there, but the smell from earlier may not have cleared yet.” “Sister-in-Law Yu is very amusing. Have you known each other long?” “Only two years. After she married Master Yu, we met at a gathering.” Furong smiled and stopped. “What happened? Another amusing incident?” “No.” She answered calmly. “The others scarcely spoke to her that day, probably because they disapproved of her background. I did not know who she was. I only thought her frank and lively and talked with her for a long time. That is how we became friends.” “I see.” Hu admired her attitude. Remembering how she had cared for A’qi without shrinking from the mess, he congratulated himself on having taken such a kind and capable woman into his household. His view of Furong changed. A man saw many distinctions between wife and concubine, the chief one expressed in the saying that a wife was chosen for virtue, a concubine for beauty. Hu himself had been captivated by Furong’s clear eyes and slender waist. Though she was now composed and dignified, her graceful figure could still draw a man like a moth to a flame, willing to perish without regret. But to think of her only that way would be a waste. It was like the British gold coin attached to his watch chain. A sovereign was worth more than other foreign coins, but used as an unusual and attractive ornament, it had a value beyond its silver equivalent. To treat it merely as money would diminish it. Finding a beautiful concubine like Furong was not so difficult; finding beauty joined with virtue was a rare good fortune that could not be commanded. She deserved special care. His mood changed again. A moment before, he had been absorbed in excitement and thoughts of leading her behind the gauze bed curtains. Now he felt a contented joy, as though contemplating a rare flower or savoring mellow wine, to be enjoyed slowly.

He said nothing, only blinking or smiling from time to time. Furong wanted to know what gave him such pleasure, but the sense of being strangers was still strong, and she could only observe proper courtesy. “Shall we go inside?” she ventured. “Certainly. After you.” His politeness made her more constrained. Stepping back, she said, “Please go first, Master. I have things to attend to.” Her task was to fulfill a housewife’s responsibilities. She extinguished the candles, closed the doors and windows, and checked the kitchen before returning to the bridal chamber. Hu was drinking alone. Four small dishes and a pot of wine had been prepared. As he raised his cup and recalled the day, countless questions pressed for answers. Seeing her immediately begin arranging the bedding, he called, “Furong, come here. Let us talk first.” “Yes, of course.” She came over and sat, moving a dish of ham within his reach and filling his cup. He held it to her lips; she took a sip. He offered her a slice of ham, and she ate that too.

“Do you know what a fool I made of myself today?” It was a good beginning, seeming to make them familiar at once. She looked at him with interested concern. “How?” “When I came in with Yu, everyone said ‘Congratulations.’ I knew nothing and thought it was his birthday and they were congratulating him. Imagine such a thing!” Furong burst into laughter, then quickly pressed her lips together and tried to look serious. “Did you really know nothing beforehand?” “Nothing at all. To keep it from me, they even sent me on a visit to Nanxun.” “Then…” She hesitated, looking intently at him. “Was taking me not your wish?” “How could you think that?” Hu hastened to explain. “I could not have wished for anything better.” She nodded, and her expression softened. “I never expected it either,” she said with lowered eyes. “I am rather afraid.” “Afraid of what?” “That I am clumsy and do not know how to speak properly, and will eventually displease the old mistress and your wife.” “That will never happen. You may be completely at ease.” At this reassurance, she smiled immediately. The smile was faint but seemed rooted deep. She was one of those naturally compelling women whose slightest expression made a profound impression. “Sister-in-Law Yu has probably told you about me,” Hu said. “What did she say?” “A great deal.” Furong compressed it all into one sentence. “In short, she urged me to enter your household.” “And you? Were you willing?” This seemed difficult to answer. After a long silence, she lowered her eyes and said, “It is the fate I was born to.” Her voice held infinite sadness, suggesting many hidden hardships. Hu felt tender concern and wanted to ask, yet feared touching some painful secret she did not wish to tell. He hesitated, unsure how to begin.

Thinking of her relatives gave him a way forward. “Don’t you have a younger brother? Why wasn’t he here today?” “He is with my uncle.” Furong raised her head and spoke earnestly. “I wanted to ask your wishes before making arrangements for him.” “What arrangements had you in mind? Sister-in-Law Yu told me from the beginning that you wanted him with you. You need not ask me. Do as you wish. Raising and educating him will naturally be my responsibility.” At those last words, two bright tears rolled from her constantly blinking eyes. She bit her lip and forced the tears back. “Even my parents in the next world would be grateful.” “Don’t say that.” Hu handed her a handkerchief. “I want to help not only your brother but your uncle too. Now that we are one family, I must look after them if I can. In time you will learn what I am like.” “I know. Sister A’qi told me.” Furong sighed. “I don’t know what to say.” “I have heard that your uncle is nicknamed Liu the Good-for-Nothing. That does not matter. Others may not dare employ him; I do. My only concern is whether he has ability.” Then he abruptly added, “I know nothing of your family’s circumstances.” She nodded. “Of course I must tell you.”

The Lius had been a business family too. Furong’s grandfather owned a large medicinal-goods shop called Liu Jingde Hall. He had three sons. The eldest was Furong’s father; the second had died young; the youngest was Liu Bucai. Liu was exceptionally clever, but had been a spoiled spendthrift from childhood. Furong’s father was an extremely honest, kindly man, unable to discipline his younger brother. Afraid that friends and relatives would call him harsh, he supplied money as generously as possible for Liu’s extravagance. Thus, although Liu Jingde Hall did a large trade, the family was not solidly prosperous. Then, ten years earlier, a terrible disaster struck. Furong’s father had gone to Sichuan to buy medicinal ingredients. Sailing down through the Three Gorges, his boat was wrecked at Xintan. He died, and an entire cargo of valuable medicines was lost in the river. Liu hurried to Sichuan to handle the aftermath, behaving everywhere like a grand young gentleman and spending lavishly. Hiring men to recover the body alone cost several hundred taels. They never found it, so he held rites for the dead locally, spending still more. “Think of it,” Furong said. “How could the business prosper with Third Uncle behaving like that? Within a year it could no longer be kept going. After reckoning what others owed us and what we owed them, there was still a debt of seven thousand taels. Third Uncle was proud then. He handed over the whole shop—the building, fittings and stock—at a valuation of ten thousand, receiving three thousand as the balance.” He spent those three thousand within another year. Then came pawning possessions, followed by selling furniture and utensils. When nothing remained to pawn or sell, they lived by borrowing. People could help in an emergency, but not support endless poverty. At last there was nowhere left to borrow. Furong shook her head and stopped. The misery needed no further description. After reflecting, Hu asked, “What about your mother?”

“She died long ago. My brother was born after my father’s death, and Mother died in childbirth. When I was fifteen, Third Aunt too was driven to her death by my uncle’s behavior. I don’t know how he managed it: our rice jar was empty every day, yet every day he came home drunk. I supported my brother by embroidering for other people. I hoped to save a few taels so that he could go to school. But that was a dream!” “Why?” “Third Uncle!” Her expression mingled anger and amusement with a resignation born of despair. “Wherever I hid the money, he found it. Fate, surely!” Hu could not help smiling. “A kind of ability in itself. But it could not go on. What did you do?” “Exactly. I thought and thought, then made up my mind.” She paused and straightened herself. “When I was twelve, a fortune-teller read my birth details and said I was destined to be a secondary wife. If I refused to believe it, I would bring disaster upon my husband’s family. So I told Third Uncle: since that was my fate, he might as well sell me as a concubine. Two or three hundred taels would let him resume the family trade, open a small medicine shop and support my brother. There would be some hope for the future.”

“Do you know what he said?” Hu understood men like Liu perfectly. What they prized was empty dignity. “He refused, for fear of losing face.” “Exactly. He said, ‘A family like ours may be poor, but its foundations remain. How could we give a daughter to be someone’s concubine? Anything else can be discussed, but never that.’ That was one thing that made me feel there was still hope for him.” Her tone did not quite accord with what she had said earlier. Hu had questions, but would not interrupt. He nodded. “And afterward?” “Afterward I married the man who is now dead.” Her voice darkened. “In little more than a year, the prediction came true.” Hu now understood: she was willing to become a concubine because she accepted what she believed to be her fate. But what about Liu? Had he remained as stubborn as before? What means had Yu used to bring him round? Should Hu ask Furong now or Yu tomorrow? While he considered, Furong spoke again. “There is one thing I cannot reconcile myself to. My first husband died of an epidemic disease. At the beginning it was not severe. Even ordinary medicines such as Huoxiang Zhengqi Pills or Zhuge Marching Powder would have saved him. But he was on a boat, in the middle of the night, and could not obtain even those. I often think that, if our shop had still been open, such medicines would have been everywhere. I would certainly have put some in his traveling chest before he left, and they would have been ready when needed. He ought not to have died, yet he did. That is what I cannot accept.”

Hu said nothing. Her story had given him an idea that required careful thought. As they sat facing each other in silence, there was a soft crack. He looked up: a large bright tuft had burst from the red candlewick. “It is late,” Furong asked gently. “You must be tired?” “You must be too.” He held her hand and lightly squeezed her arm. Even through the purple satin jacket, he could feel that it was soft without being weak or slack. “You are not thin, after all.” Her eyes shifted playfully. Pretending indifference, she asked, “Do you like women thin or plump?” “Neither thin nor plump—just as you are.” She said nothing, but looked pleased. “What about your wife? Is she thin or plump?” “She was much like you, but grew plump after childbirth.” An important question suddenly occurred to him. “Have you any children?” “No. The fortune-teller said I was destined to have two sons.” This pleased Hu considerably. Cupping her face, he said, “I can read faces too. Let me have a good look.” With their eyes meeting directly and no room to evade his gaze, Furong felt deeply ill at ease. Smiling in embarrassment, she pulled his hands away. “There is nothing to look at.” She moved aside.

Taking her hand, Hu sat with her on the edge of the bed. “Tell me something. The other day you agreed to the vegetarian meal. Why did you change your mind as soon as we left Tiansheng Temple?” “I was a little afraid.” “Of what?” She gave a secretive smile and kept shaking her head, unwilling to answer. “Tell me. What is so difficult to say?” After hesitating, she finally spoke. “I was afraid of being taken in by you.” “Taken in how?” Hu laughed. “Did you think I would put poison in the food?” “Not poison—I was afraid you would slip me a bewitching potion!” She laughed herself, then turned and buried her face in a white satin quilt embroidered with a phoenix greeting the rising sun, too embarrassed to look up. Whether or not she meant it, Hu found the answer delightful. He bent over her, kissing her neck and hair, then kicked both feet and sent his brand-new satin shoes flying far away.

The next morning he did not rise until the clock struck ten. Drawing back the bed curtains, he saw Furong already neatly dressed and tidying her dressing table. At the sound of the curtain hook, she turned, smiled shyly and asked softly, “Won’t you sleep a little longer?” “No.” Hu stepped down barefoot. “A happy occasion refreshes the spirit. Why sleep?” “Look at you!” she said anxiously. “The chill of the brick floor will rise through your feet. Get back on the bed.” She draped a lightly padded jacket over him, pushed him onto the bed’s edge and put on his socks, leggings and shoes. Then she drew him to his feet, tied his waistband and helped him into his long robe. Hu had never been attended to like this and felt a strange new pleasure. “No wonder they call a concubine an attendant,” he murmured. “So that is what the word means.” She had not heard clearly and looked up. “What did you say?” “That I really am enjoying a fortunate life. Now let us talk about serious matters.”

Hu had countless serious matters, but only two concerned Furong—or, in a sense, only one: making provision for her uncle and brother. “What is your brother’s name?” “He was born in the Year of the Rabbit. His childhood name is Little Rabbit.” “Send for him today. Your uncle won’t refuse to let him come, will he?” Hu understood human motives thoroughly, those of decent men and scoundrels alike. At present Liu could not be treated as a gentleman. Judging him by a less generous standard, Hu feared that he might use the boy as something valuable to bargain with. Hence the question. It was well founded. Furong immediately looked worried. “He might. I mentioned it to Third Uncle. He said that, as the boy was of the Liu bloodline, it would be improper—improper…” She did not know how to continue. Her face reddened: repeating the words would humiliate both herself and her family. Liu had spoken harshly. “You say you are destined to be a concubine and choose it yourself. I have nothing more to say. Yu is influential, and I cannot stand against him. But Little Rabbit is of our family’s blood. What would taking him to that man Hu mean? You have already lowered yourself. Must your brother become someone’s little servant too?” She had been near tears with anger, yet could not argue. She had intended to find a solution gradually. Now Hu had raised the matter first, and she did not know how to explain. Improper in what way? Hu quickly understood: it was a question of status. That was easily settled. “Naturally, a child of the Liu family will retain the surname Liu. I am his brother-in-law now. Surely I can look after your own flesh and blood?”

Furong wondered whether she had heard correctly. Thinking it over, she was certain he had said “brother-in-law.” Surprise and gratitude filled her, yet she could scarcely believe that such good fortune existed. “What other difficulty is there?” Hu asked. “Tell me, and we can discuss it.” What difficulty remained except the fear that he might not mean it? She would not let the crucial point slip. “You mean Little Rabbit may call you Brother-in-Law?” “What else should he call me? Master, as you do?” Furong’s use of “Master” was conventional; a principal wife used it too, so it did not openly mark a difference in status. But for Little Rabbit, calling him Master would mean something different. Hu’s generosity and delicate consideration left her completely content. She gazed at him with a smile, unable to speak for a long time. Her emotion confirmed that his guess had been right. “Then your uncle should have nothing more to say?” “Certainly!” she answered brightly. “I will go and fetch my little brother myself.”

Hu did not immediately answer that. “I should like to meet your uncle. Do you think I should call on him, or invite him here?” “He may not come. You need not trouble with him yet.” She was thinking chiefly of Hu. “To be honest, it is best to avoid him if you can.” “Tell me, how well does he know the family trade?” She had not expected the question, and its purpose was unclear. After thinking carefully, she guessed that Hu meant to recommend him for employment at a medicinal-goods firm. His skill was not poor, but he could not be trusted with money; if trouble arose, it could implicate whoever had guaranteed him. Yet Hu was offering help, and the man was her own uncle. She could hardly reject an opportunity for him. Torn between these considerations, she found it still harder to answer. “I mean his ability,” Hu clarified. “Does he know the trade?” “How could he not? It is the family business. He could have learned it simply by watching from childhood.” An idea suddenly occurred to her. “Master, I have a suggestion, though I don’t know whether it can be carried out.” Liu possessed several secret family formulas for pills, powders, ointments and medicinal preparations. They had been adapted from prescriptions used at the Ming court, and half the business of Liu Jingde Hall had depended on them. When the shop failed and its accounts still showed a debt of seven thousand taels, someone had proposed accepting the formulas in settlement. Liu had preferred to give up the premises and fittings and keep them. Poor as he was, he had retained his ambition. He told the creditors, “Liu Jingde Hall failed in my hands, and it must be restored in my hands. When the old shop opens anew, I shall need these formulas myself.”

“Reopening it now looks like a foolish dream,” Furong said. “I can be at ease about Little Rabbit, since he can depend on you. As for Third Uncle, except for smoking opium, he has committed every disreputable act I can think of. But I am his niece. However badly he has treated me, I cannot watch him go hungry without helping. Nor dare I ask you to find a way for him and bring trouble on you. That would only make trouble for myself. So I thought of persuading him to sell the formulas. Someone once offered seven thousand taels. Might they fetch ten thousand now? With ten thousand, he could squander money on women and gambling and still live for several years. Or perhaps he would reform, use it in business, behave decently, live frugally and become dependable. Then you would naturally be willing to help him. That would truly show the hidden merit accumulated by our ancestors.” Listening to this long explanation, Hu felt respect deepen within his affection. She was both sensible and naturally kind. Her words also gave him a clearer view of Liu: the man had ability and retained ambition. Disreputable though he was, so long as he did not smoke opium, he was not beyond recovery. Hu immediately made a decision and felt excited by it. But nothing could be settled until he met Liu. It was too early to tell Furong the whole truth.

“Your plan is excellent. Never mind whether the formulas are worth ten thousand taels. If he is willing to part with them, I can certainly get that price for him. Let us visit Third Uncle together this afternoon. You may wear a red skirt.” Traditionally, only a principal wife married through the proper ceremonies was entitled to wear red. Furong was grateful and delighted. Even if she could overstep the rule only temporarily because Mrs Hu was absent, it gave her dignity. Then another worry arose. If she was destined to be a concubine and assuming a principal wife’s position would bring harm to her husband, could she wear that red skirt? She must consult a fortune-teller before deciding. Instead of thanking him, she merely nodded vaguely. Just then A’zhu’s mother and A’qi arrived independently, each having had the same thought: both had sent bearers carrying food boxes with dishes and refreshments. Naturally, their meeting brought teasing. A’zhu’s mother was comparatively restrained; A’qi had no such limits and even asked about the pleasures of the bridal chamber, embarrassing Furong terribly. Fortunately, Yu and Chen soon arrived and interrupted her outrageous jokes.

After lunch together, the men and women separated. Furong took A’zhu’s mother and A’qi aside to ask whether she might wear the red skirt, while Hu invited Yu into the outer hall to talk. The subject was Liu Bucai. Yu too felt that this remained unfinished business. A’qi had persuaded Furong, and the He family had long said she could decide for herself, so they had raised no obstacle. With Liu, Yu had intended to let him name a price and meet it unless it was absurd. Instead, Liu had answered with unexpected pride. “Poor as I am, I have not sunk to selling my niece. A first marriage is for the father to arrange; remarriage is the woman’s own affair. If she chooses to become a concubine of the Hu family, I have nothing to say. But I do not intend to acknowledge the Hu family as my relatives.” “That is unlike his usual behavior,” Yu said. “I don’t know what he is planning. We ought to settle matters cleanly. Leaving a loose end is tiresome. I was about to suggest finding some way to give him money.” “You have taken more than enough trouble, Brother Yu. This need not concern you further. Furong has explained everything.” Hu smiled. “He may refuse to acknowledge me, but I am determined to acknowledge him.” “How?” Chen asked with youthful curiosity. “Liu Bucai is very difficult. I should like to see how you deal with him.” “I shall ask you to go ahead and clear the way.” Hu briefly recounted what Furong had told him, gave Chen a few instructions and sent him to sound out the ground.
