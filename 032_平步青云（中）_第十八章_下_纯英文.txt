INSTALLMENT 032
A Swift Rise to Prominence, Volume II — Chapter Eighteen, concluding part

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

Chen Shilong found out where Liu Bucai lived and went straight there. Like Ji Heling, Liu had rented spare rooms in the house of an old family fallen on hard times, though his quarters had a separate entrance. After two knocks, a fine-featured but very thin boy opened the door. His dark eyes turned towards Chen. “Who are you looking for?” Remembering Hu Xueyan's account, Chen guessed that this must be Furong's brother. “Little Rabbit, where is your Third Uncle?” “Inside.” But when Chen tried to enter, the boy blocked the doorway. “Don't come in. Tell me your name first.” “Why? Are you afraid I've come to collect a debt from your uncle? No, no! My name is Chen. I've come to bring him money.” Little Rabbit remained doubtful, but at last let him through. At once a delicious aroma made Chen swallow. He recognised the scent of stewing ham. “The fellow certainly knows how to enjoy himself!” Before he had finished, Liu appeared, strolling out and humming an operatic tune. He wore an old padded jacket of Hu silk and black imported-crepe trousers, their ankles tied with such neat precision that he looked remarkably brisk.

“Little Monk! I didn't expect you.” “Third Master Liu, I've come especially to pay my respects.” Excessive humility turned the greeting into mockery, and Liu retorted, “Away with you! Why make fun of a poor man?” “That isn't what I mean. I used to call you Liu Bucai. Now things are different. You've become my elder, and I must observe the proper forms.” “Well!” Liu blinked. “I never expected a younger relation to spring up so suddenly. Tell me, where have you come from?” “You've become related to my teacher. Doesn't that make you my elder?” Liu paused, then assumed an arrogant expression. “I don't know who your teacher is. In any case, I haven't recently formed a marriage connection with anyone. I return the honour you offer. My humble dwelling cannot accommodate your distinguished presence. This way, please!” Pointing outside, he ordered his guest to leave. Chen felt embarrassed, but could hardly quarrel. Ordinarily he might have done so without concern. Today he had been sent on a mission. He must endure it and find some way to make Liu Sancai withdraw the dismissal.

He forced the most natural smile he could. “Third Master Liu, you're no friend! Stewing something so delicious and keeping it all to yourself—aren't you ashamed? Or don't you expect to meet me at the gambling table again?” Mention of gambling weakened Liu's defiance. They were not close friends, but after losing, he had forgotten his dignity and borrowed gambling money from Chen two or three times. Remembering the obligation—and the hold it gave Chen—he could no longer maintain his stern face. “Blame your own tactlessness! You always pick up the kettle that won't boil. You know I can't stand that niece of mine, yet you bring her up to provoke me. How could I help being angry?” “Very well. Let that be the end of it. If you'll keep me to dinner, you provide the food and I'll provide the drink. Little Rabbit, come here!” Chen took out a scrap of silver weighing five or six qian. “Can you go shopping?” “What do you want to buy?” Liu asked. “I've tried the Shaoxing spirit at the shop by the lane entrance. It's good. Ask them to send two jin and take the money.” He put the silver into the boy's hand. “Keep the change for pear-syrup sweets!” “There's no reason for you to spend money!” Liu hurried between them, reaching with one hand for the silver and pushing Chen with the other, as though preventing payment. It was pure sleight of hand, like pretending to fight over a restaurant bill while holding only the other man's unoccupied left hand. The silver ended up in Liu's possession, while Little Rabbit was sent to obtain the liquor on credit. Chen saw through him. His backbone was not so stiff after all! He would never truly break with Furong either.

“Come along!” Liu's spirits had recovered. Lifting the earthenware pot's lid, he sniffed twice and smiled proudly. “‘Thank your feet for bringing you here.’ You're fortunate in your food! With aged ham, everything depends on cleaning it properly. I've spent the whole morning plucking every hair from it.” “Third Master Liu,” Chen seized the opening, “you'll have more aged ham than you can eat. I've come to bring you a business opportunity.” “Business?” Liu was unconvinced. “Why come to me? What business could we discuss?” “There is some. I'll tell you shortly.” Only when they had their cups in hand did Chen explain. A friend intended to open a very large medicine shop in Hangzhou. Knowing Liu Jingde Hall's reputation and Liu's expertise, he had asked Chen to inquire whether Liu would become his partner. “A partner? On what terms?” Liu shook his head. “We can discuss anything else, but this won't work. Where would I find capital?” “Haven't you several medicinal formulas handed down in your family?” Liu's expression darkened immediately. His smile vanished. “So that's what you're after! No wonder! I thought it strange that someone should be kind enough to overlook my poverty and invite me into partnership.”

His words and manner angered Chen. “Well!” he replied just as sharply. “No wonder people call you Liu Bucai! Like the dog that bit Lü Dongbin, you don't recognise kindness. What makes you think he wants to steal your formulas? Do you know what he actually proposes?” “Wait.” Liu became more amenable. “First, who is this would-be partner? Is his surname Hu?” Chen promptly turned the question back on him. “Have you ever met my Mr Hu?” “Never.” “Then let me tell you.” Chen neither confirmed nor denied the identity. “This man is an expectant county magistrate, very well regarded in official circles. Though he doesn't appear in person, he is connected with many public appointments. He wants a medicine shop for more than trade. His intention is to help the world...” “Enough!” Liu said scornfully. “‘Though none see the compounding, Heaven knows the heart.’ Do you think I don't understand that couplet hanging in medicine shops? Why try to sell me that remedy?” “I'm not selling you anything. I'm merely repeating what I was told. Believe it or not, as you please.” “Spare me the talk. Whether he wants profit or to help the world is nothing to me. If the partnership means getting hold of my family's secret formulas, tell him to abandon the idea.” “It all comes down to your fear of the formulas falling into someone else's hands. You should know that he doesn't want possession of your precious recipes!” “Then...” Liu was astonished and could not understand.

Chen explained the arrangement. Naturally the inherited formulas would be used, but Liu would not have to disclose them. Once the shop opened, he would work there as a partner and compound those medicines himself. Which ingredients served as sovereign, minister, assistant, and envoy, their quantities, and the methods of preparation would be known to him alone. Why fear the secrets leaking out? Realising that nobody meant to take his formulas, Liu felt he had been rash. He nodded apologetically. “That is something we could discuss.” “And the terms are reasonable. You compound the medicines; he sets the prices; you receive a percentage. If your medicines work and sell well, your share increases. If they don't work and nobody wants them, then, I'm sorry, you take your precious formulas and pack your bags.” “How could they fail to work? There's one dogskin plaster that enabled a Ming emperor to father a crown prince. It's priceless!” “What boasting!” Chen laughed. “Everyone knows Liu Jingde Hall's dogskin plaster. It's a complete fraud!” “You don't understand. Honestly, the formula is genuine, but the ingredients are terribly costly and the preparation very troublesome. We had to cut corners, so naturally its effects were poorer. Never mind that now.” Liu straightened, braced his hands on the table, and leaned earnestly towards Chen. “Your friend speaks like someone who knows his way about. He's no fool. His proposal benefits me without harming me. I can go forward or withdraw. If we agree on my percentage, I'll join him.”

“Then tell me what share you want.” “First, why should he set the prices? We ought to discuss them together.” “There's no room for discussion there. You would want them high. Since he wants to help people, he'll naturally keep them low.” Feeling that this was not well put, Chen added, “Besides, small profits and large sales make good business. If you frighten customers away by overcharging, even the best medicine is useless.” “That's true. But if profits are small, my percentage should be higher.” Chen was shrewd too. “With small profits and large sales, your total won't be small either. Why should the percentage increase?” Then he softened his position. “Still, all that can be discussed. Settle it face to face when you meet. I'm sure you'll get on very well.” Liu nodded. Gnawing a piece of ham trotter held in his hand, he rolled his eyes about in a way that showed how carefully he was calculating. Chen did not press him, merely watched coolly and considered his own next move. “That's settled!” Liu threw down the bone and wiped his hands vigorously. “I've decided to make this man's acquaintance. Little Monk, when shall we meet? Once a thing is decided, there's no point delaying. The sooner the better!”

Seeing his eagerness, Chen encouraged him further. “Third Master Liu, is there anything you find awkward to say? I can pass it on. Before you meet, I can help with any difficulties. Afterwards you'll talk directly, and it will no longer be my affair.” Liu had wanted just such an opening. Collecting himself, he said, “Little Monk, since you're kind enough, I needn't conceal anything. You know my circumstances. If he wants me to join him in Hangzhou, even after we agree, I must be able to leave.” “I understand. How much debt do you have?” “Only a few hundred taels.” “And your nephew? Someone must look after him.” “No need. I'll take him to Hangzhou.” “I see.” Chen stood. “Then I'll report to him first. I'll bring word early tomorrow about when you can meet.”

Next morning Liu rose unusually early. His home might be shabby, but his visiting clothes remained splendid. Not only their material but even their patterns were carefully chosen. His iron-grey robe of moben satin had a subtle design of pine, bamboo, and plum. The plum blossoms were only just opening, appropriate to the morning. For an afternoon outing, he had another robe on which they were in full bloom. Dressed, he sat waiting for Chen's message. At nine, someone knocked. Opening the door himself, he was astonished to find two sedan chairs and four people: an older woman servant, a maid, an exceptionally beautiful young woman, and his own niece. “Third Uncle!” Furong, wearing a red skirt, called to him. Without waiting for his reply, she turned to introduce her companion. “This is Mrs Yu. This is my Third Uncle.” Mrs Yu was naturally A’qi. With a graceful smile, she greeted him. “Third Master Liu!” He grew anxious. He cared about appearances, and his home was in chaos. How could he receive this radiant Mrs Yu, her hair resplendent with jewels? Flustered, he desperately sought some way to keep them from entering. Just then, Little Rabbit slipped beneath his arm.

“Sister!” “Little Rabbit!” Furong caught her brother in her arms, then held his face to inspect it. “Look how dirty you are! Your nostrils are like chimneys. I don't suppose you've washed your face for three days!” Distressed, she pulled the embroidered handkerchief from her button and wiped his nose without a thought for the cloth. “Third Master Liu, forgive the intrusion,” A’qi said. “I've brought my younger sister to pay respects to her uncle. Please sit inside so that she can make her bow.” Suddenly the visitor had taken command. Liu lost his bearings, while Furong had already led Little Rabbit indoors. Now he had no course of his own. Whatever concerned Furong must wait. His sole immediate task was to receive the beautiful Mrs Yu properly. Smiling apologetically, he said, “I'm terribly sorry to trouble you. Please come in. Such a small, dirty place is unworthy of distinguished guests.” “No formalities!” A’qi smiled and changed her form of address. “Third Uncle Liu, we're all family. There's no need for ceremony.” “Yes, yes! Quite right, Mrs Yu. This way. I'll show you in.”

With a graceful swing of his sleeves, Liu led them into the room that served as both sitting room and dining room. He fetched his only respectable piece of furniture, a rectangular rosewood stool, and dusted it with his spotless handkerchief before inviting A’qi to sit. Then he searched for tea, washed cups, and kept up polite conversation, bustling about like a performer in a one-man show. He seemed to have forgotten Furong entirely. The two women exchanged a glance, amused and reassured. He now appeared quite easy to deal with. “Third Uncle Liu, don't trouble yourself. Sit down. I have something serious to say.” “Very well. Obedience is better than ceremony. What are your instructions?” Only after sitting did he notice that Little Rabbit's face was perfectly clean and that he wore a new outer robe, sitting quietly against his sister. “Why didn't you come to the wedding feast the day before yesterday?” A’qi's first question embarrassed him. He could only pretend surprise. “Wedding feast?”

“Yes, Furong's wedding feast.” A’qi made her meaning plain. “Third Uncle Liu, there must be some misunderstanding. Look at the skirt she's wearing! Mr Hu is the sole son carrying on three family branches. By custom, he may take a wife for each branch. Furong is his Huzhou wife and won't even meet his Hangzhou wife. Others honour her, yet her own uncle treats her as though she were worthless! You stayed away even on her happy day, making people think she was some low creature. Tell me, how can that be right?” Her words were brisk and her expression vivid; whether glancing sideways or directly at him, her eyes flashed. The accusation brought Liu entirely to submission. Smiling apologetically, he said, “You're right, Mrs Yu. I was wrong.” Turning to Furong, he added, “How was I to know? If I'd understood, I'd naturally have arranged your wedding myself. There's only one thing now: I'll send invitations and give a belated feast.” “That's one matter,” A’qi continued. “Here's another. Your wife has died, and you haven't remarried. Living alone and caring for a nephew is inconvenient. Why not let Furong take her brother?” “As for that...” At last Liu agreed. “Very well.” A’qi smiled delightedly. “Thank you, Third Uncle Liu. You've done me an honour. One more thing: when will the families meet?” This meant meeting Hu. Naturally, Liu thought, his niece's husband should first call on his uncle by marriage. But the house was dreadful. Better to wait until the medicine-shop partnership was settled and he had a little money to improve appearances. “We must choose an auspicious day. I need to make preparations. Could we wait a day or two?”

A’qi too had been sent to sound him out. The important point was his attitude towards Hu, not the date. Furong was his own relation; whatever happened, he could hardly ignore her. Hu was another matter. Liu might put on grand airs and refuse the connection, or agree publicly and then say something offensive at their meeting. In Hu's present position, he could not afford that humiliation. He had therefore sent out two separate parties: Chen to discuss business alone, and A’qi to elevate Furong's status, dispel Liu's resentment and suspicion, and then test his response to a family meeting. A’qi had seen enough of the world to recognise that no hostility towards Hu remained. His wish to prepare was probably genuine. Her mission was accomplished. Now she should give uncle and niece a chance to speak privately. That was easy. Drawing Little Rabbit over, she asked his age and whether he had begun lessons, then naturally led him onto the veranda to see his uncle's caged birds. Left alone, Liu asked, “Furong, what is this man Hu really like?” “You'll know when you meet him.” The answer showed her satisfaction. Liu reflected that he no longer felt the causeless anger of the previous two days. A remarrying widow, supposedly destined to be a concubine, had instead become one of two wives with equal standing in separate households. That was tolerable. And the medicine-shop partnership filled him with excitement. His luck was turning; recovery seemed near.

“This is passable, after all. If you'd really become someone's concubine, how could I face people? Of course, that's partly because your uncle has amounted to nothing. Never mind that now. Furong, I have good news. Someone has asked me to join him in opening a medicine shop.” He told her everything Chen had proposed. She listened patiently. Only now did she fully understand Hu's intentions. No wonder everyone called him capable! His method was admirable. Yet her pleasure was mixed with worry, which she expressed at once. “Third Uncle, it's a good thing. I'm only afraid you'll make a mess of it.” “You're always like this!” Liu was displeased. “You never trust me.” “It's not that I don't trust you. You don't understand how anxious I am! You're over forty and still drift about all day. How can that continue? Such a chance is rare. If you ruin it until people avoid you whenever they see you, where will you find another chance to recover?” “Hmph! You don't understand.” He remained unconvinced. “I'll only compound medicines according to the formulas. I won't handle money or run the shop. I'll receive my share every month. What mess could I make?” “It needn't be a matter of mishandling money. Spending your days gambling and neglecting your duties would make a mess of things too.”

“And another thing,” Furong continued. “Suppose they recognise your ability and ask you to be the manager. What then?” This had not occurred to Liu. On reflection, it was a real question. “You see?” She pressed him to exert himself. “You don't even trust yourself. Why blame me for not trusting you?” “Simple. If they ask me to manage, I'll refuse. Then there'll be no mess to make. Surely you can be easy now?” “A lazy monk hopes for no offerings!” Furong was annoyed. “I've never seen anyone like you. All you do is boast!” “When have I boasted?” He became angrier. “You're talking nonsense.” “Then let me ask: didn't you say that, since Jingde Hall had failed in your hands, you would restore it yourself?” “Yes, I did. That's no boast.” “Isn't it?” She looked directly at him. “What will you restore it with? Your hope now lies in just such an opportunity, and everything depends on your effort. On one side, live frugally and save little by little until you have some capital. On the other, behave responsibly and help them establish the shop, so that you can ask for their support. Together, those things might put Liu Jingde Hall's signboard up again. As you think now, spending everything you have for present pleasure, what plans have you made for the future? Tell me, Third Uncle, isn't that boasting?” Her sustained argument left him speechless. Thinking it over, he had to admit how clearly she saw and how carefully she had planned. For a clerk aspiring to become a proprietor, no path could be sounder. Yet he still could not answer. Judging himself honestly, he had no confidence that he could live frugally and behave responsibly as she demanded.

During the silence, a young man hurried in. Liu went to the doorway and recognised Chen. “Little Monk!” He wondered how Chen knew Mrs Yu too: they were facing each other, speaking in low voices, as only acquaintances would. Chen answered and came over. Seeing Furong, he respectfully greeted his teacher's wife. “Madam!” Then he turned to Liu. “Third Master Liu, I've arranged the meeting. We can leave whenever you're ready.” “Yes, let's go.” Liu told his niece, “It's the man who proposed the partnership.” Furong took Little Rabbit, and she and A’qi departed in their chairs. Liu invited Chen to sit, wanting first to understand who the other man was and where they would meet. “At Jucheng Bank, Mrs Yu's...” “Wait!” Liu interrupted. “That Mrs Yu is Yu Si's wife?” “Yes. Didn't you know her?” “No, and it never occurred to me. I'd only heard that Yu's concubine had parted from him after a quarrel some time ago. I never imagined she was the Mrs Yu here.” An idea struck him. “Then the proposed partner must be Hu Xueyan?” There was no need to conceal it now. Chen nodded. “Exactly. Mr Hu. A partnership between close relations—what could be better? A man needn't fear lack of success or luck, Third Master Liu. He should fear missing an opportunity. Failure to recognise one may be excusable. Seeing it clearly and letting it pass brings the bitterest regret.”

Liu remained silent. It had come so suddenly that he needed to think. “Gambling requires coolness, accuracy, and daring,” Chen said. “Here's a splendid opening. If you don't seize it, you'll truly deserve the name Liu Bucai and never recover.” “Is it really such an opening?” “Of course. Take Uncle Four alone!” From Yu, Chen went on to You Laowu, from Wang Youling to Ji Heling, then from Old Zhang to himself. His conclusion was that everyone who associated with Hu found good fortune. Half fact and half skilful embellishment, the account held Liu's complete attention. He missed not a word. “Very well!” Liu declared with the heroic air of a gambler staking everything in a final battle. “I'll take your advice. I'll make this wager!”

Chen slowly drank his tea to ease his thirst while considering the next move. Calling Hu his teacher was no empty form: he had learned much about managing people. Liu was enthusiastic now and would not change his mind. The question was how to win his wholehearted loyalty at once. “Well?” Liu could not understand his silence. “You've made me talk till my throat is dry. Give me time to moisten it.” Chen set down the cup and rose. “I'll go ahead and arrange matters concerning your difficulties. Come to Jucheng yourself at noon. Will that do?” “You mean you'll first find money for me? There's no need to worry about that now.” “No ceremony! Ceremony will only make you suffer. Frankly, your circumstances aren't good. You might find even a dinner party difficult to afford. If you have to attend to business while scraping money together for creditors, your situation will be exposed and your dignity lost. Better to let me arrange it beforehand.” Liu saw the sense of this and left it to him, promising to meet Hu at Jucheng at noon.

At the appointed hour, Chen was waiting at the entrance. He led Liu into the reception room, where Hu greeted him with a deep bow and called him “Third Uncle.” A sumptuous banquet with silver tableware was ready, including four dishes of dried fruit and four of fresh. Liu addressed him as “Brother Xueyan,” speaking only of his admiration and avoiding the family connection. Yu kept them company, while Chen helped the conversation along. Treated as the guest of honour, Liu felt wonderfully flattered. After tea they took their places, Liu at the head, Yu and Chen on either side, and Hu in the host's seat. Following three rounds of wine, Yu introduced the business. “Brother Liu, I didn't originally approve of Old Hu's plan for a medicine shop. Now I think it makes sense. Doing good and helping people is always worthwhile. I mean to invest too. But he and I know nothing of the trade. We must rely on you for everything.” “You flatter me. This is my own trade, and I'll gladly do whatever I can. But I don't yet know the plan or its scale.” “That's where we need your advice, Third Uncle. As for scale...” Hu considered. “To begin with, I mean to raise a hundred thousand taels.” A hundred thousand, merely to begin! Without Chen's advance account and Yu's promise to invest, backed by his reputation for wealth, Liu would hardly have believed it. Excited but cautious, he said, “That would be a very large concern. But in troubled times, trade may not be as good as in peaceful years.”

“In peaceful times, people take nourishing medicinal pastes. In troubled times, we should sell medicines that save lives and fewer tonics,” Hu replied. “Don't worry about trade, Third Uncle. Great wars are followed by great epidemics. Refugees suffer hunger in the morning, cold at night, and difficulty adapting to unfamiliar conditions. When they fall ill, they must buy medicine. Those who can't afford it shall receive it free.” Liu nodded, thinking that the man certainly spoke on a grand scale. Yet there was calculation behind the grand words. “The principles of business are the same everywhere,” Hu continued with a smile. “Establishing a name comes first. That's why I propose giving medicine away.” “I understand that,” Liu answered calmly. “Every medicine shop gives to the poor and sick. It doesn't do much beyond being a good deed.” “Then they're doing it the wrong way. I heard an interesting story in Shanghai. Let me tell you.”

The story concerned a small medicine shop in the capital during Yongzheng's reign. It supplied medicines to the palace dispensary, choosing particularly genuine, high-quality ingredients, and the emperor trusted its products. In one of the Dragon, Dog, Ox, or Goat years when the great examination was held, the metropolitan examination fell in the third month and was known as the Spring Examination. The preceding winter had been mild, with little snow. Abnormal weather in early spring brought an epidemic, and many provincial examination graduates fell ill. Even those who could carry on often had no appetite and felt listless. The shop's owner prepared a remedy for the seasonal illness and asked a minister of the Imperial Household Department to inform the emperor that he wished to give it to every candidate to carry into the examination compound for use if needed. The cells there were so small that a man could neither stand fully upright nor stretch out his legs when reclining. After three sessions, a weak constitution might give way even in good health, let alone when unwell. Yongzheng, an emperor keenly attentive to people's circumstances, had already been worrying about the candidates. He warmly approved. Acting by imperial command, the shop stationed men at the entrance to the examination compound. As the candidates entered, a packet of medicine was placed in each examination basket without anyone needing to ask. The wrappers were elegantly printed and bore the words “By Imperial Command.” Each packet also contained a leaflet listing the shop's celebrated pills, powders, ointments, and elixirs. The outcome owed something to the quality of the medicines and something to the shop's good fortune: no more candidates withdrew through illness than in previous examinations. This was taken as evidence of the remedy's effectiveness. Afterwards, whether they had passed or failed, the candidates all went to buy the shop's medicines, and business flourished extraordinarily.

“Think of it,” Hu said. “People in all eighteen provinces, even distant Yunnan and Guizhou, came to know their medicines. However much you spent hiring men to put up notices throughout the empire, you couldn't achieve that effect. It all depends on whether you use your head.” “Exactly!” Yu laughed. “That's what makes you so formidable in business. You think of things nobody else can.” Liu's attitude had changed too. Eagerly, he asked, “Then how shall we give ours away?” “To the military camps...” “Nothing could be better!” Liu interrupted. “I have a formula for Zhuge Marching Powder. Its ingredients are quite exceptional, and its effects are miraculous.” “Excellent indeed! We'll need to give large quantities to the camps, and naturally there's a method. I'll devise ways to have others provide the money, while we charge only cost. People contributing to the army, including those donating military funds, can specify quantities of our Zhuge Marching Powder and other medicines, with their value calculated in silver. As long as the medicines are good and the soldiers trust them, I have a second step that will bring a profit.” Hu deliberately withheld the next step to test Liu's ability to guess. Liu could not, but Chen spoke. “I understand! Do you mean to deal with the military supply offices?” Naturally Liu had failed to guess: he knew nothing of army arrangements. Hu nodded earnestly at Chen, looking pleased with such a promising pupil, then developed his explanation.

“A military supply office attends to almost everything except fighting at the front. The most troublesome business is naturally dealing with casualties after a battle, so it uses enormous quantities of medicine. That's what we would sell it. Prices must be low and goods good. We can extend credit, making the public accounts easier to manage. Privately, of course, we'll pay the usual kickbacks...” “What a business!” Liu exclaimed. Interrupting was a habit of his. “But what an amount of capital it would require!” “Yes. Though the reverse also holds: if we can allow credit, we can request payment in advance. It depends on whether the supply office has money. The Jiangnan Grand Camp relies on military subsidies from the provinces. Vice Minister Zeng of Xiangxiang in Hunan also relies on contributions from various places to support his volunteer troops beyond his own province. With the right connections, payment for our medicines might be deducted directly from those subsidies. It depends on how one goes about it.” “On how one uses one's head!” Liu raised his cup. “Brother Xueyan, I drink to you.” “You do me too much honour. We shall still rely on you, Third Uncle.” “There's only one thing to say!” Liu pointed at Chen. “He knows my temperament. I've told him already: I'll stake everything on this one wager!”

From a white pouch worn against his skin, Liu produced a small, beautifully made book, bound with silk thread, its cover of red silk and its corners protected with blue. He handed it over. Seeing the title, Secrets of the Apricot Grove, Hu knew what it contained. “Here's my stake. When I say I'll stake it, I do.” Liu looked again at Chen. “Tell me, am I doing the right thing?” Chen thought not only that it was right, but that Liu had acted splendidly. As Hu's advance negotiator, he shared the credit. Smiling broadly, he nodded repeatedly. “Please put it away,” Hu said. “Since you approve of my proposal, matters are easy. We'll discuss them carefully afterwards.” Both men behaved generously: one offered his treasure to prove his sincerity, while the other would not casually accept it. They pressed and declined for some time until Chen proposed a solution. He fetched a sheet of mulberry-bark paper used for wrapping silver dollars, wrapped the book, sealed it, and marked the closure with a personal sign. It was entrusted to Yu, who immediately locked it in the safe.

After dinner, over tea, Liu did all the talking. He explained how to open a medicine shop, hire staff, obtain supplies, and prepare the medicines; what advantages and difficulties to expect; and what required caution. He spoke enthusiastically while the others listened intently. Everyone took the matter seriously. “Third Uncle,” Hu said when he finished, “I can't grasp all these rules and practices at once. We shall depend on you. But I have one thought. Nothing could be worse than quoting genuine formulas and selling false medicines. Customers must see plainly for themselves before they'll trust us.” “That can be arranged. If we're preparing Shiquan Dabu Pills, for example, we could announce it beforehand and invite people to see whether the ingredients are genuine and worth the price.” “That's precisely the difficulty. How do I know whether what you're using is genuine?” Liu paused. “By that reasoning, if we sell deer velvet, must we keep a deer in the shop?” He sounded thoroughly puzzled. Hu answered without hesitation. “Yes! Why not?” This opened Liu's eyes. After consideration, many ideas occurred to him. “Then let's give ourselves a free hand. If we're willing to spend, there'll be no shortage of fresh ideas.” “We aren't devising tricks merely for their own sake. We want business to be both honest and lively.” “Honest and lively!” Liu repeated, committing the words firmly to memory.

At this point, some further gesture was needed. Judging the moment right, Chen gave Liu a meaningful glance. Hu noticed too and rose before Liu could respond. “Stay a little, Third Uncle. Brother Yu and I have something to discuss.” In fact, there was nothing. He merely went inside to lie on the opium couch with Yu, leaving Chen free to speak. “Third Master Liu, look at this.” Chen handed him a booklet. It was a passbook issued by Jucheng, but when Liu opened it, there was no balance entered. It looked like a useless blank book. “Why is there no amount written in it?” Chen asked. “Third Uncle, do you understand the meaning?” “Honestly, no. Xueyan has so many ingenious devices that I'm convinced of his ability. Tell me what this means.” “It's for whatever you need. You can withdraw as much as you require, so there's no need to enter an amount. Only, you may withdraw no more than once a day.” Liu had never heard of anything so fortunate. Naturally, he did not suspect Hu of joking. After considering, he tested the offer. “Such unlimited freedom shows real trust. Suppose I wanted, say, eighteen thousand taels?” “That depends on the purpose. With sufficient credit, even eighteen thousand would not be impossible.”

Now Liu understood the deeper meaning. Naturally, Hu had given instructions and set a limit. Beyond it, Jucheng's clerks would find a reason to refuse. The daily restriction guarded against his taking the money to a gambling house. For a proper purpose, any shortfall could wait until tomorrow. Bets alone could not be placed on credit. This thought revived his haughty temper. He handed the book back. “Thank you.” His voice was cold. “I'm afraid I can't control myself. With an inexhaustible source of money, I'd certainly gamble until I no longer knew my way home.” Chen remained calm. “Third Master Liu, you said you'd stake everything on this one wager. Did you mean it?” “Naturally! Those formulas are my stake, and I've handed over every one. What more is there to say?” “That isn't the stake. Mr Hu says that, if you're truly determined, there's one thing you must do to show that you have really committed yourself. It isn't altogether difficult, nor altogether easy. I'll wait until you've thought it through before telling you.”

Liu considered. “Is it something I can do?” “Of course.” “Very well. Tell me.” Chen became unusually solemn. “A man who goes about in the world must keep his word.” “Need you say that? Little Monk, you look at people through a crack in the door!” Liu was offended. Hu had instructed Chen, and Furong had described her uncle fully. Deliberately seeking to rouse Liu's resolve, Chen answered casually, “I'm not looking through a crack to make you seem small. I'm much like you myself and know this won't be easy. Besides, mentioning it might hurt our friendship. That's why I have to be certain.” He gave a calculated sigh. “Perhaps I'd better not say it.” Liu ground his teeth but could hardly lose his temper. After restraining himself repeatedly, he managed, “Say it or don't, as you please! I refuse to believe that I, Liu, will give you reason to despise me.” “That's hard to say. There's one thing you may not be able to do.” “Tell me!” Liu struck the table with his fist and rose, bracing both hands on it and leaning forward, looming like a mountain about to crush Chen to the floor.

“Then can you do as I've done and never enter a gambling house again?” At the question, Liu's body sank involuntarily back into his chair. For a long time he could not speak. “Mr Hu says that only such a resolution will prove your sincerity. He asks nothing else. Of fine food, clothes, brothels, and gambling, he only wants you to give up one. In my view, if you can't abandon even that...” Chen shook his head. “There's no need to say more. It would sound unpleasant.” Even without the words, Liu could imagine them. Indulging all four would certainly reduce him to beggary. “Mr Hu also says that gambling is a contest of minds, and business is a contest of minds too. Why not put your gambler's ingenuity into business? Once you learn its ways, you'll naturally find it interesting. Then you won't want to gamble.” Liu considered in silence. Gradually his expression changed. The flightiness, impatience, and anger faded, giving way to calm and steadiness. At last he nodded. “That's sound advice.” His words were clear. “I'm giving up gambling!” “Congratulations!” Chen smiled and clasped his hands, pushing the passbook towards him again.

“Then let's discuss business. After all this talk, how should I begin? I want to understand.” That naturally required Hu. With matters settled, there was no further need to borrow Jucheng's premises. Hu easily persuaded Liu to come home with him, and the estrangement between uncle and niece dissolved almost without their noticing. An evening's discussion brought the undertaking into shape. The shop would be called Hu Qingyu Hall, and Liu would take charge of preparations. They agreed that he would accompany Hu to Hangzhou in three days. Details could be discussed there. “Third Uncle,” Furong urged, “you really ought to settle down. If there's a suitable woman, marry her and give me a new aunt.” “It's too early for that.” Liu shook his head. “My whole mind is on Hu Qingyu Hall.” He immediately changed the subject. “Xueyan, I think we should draw up plans and build our own premises.” “I think so too. Start everything from the beginning!” “Exactly,” Liu replied. “That's what I'm doing myself.”

He truly began afresh. During those three days, he produced a detailed plan for Hu Qingyu Hall, covering the purchase of land, construction, staffing, supplies, layout, and management. Hu had never before planned a business so thoroughly. Yet never before had he undertaken one on so uncertain a basis. This time the boastful man was Hu, not Liu. He had spoken of raising an initial hundred thousand taels, but did not know where the money would come from. Yu had offered to invest, yet was already supporting him to the limit of his resources. Hu could hardly ask him to sell his land, and in these unsettled times property could scarcely be turned into cash anyway. Fortunately, no immediate payment was required. Hu intended to consult Wang Youling in Hangzhou. A medicine shop was a sound business, with the admirable purpose of saving lives and helping people. Perhaps Huang Zonghan would contribute part of his well-filled official purse, investing under a member of his family's name. With Governor Huang taking the lead, finding other wealthy officials to provide the rest would be easier. This was only an initial plan, aimed at getting the enterprise established. There could be no talk yet of profits, much less of conducting it entirely according to Hu's ideals. Liu never imagined these calculations. Still enthusiastic, he talked of the shop whenever they met, all the way to Hangzhou. There Hu lodged him at the bank and assigned a young clerk to accompany him on daily outings, treating him with the greatest consideration.
