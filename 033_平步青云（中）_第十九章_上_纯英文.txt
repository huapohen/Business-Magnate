CHAPTER NINETEEN

Spending every day at leisure could not go on. What tormented Liu Bucai most was having nothing to do. His fingers itched, and he kept wanting to gamble. But whenever the thought arose, he remembered what he had told Chen Shilong and fought it down. After ten days of this, he could bear it no longer. What he could no longer bear was waiting for Hu Xueyan to tell him something definite. He waited another two days and finally caught him on the third. “Xueyan!” he said with some agitation. “I’ve been here more than half a month and haven’t done a thing. I know you’re busy, but if this goes on I shall fall ill from boredom.” “I know, I know! I’m terribly sorry. There were matters in several places that I had to attend to myself. Most are now in hand. Above all, the transfer of cargo for sea transport has at last been settled. I can spare some time. Tomorrow we shall begin by looking at sites.” Then Hu asked, “Third Uncle, how much can you drink?” “I can hold my own.” “Then let me introduce you to a friend.”

The friend was Qiu Fengyan. He had carried out the escort of the foreign guns very efficiently. Wang Youling had given him a sum of money and praised him warmly, so he had lately been in excellent spirits. He and Liu took to one another at once. Moreover, Hu had asked Qiu to look after him, and Qiu made a special effort to please. Liu felt that acquiring such a friend was something to be happy about. Qiu therefore undertook to accompany him in looking at land. Every morning they began with tea at a teahouse by Fengle Bridge. Qiu had lived in Yangzhou and was particular about his morning meal. He ordered two stir-fried dishes to go with his early wine, followed by noodles. Then a broker led them out to inspect sites. Some were too small; elsewhere they could not agree on the price. By the time they had weighed one consideration against another, it was three in the afternoon, and Qiu wanted tea and wine again. With his company, Liu was no longer so bored. The days passed peaceably, and his desire to gamble subsided.

One morning Hu unexpectedly came to see Liu and began, “Third Uncle, I need you to entertain a guest. He knows everything about women, gambling, fine food and clothes. You are the only man who can keep him company.” For a moment Liu could not answer. First, the request was unexpected. Second, it contradicted Hu’s original intention: he had wanted Liu to give up gambling, and now was asking him to gamble. Third, Liu had promised to stop and had actually done so. To break that resolution now, throwing away his hard-won determination and self-control, seemed a pity. “Third Uncle,” Hu said seriously, “have no misgivings. This is precisely where I need your help. To put it plainly, I am making use of your strengths.” There was nothing more to say. “If it will help you, of course I shall do it. But first you must explain what it is all about.” Hu hesitated. “It is a long story, with complications I cannot explain in a moment.” He paused. “In a word, if we keep this young gentleman amused, it will be a great help to my business.” “Mm, I understand. You want me to be his companion and entertainer?” “Not a dependent companion—a wealthy guest. Of course, a wealthy guest who also entertains him will be all the more effective!” Only now did Liu truly understand. Nodding gravely, he said, “Provided you don’t mind the expense, I know how to play the rich man, and how to make friends with rich men.”

“Of course! Why should I mind the expense? Third Uncle, what is the most you have ever lost at one sitting?” “I once lost...” Liu said. “I lost a pawnshop. Not a large one. It was worth thirty thousand taels.” “Good. You have seen high play. That will do. Have no worries: I can put thirty or fifty thousand taels in ready silver into your hands. More would be no trouble either. I can raise it whenever it is needed. Losing does not matter. What matters is never to look small-minded or mean.” “You can trust me there. At the gaming table my nerve is second to none.” They agreed that Hu would return that afternoon and accompany him to meet the young gentleman, bringing banknotes then. Liu went off in high spirits to have his head shaved and dressed himself as an elegant man of the world, then sat waiting. Shortly after noon Hu returned. Liu wore a grey-squirrel fur robe faced with iron-grey satin, a maroon Baturu waistcoat, and a little black satin cap with a coral knot on top and a piece of green jade carved with the longevity character at the front. There was a snow-white spun-silk jacket, crisply fitted trousers bound at the ankles, and a pair of double-ridged Zhangzhou velvet shoes. “Splendid! I have brought two things that will go perfectly with your clothes.” One was a gold repeating watch on a very thick gold chain; the other, a thumb ring of mutton-fat white jade. There were also twenty thousand taels in banknotes, none smaller than five hundred taels.

“It is still early. Let me tell you who this rich young gentleman is.” His surname was Pang. Hu had met him during his two days in Nanxun, and everyone called him Second Master Pang. His family had been in the silk trade for generations, accumulating wealth; then, when foreign trade opened late in the Daoguang reign, they had made large profits in the export business. Even if their fortune did not rival a state’s, its depth was far beyond what outsiders imagined. Second Master Pang was an idler of the first rank, but his family upbringing had given him a thorough knowledge of business. That was why Hu wanted to deal with him. He had purchased the rank of circuit intendant, though naturally he would never wait at an official headquarters for an appointment or an assignment. He did not ordinarily wear official dress. But if any prefectural or county official put on airs before him, Pang could outdo them all in official grandeur. That alone showed his character: he could be won by courtesy, but never bullied. So that was the man! Liu listened and considered. As a fellow Huzhou man, he naturally knew of Second Master Pang, but among wealthy young gentlemen he himself had stood at least two grades lower. Now he had fallen on hard times as well. To be introduced as “the third son of the old Liu Jingde Hall family” was hardly glorious. He could not help wondering beforehand what Pang would think.

“Third Uncle,” Hu continued, “to draw Second Master Pang closer, I specially asked Prefect Wang to give this party. He is your district’s governing official. However busy Pang may be, he cannot refuse. Today is only a dinner, so the gaming will not be on a very large scale—just some mahjong. You must show your skill and make him want a second game with you after the first. That is how the friendship will grow.” “I understand. You can rely on me for that. But...” Liu found it difficult to say. “We have never met, and he spends most of his time in Shanghai, but he probably knows who I am.” “That does not matter. A prodigal who reforms is worth more than gold. Nobody will laugh at you. Besides, I have spoken to Prefect Wang. He will treat you with particular courtesy. With the host showing you such respect, nobody will be able to fathom your circumstances.” Hearing this, Liu looked down at his finery and then at the twenty thousand taels in notes. His outlook changed. Everything else might be a pretence, but money was real. Money gave a man courage. What was there to fear? “You are right, Xueyan.” His spirits rose. “When do we leave?” He opened the repeating watch. It was only two in the afternoon. “We are expected at four. Naturally I must arrive early. Rest a little longer and come to Mr Wang’s residence at the appointed time.” Hu left a slip of paper bearing the address. With this agreed, they parted. Liu duly reclined on a couch and closed his eyes, carefully considering Pang’s temperament and how best to approach him. Feeling fairly confident, he opened his eyes cheerfully, made a few further adjustments to his appearance and hired a small chair to take him to the party.

At the Wang residence the host was indeed most courteous, addressing him at every turn as “Brother Sancai.” They sat exchanging civilities while the other guests arrived. Apart from Ji Heling and Qiu Fengyan, there were two wealthy young men. One was Zhou Wu, fifth in his family and a younger brother of the former Tianjin customs intendant. The other was Gao Si, the fourth son of a family whose members combined official service with commerce. Their conversation had no serious purpose. Either they spoke of how much they had lost at pai gow, or of some extraordinary beauty newly appeared aboard the Jiangshan pleasure boats. Second Master Pang arrived last. He was thirty-four or thirty-five, with a broad, pale face like a silver basin, resembling Cao Cao on the stage. His grandfather had recently died, so mourning restrictions obliged him to wear a plain grey-cloth robe lined with sheepskin. Yet on his hand was an “oil diamond” ring full of fire. He wore the stone turned inwards; whenever he raised his hand, flashes from his palm caught the eye all the more. The host made the introductions. Only Ji, Qiu and Liu were new acquaintances for Pang. Hearing Liu’s Huzhou accent, he warmed to him. “Brother Liu,” he asked, “where do you live? How is it that we have never met?” Liu gave his address. “I have long heard of you, Second Master Pang. I am delighted to make your acquaintance.” “The pleasure is mutual.” Pang was courteous, without the airs of a grand young idler. “Come, come!” cried the impatient Zhou. “Time to play!” Their host led them into a side room, where a mahjong table had been prepared. They stood discussing who should join. Pang, Zhou and Gao were obvious choices. For the fourth place, the host deferred to Ji, Ji to Liu, and Liu to Hu. Hu declined, settling the matter: Liu would play.

They drew seats and agreed on “one-two” mahjong, a base stake of ten thousand taels, a forty-point base score and sixteen rounds. Soon the tiles were clattering. Liu was in no hurry to go out; first he studied the others’ play. Pang and Gao were both highly skilful, but Gao had one weakness: he liked building elaborate hands. Zhou played as impatiently as he did everything else, taking any winning hand, large or small. Once he needed only one tile, he turned his tiles face down and stared into the pool of discards as though longing to snatch out the one he wanted. Yet bad player though he was, luck favoured him. In four rounds he made two all-one-suit hands and a Three Dragons hand. He had won nearly a full base stake and was delighted. “All because Gao insists on building big hands and discards so freely,” Pang said coolly as he threw the dice to change seats. “If you sit below me this time, watch out!” As it turned out, Liu sat immediately before Zhou, with Gao before him and Pang opposite. Gao persisted in his old habit: if seven of his thirteen tiles belonged to the same suit, he aimed for an all-one-suit hand. His discards were therefore still generous. Liu decided he must not claim too many of them. Otherwise his own discards would become loose, allowing Zhou to profit; once Zhou’s luck gained momentum, it would be hard to check. He refused even tiles that would complete an edge or a gap in a sequence and concentrated on the player after him, keeping Zhou firmly pinned down. Within two rounds, Zhou had lost half his winnings. But Pang, the heaviest loser, had made no recovery. Liu now decided that besides restraining Zhou, he must help Pang win. He played so well that a little attention to the tiles Pang drew and discarded showed him what Pang needed. Just as Pang reached a waiting hand, Liu would discard the winning tile. Pang went out twice in succession; his luck turned and he recovered his losses. When they stopped for dinner after eight rounds and counted up, three were winning from one. Gao was the heavy loser.

“You play extremely well,” Pang told Liu as they left the table. “And I admire your conduct at play still more.” “You are too kind!” Liu was gratified, and felt some admiration in return: Pang was a man who appreciated a favour. After dinner Pang’s luck grew stronger. Whenever he became dealer he retained the position, and his winning hands grew larger. This too owed something to Liu. He no longer fed Pang winning tiles, but concentrated on holding Zhou and Gao back, particularly preventing either from making a big hand. At any sign of danger, he either went out quickly himself or let someone else win a small hand. By the final reckoning, Pang was the great winner and Zhou the great loser. “Always the same—win first, lose afterwards!” Zhou said bitterly. “Losing is nothing, but the tiles are infuriating. That is why I dislike mahjong. There is no pleasure in it.” Pang and Gao, accustomed to this performance, merely exchanged smiles. Liu spoke. “Brother Zhou is impatient. Pai gow would suit him better.” “Exactly! Let me take the bank.”

Gao was willing either way, and Liu said nothing. Only Pang hesitated. “Isn’t it too late? We ought not impose on our host.” “Not at all!” Hu answered for Wang. “Please enjoy yourselves. Shall we have supper before playing, or...” “Too early for supper,” Zhou broke in. “Let me bank a game first.” Pang knew his temper. If Zhou took the bank, win or lose he would not stop before daylight. Pang therefore spoke at once. “We are all friends. Let us keep it modest. We can rotate the bank, playing big or small pai gow as you please, with ten thousand taels to each bank. Lose it all and give way; even a winning banker may play only four sets.” “Four is too few. At least eight.” “Come now. Four each makes sixteen sets. After that, we can enjoy our host’s supper and go home to bed.” “Quite right,” Gao agreed. “I have business tomorrow morning. Let us finish early.”

Zhou could not carry the argument alone and had to agree. When the dominoes appeared, Zhou naturally banked first. From his clothes he produced an ivory case shaped like a bean pod, slid off its lid and tipped out four dice. He had many little rituals. He threw all four until they showed a four and a five, then removed the red die. He threw the other three until he obtained a four and a six, and only then would he use the red one. Four and five made nine, which sounded like the word for wine; six and four made ten, made to sound like meat. He called it “treating the dice to wine and meat.” “Mahjong should be quiet, but pai gow needs a lively crowd,” he announced loudly. “Everyone must join in. You can bet even a single tael.” Qiu had not yet left. Liu gave him two hundred taels of “luck money” so that he could place bets of five or ten. Wang was persuaded to play too. Hu disliked gambling, but naturally had to contribute to the fun. Taking a hundred-tael note, he placed it on the upper gate, where Pang sat. “Big pai gow or small?” Pang asked. “You’ll know when I open the hands.” With Zhou’s temperament, it was bound to be small pai gow, where the whole issue was settled at a single turn of the tiles. His bank lasted only two sets before Pang and Gao broke it. Pang banked next and went through his four sets without much gain or loss. Zhou, however, lost over ten thousand more. Gao was the chief winner, while Liu won five or six thousand taels.

Liu was the third banker. Rolling up his snow-white cuffs, he shuffled and stacked the dominoes. As he opened the hands, he said, “Since Brother Zhou likes small pai gow, I shall play that too.” Zhou’s temper was rising with his losses. “Exactly! ‘In spring, never ask the way.’ I’m sitting at Heaven’s Gate, so that is the gate I’ll back.” He pulled a sheaf of notes from his clothes and slapped them on the table. “I’ll take the whole stake!” “Come now!” Pang objected sharply. “We are all here to enjoy ourselves. What pleasure is there if you leave nobody else room to bet?” “Why not? Each man can play his own game. If you fancy your lower gate, shift my stake over to it. Isn’t that just as good?” “Shifting stakes” meant that players at the side gates gambled against one another. If the banker won from all or paid all, the person who had shifted a bet was unaffected. But if one gate won and another lost, the difference was considerable. In pai gow and dice-shaking games, some players particularly liked shifting other people’s bets. If the original gate lost and theirs won, they won from both banker and fellow player, doubling their profit. They could also congratulate themselves on their judgment, which was very satisfying. But shifting stakes often became a contest of pride. One man shifted a stake across; the other shifted it back. Back and forth it went, each move doubling the gain or loss at issue, until the gambling grew wild. Zhou and Pang were now showing signs of such a quarrel. When gambling spoiled the harmony, others naturally had to intervene. But both were wealthy young gentlemen who could afford to lose money and could not endure losing a single word of argument. Making peace was difficult, and Hu grew anxious.

Just as Pang, beginning to lose his temper, was about to shift Zhou’s stake by declaring that Heaven’s Gate must follow the lower gate, Liu spoke first. “Brother Pang is quite right. We are all friends. This is only a friendly parlour game...” As expected, the hot-tempered Zhou interrupted. “What sort of thing is that for the banker to say? Tell me—if he is right, am I wrong?” “You are right too,” Liu answered calmly. “And Brother Pang need not shift any stakes. Since Brother Zhou is keen to play, I ought to oblige him. How about a private side bet of ten thousand taels?” It was Qiu who cried, “Good! That satisfies both sides.” A separate wager between the banker and another player was nobody else’s concern. There could be no reasonable objection. With ten thousand taels Liu had given both men a way to save face. It was skilfully done, and the money was not necessarily lost. Hu silently approved. Liu certainly knew how to handle a social occasion.

The dice showed seven. Zhou seized the two dominoes dealt on the outside and turned them up. Now he truly stared: an Ox-head and a Three–Six. His face turned livid with anger. “What do they call that?” Qiu said. “I heard a saying in Songjiang: ‘A black devil shouldering a foreign rifle!’” He spoke without a smile, in complete seriousness, so it did not sound like mockery. Everyone laughed, and Zhou laughed too. The banker swept all the gates. Liu could have put aside the ten thousand taels won on the private side bet, recovering his original stake and making the rest of his bank pure profit with nothing left to lose. Instead, with a fine gesture, he left it in play. He counted the money and announced the total—twenty-seven thousand taels—so that the other players could judge how much to bet.

His bank was steady, taking more than it paid and showing a gain on every deal. By the third deal of the third set, custom called for omitting the final deal and shuffling again. Instead, Liu offered to play on. “Only one set remains,” he said. “I’ll play the last deal. Place your bets quickly!” “My friend,” Pang cautioned him, “the remaining tiles favour the lower gate. You had better leave this deal alone.” “Thank you for the warning. But that is the pleasure of banking at pai gow. If the dice help, the banker alone may have the high hand and sweep everything in. It happens often enough.” “Then let’s try it! I don’t believe the lower gate will come up empty.” Zhou took out another handful of notes. “How much has the banker?” Liu counted: forty thousand taels in all. “Let everything follow the lower gate.” Zhou put his notes there. “I’ll take whatever remains.” Everyone grew tense. There were no draws in small pai gow. On this one deal, the banker would either turn his forty thousand into eighty thousand or lose everything and surrender the bank. It was the largest single wager of the evening. So much money changing hands commanded even Pang’s close attention.

Liu’s face and voice remained unchanged as he cast the dice. The three gates showed their hands: the upper gate had nine points, Heaven’s Gate seven, and the lower gate a Heaven tile with a Red Nine. They had agreed that this combination would count as one point, not as the special Heaven Nine hand. “You see? The lower gate is the live one!” Zhou declared, with something of bravado covering unease. “One hand overcomes the next. What else would you call it?” “Live it may be, but the point count is too small,” Pang replied. “The last deal often produces strange hands. A full bowl of rice is easy to swallow, Old Five; a mouthful of boasting is harder.” “Any points will bring money!” Zhou persisted. “If this hand loses, I’ll eat the dominoes.” The enormous wager was interesting enough. But Zhou’s boast, which might make the outcome impossible to manage, fascinated everyone in the room, from the players seated at the table to the servants and maids standing beside them. All longed to see what the banker would turn up. A Mixed Seven and a Mixed Five would make an ordinary two-point hand, enough to beat the lower gate’s one-point Heaven Nine. What an expression Zhou would wear then, after his sweeping claim! Yet nobody, not even Pang, expected what happened. Liu did not turn over his dominoes at all. “Brother Zhou, you are right. Your single point is worth a great deal.” With a calm, natural smile, he pushed the money forward. He seemed to lose to Zhou entirely willingly—indeed, almost to have defeated him.

By now Pang liked Liu very much and sided with him at every turn. “You haven’t even looked at your tiles!” he reminded him. “Can you really fail to beat one point?” “All the other tiles are out,” Liu said. “There is no need to look. One point loses to one point.” “I don’t believe it.” Pang began sorting through the exposed tiles, starting with the highest “Treasure” combination. He found a Two–Four but no Ding tile. If the banker’s one point lost to the other one point, his hand ought to be a Man-and-Ding One. Pang searched for the Man tiles. Sure enough, only one was there. When the banker’s tiles were turned over, there it was: a Man-and-Ding One. Ten red spots made the single black spot stand out all the more sharply. The silent room erupted in regret and laughter, astonishment and exclamations. Naturally, Zhou was the loudest. “Come, come! I’ll make the payments.” He drew the bank’s money and his own notes in front of him, paid the smaller wagers and kept the remainder as his winnings. “To think there really was such a hand!” Pang shook his head. “He couldn’t even turn up two points.” He regretted the banker’s loss so keenly that he almost resented it. Liu, however, behaved as though nothing had happened and pushed the dominoes towards Gao. This was the last bank. Its four sets also passed without much gain or loss. Their host then invited everyone into the hall for supper. As they ate and talked, the conversation inevitably returned to Liu’s hand.

“You have a remarkable eye,” Pang said. “You saw at once that a Man tile and a Ding were missing. That is a skill one cannot acquire in three or five years.” “Old Liu is a man of real quality,” even Zhou admitted. “Losing to you is a pleasure. We must have a proper game some time.” “Why some time?” Pang put in. “Tomorrow.” “But we have already agreed to dine with Old Hu tomorrow. Make it the day after.” “Tomorrow will do just as well,” Hu said. “Tell me whom you would like to play with, and I can send additional invitations.” “No need,” Pang replied. “The day after tomorrow, I shall invite you all to dinner and bring in a few friends for a proper game.” He turned particularly to Liu. “Are you free that day?” “I am free any day.” “Good. Come early, then. I hope everyone will honour me. Those who like to play can play; the others can simply dine. I have a new cook who does shark’s fin rather well. Come and try it.” “Thank you,” Wang said, “but I return to Huzhou the day after tomorrow.” They settled it there and then: everyone except Wang would accept Pang’s invitation. Ji Heling was naturally included. Pang admired him greatly, said they must be sure to have him and expressly asked Hu to convey the invitation.
