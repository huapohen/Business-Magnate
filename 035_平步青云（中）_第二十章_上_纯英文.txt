CHAPTER TWENTY

Now that he had the letter of authorisation, Hu Xueyan needed to think carefully. He had kept up a steady correspondence with Gu Yingchun, who handled his business contacts in Shanghai and passed on all news of You Laowu. Once the agreement with Pang Er was settled, his first act was to write to Gu. Then he attended to the affairs in his charge in Hangzhou, intending to leave for Shanghai early in the twelfth month and sell the silk before mid-month to meet his public and private obligations. After the New Year, he would launch fresh plans and make a great effort. Gu's reply arrived a few days later with an unpleasant surprise: the foreign merchants had changed their minds and had no intention of buying silk before the year ended. They claimed that letters from home reported ample stocks and advised suspending purchases. In Gu's view, however, they had grown shrewd. They knew the Chinese business custom of settling accounts at the three major festivals and making a final reckoning at year-end, when large sums of ready money were needed. They meant to “slaughter the New Year pig.” Yet if Hu did not hold out for too high a price, their interest in future dealings would keep them from driving him entirely to the wall. “This makes things difficult!” Hu told Liu Bucai. “My own need for funds is secondary. Many small dealers won't get through the year. They'll have to turn round and beg the foreigners. Their small quantities won't affect the whole market, but we Chinese will lose face!” Liu now admired him so completely that he thought no difficulty could defeat him. His answer was therefore quite casual. “There's only one solution. Raise some money to help the small dealers: buy their goods or lend against them. Keep them out of the foreigners' trap, and the problem is solved.” That had been Hu's original plan. The difficulty was the lack of money. He could only smile bitterly. Liu understood. “Old Hu, I think Pang Er responds to persuasion but resists pressure. He won't swallow such behaviour from the foreigners. Why not discuss it with him? He has ample resources. If he agrees to your plan, won't that see you through?” True enough. But Hu also had many public and private accounts of his own to settle. What was he to say about those? The whole matter required careful consideration.

That night Hu and Liu Qingsheng worked over the accounts until morning. They extended whatever payments could be extended and postponed whatever could be postponed, but still needed three hundred thousand taels to get through the year. Receivables and funds that could be mobilised amounted to less than a hundred and fifty thousand. Half the required sum was still missing, even before finding year-end cash for the small silk dealers. He had reached the point of having to submit to the foreign merchants. The thought was intolerable. So much time and effort had gone into securing the advantage of a unified grip on the business. With Pang's support, that advantage was finally taking shape. Yet he could not maintain his grip and would still lose to the foreigners. How could this be? Unwilling to yield, with the year's end fast approaching and time running out, he worried through the night without finding an answer. Anxiety brought grey hairs to his temples. Liu Qingsheng added a warning: failure to pay what was due would look very bad. Hu understood what that meant. If word spread that he was short of working funds, Fukang's depositors would rush to withdraw their money. Such a run would make matters worse and force the bank to close. He thought again of Liu Bucai's suggestion. Pang Er seemed a man with whom one could share adversity. Better to let one of their own profit than the foreigners. Asking him for help would certainly cost face, but the same principle applied: better to lose face before one of their own. “Third Master, come with me to Huzhou,” he said. “This journey will depend on my luck. If Pang's family dispute has been settled smoothly, I can speak more easily. If his own affairs are in a muddle, how can I open my mouth?” “Very well. I think we should go straight to Nanxun. Going to Huzhou first and then doubling back would waste time.” Hu nodded without committing himself. He was considering the arrangements needed in both Hangzhou and Shanghai. On reflection, even three or five days were hard to spare. Business at year-end might not change every instant, but unexpected developments were frequent. If something happened while he was unprepared, it could ruin everything. Seeing his hesitation and understanding that he must remain in charge in Hangzhou, Liu ventured, “Xueyan, why don't I go in your place?”

That offered a solution. Liu had the ability to handle such a mission, but one concern remained. “Third Master, when you arrive, you mustn't look desperate...” “Surely you don't need to tell me that!” Liu interrupted. “I understand that much.” “No, there's more. If it doesn't seem desperately urgent, there will seem to be time to spare. You know that rich young master's temper better than anyone.” Imitating Pang, Hu said, “‘All right, all right. Leave everything to me. Let's have a game first.’ What will you do then?” Liu saw the point. Once they started gambling, he might delay Hu's business. He must not play. “I'll tell him I have a great deal to attend to in Hangzhou and must hurry back. On New Year's Eve I'll come to Nanxun and play as many games as he likes.” “Exactly. Say just that.” Hu added solemnly, “Third Master, you mustn't let me down!” “If you don't trust me, don't send me.” After that, Hu could say no more. He wrote the letter and hired a boat, adding extra hands to speed it on its way through the night to Nanxun. They agreed that, successful or not, Liu must return in three days.

Those three days naturally seemed like years. But Hu would never sit helplessly with his troubles and leave everything to fate. He needed a fallback. Each morning he took his place at Fukang and kept sending people out to make contacts and test possibilities, hoping to find some means of raising the large sum. The first two days produced nothing. On the third he was beginning to lose his composure. While he sat frowning in thought, Ji Heling called at the bank. “Xueyan!” Ji exclaimed. “It's only been a few days since I saw you. How have you grown so thin?” There was no need to conceal anything from a sworn brother. “I'm still three hundred thousand taels short,” Hu said frankly. “How could I help worrying myself thin?” Ji was shocked. “Why didn't you tell me? When I asked the other day, didn't you say you could put everything in order?” There was reproach in his voice. “What good would it do to make you anxious too?” Hu adopted a reassuring tone. “As long as you can keep that outstanding Sea Transport Bureau account from being pressed, the rest isn't too serious.” Not serious? Plainly he was trying to put Ji at ease. After thinking a moment, Ji asked, “But you must be doing something?” “Yes.” Hu explained Liu's mission to Nanxun. “In my letter, I offered to sell Pang a quantity of silk at the current market price, so the benefit would stay among ourselves. Since I'm accepting a loss and swallowing my pride, he ought to help.” “Raising three hundred thousand at once at the year's end is no easy matter.” “Actually, half would see me through.” “A hundred and fifty thousand is no small sum either.” Ji beckoned. “Come here. I have something to tell you.”

In a secluded spot, Ji passed on news from Qiu Fengyan. A foreign merchant had approached Gong Zhenlin of the Cannon Bureau and his son Gong Zhitang. They in turn had approached Governor Huang's third concubine, and a purchase of fifteen thousand foreign guns had been agreed at thirty-two taels apiece, with sixty per cent paid in advance. The contract was to be signed and the money paid within the next day or two. Hu was astonished. Buying foreign guns had been his own initiative. If the trial proved satisfactory and a large purchase followed, surely he, as the original agent, should handle it. Why had the business changed hands, with the Gongs acting as intermediaries? Obviously there must be some trick behind it. “Do you know the merchant's name?” he asked. “No. I hear he's Prussian.” “Then it isn't Hudson. There's at least twelve taels of padding in the price of each gun. On fifteen thousand guns, that's a hundred and eighty thousand, before counting any commission. Their consciences must be very black to handle public business like this.” “Leave that aside. I'm telling you so that you can consider whether the money might pass through your hands. If that could be arranged, wouldn't it solve your immediate difficulty?” It was a fresh suggestion. Hu always took an interest in an idea that had not occurred to him. Counting on his fingers, he said, “Fifteen thousand times thirty-two makes four hundred and eighty thousand taels. Sixty per cent in advance is two hundred and eighty-eight thousand. If I could get roughly half of that, it would be about enough.” “Do you know the Gongs? A friend of mine knows the younger Gong well and could introduce you.” “Excellent! Let me think. This avenue is bound to be useful.”

A little thought revealed a weakness in the transaction. Fifteen thousand foreign guns were a consignment of arms that would attract enormous attention. The Small Swords Society nearby in Shanghai and the Taiping forces farther away in Jinling would certainly covet them. Whether sent from Shanghai by land or water, they would be vulnerable along the route. “If only we could discover what the contract says,” Hu murmured. “I suspect something isn't sound here.” “Not sound? The information is absolutely certain.” “I don't doubt that the transaction exists. I mean it may go wrong and land the Gongs in serious trouble.” He explained his concerns. “I see,” Ji said. “The crucial point is where delivery takes place. If it's Shanghai, Governor Huang will have to send a strong escort. That would be troublesome.” “I have it!” Hu summoned Liu Qingsheng. “Have we sent Second Master Liu at the governor's yamen his festival gift?” “It's still early.” “Send it ahead of time. I recall it's a hundred taels at each festival and two hundred at New Year. Put another four hundred in a separate envelope and send both. Tell him I have a favour to ask and beg him to take special care.” The favour was to obtain a copy of the original draft contract. For two red envelopes containing banknotes worth six hundred taels, Liu Er could hardly refuse. Besides, he resented the Gongs for bypassing him. As people from the same native place, their womenfolk called frequently on the governor's household, and they had used that advantage to approach the third concubine directly. Liu guessed that Hu meant to find a way to stop the purchase, a prospect he welcomed. He was particularly obliging. That same day, fifty taels bought the cooperation of Huang Zonghan's young male favourite, who also served as a junior attendant in the official signing room. The attendant stole out the draft. Liu shut the door, copied it himself, and delivered the copy to Hu in complete secrecy.

The contract specified delivery by boat at the Zhejiang border. Hu was puzzled. What remarkable powers did this Prussian, Rudolf, possess that would let him pass safely along the waterway from Shanghai to Jiashan? If trouble prevented delivery, what would happen? A closer reading revealed a tremendous loophole. The seller had guarantors, two Shanghai banks, but their guarantee covered only shortages in quantity and discrepancies in specifications. There was also a special clause: “After the seller has transported the guns from overseas to Shanghai, the Zhejiang governor's yamen shall be requested to appoint commissioners to inspect them jointly at the customs house. Only after the specifications and quantities have been verified as conforming may the goods be released for onward shipment.” It looked like careful administration, wholly intended to protect the buyer. In reality, it provided precisely the means for the seller to escape responsibility. “What skilful legal drafting!” Ji, studying the contract beside him, could not suppress a scholar's indignation. “This is how public business is done! They think themselves clever and fall into someone else's trap.” “Perhaps they're pretending to be clever,” Hu replied. “Under this contract, once the guns reach Shanghai and the Zhejiang commissioners have verified their quantity and specifications at customs, the seller has discharged his responsibility. If they're robbed on the way, it's like the rule on a pawn ticket: ‘The pawnbroker is not liable for natural or human disasters.’ The guarantors don't cover war risks. If a dispute arises, the foreigner need only say, ‘They were stolen in your country. If you can't maintain order in your own territory, what has that to do with us?’ We couldn't refute him. We'd have to hold our noses and swallow it.” Ji, himself exceptionally able and perceptive, nodded repeatedly. A deep, cryptic smile appeared at the corners of his mouth. His eyes kept blinking, as though he had reached some further understanding.

“Elder Brother, have you another thought?” Hu asked. “I'm taking your argument a step further. Perhaps it's a base suspicion, but the others may not be honourable men. My guess needn't be wrong.” What, after all that, did he mean? Hu grew impatient. “Tell me quickly! There may be some way forward in this. Time is short. We must think and act fast.” Ji needed only one question to make his point. “Could it be a fraud?” Under the contract, fraud was certainly possible. The guns would reach Shanghai customs, and Zhejiang's commissioners would report the quantity and specifications correct. During onward shipment, the seller could claim robbery, an unforeseen calamity for which he bore no responsibility. Whether the guns had actually been stolen would be impossible to establish. That opened the way to a swindle. With collusion arranged beforehand, the commissioners might merely go through the motions. Even if the quantity were short and the specifications wrong, they could report conformity. Once the goods were said to have been stolen, investigation would be equally impossible. That would be an even greater fraud. Hu was, after all, a businessman. Hesitantly he said, “Surely they wouldn't dare go that far?” “Ha!” Ji sneered. “You don't know how filthy official life is! Here are the facts: there's a loophole in the contract. Why should everyone else be less intelligent than we are? We saw it at a glance. So many people have examined it. Who could believe none of them noticed?” “Yes.” Hu nodded, then asked the essential question. “Now that we've seen it, what should we do?” Ji smiled. “With your intelligence, why ask me? Decide on a plan, and I'll see how I can help.” Hu thought Ji remained a gentleman. Even with the closeness of sworn brotherhood between them and time pressing so urgently, he would not suggest an underhanded scheme when the opportunity arose. That was rare indeed.

“There are plenty of ways,” Hu said after a little thought. “A man of the world doesn't cut off another's livelihood. If they aren't committing fraud, let them keep the business. Still, Governor Huang shouldn't treat me this way. I've helped him often enough, and I opened the way to buying foreign guns. To see someone else given the transaction now—it's hard to accept.” Ji understood the way Hu's words advanced and retreated. So long as the matter helped him through his immediate difficulty, he did not want further trouble. On that basis, Ji thought they ought to negotiate with the Gongs. “Who should negotiate?” Hu asked. “Your friend?” “No. Neither of us should appear at this stage. Send Qiu Fengyan.” “Excellent!” Hu clapped his hands. “Let's get him here at once.” They used Ji's house, where Ruiyun set out a brazier and wine, and sent for Qiu. It was late and cold. Having drunk himself pleasantly tipsy at dusk, Qiu had long since gone to bed. But the invitation to a late supper round the brazier brought him up at once. He dressed and hurried eagerly to Ji's through the bitter north-westerly wind. On entering, he altered the poetic line “On a cold night, a guest arrives; tea takes the place of wine,” and declaimed, “On a cold night, a guest arrives; wine takes the place of tea!” Ji and Hu exchanged smiles. Even Ruiyun in the next room laughed. A little maid lifted the door curtain, and Ruiyun appeared with a bottle in one hand and a wine pot in the other. Raising them, she said, “Mr Qiu, there's plenty! Chinese wine and foreign wine—drink all you please!”

“My thanks, Madam!” Qiu bowed deeply, took a bottle of brandy, pulled out the stopper, and drank straight from its mouth. Ruiyun laughed again. “Mr Qiu makes drinking so easy. There's no need to prepare food!” “You might say that elsewhere, but I won't let you say it here.” “Why not?” “Because I'd lose by it. At the risk of being laughed at, I've spent the last few days positively drooling over the thought of your sea conch and chicken in red wine lees.” “Then you are fortunate. We happen to have both today.” She smiled. “Only I'm ashamed to serve them to guests. They've already been eaten from.” “What does that matter? Coming here is like coming home. Nobody objects to leftovers in his own house.” Using what was ready, Ruiyun prepared a hotpot and four small dishes for the three men's supper. As Ji urged Qiu to drink, he explained the faults in the gun contract. Though he said nothing of Hu, Qiu, already a little tipsy and always friendly towards him, felt indignant on his behalf. “If this can be tolerated, what cannot? We must have the rights and wrongs of this properly argued!”

“Calm yourself.” Ji took his hand. “We invited you precisely to discuss how to put this injustice right. We've found the weakness, but you don't undertake to mend porcelain without a diamond drill. The Gongs aren't easy men to deal with. We must discuss it calmly.” “Very well!” Qiu drank, put a piece of chicken in his mouth, and said indistinctly as he chewed, “With you two here, I've no opinion to offer. You discuss it. I'll drink and listen.” Ji and Hu exchanged a glance. Even an honest fellow could be hard to manage. They had planned to play off one another, approaching the matter indirectly until Qiu volunteered. Now that he was placing himself entirely at their disposal, further circumlocution would be unfriendly. It left them in an awkward position. Naturally Ji had to speak. After a moment's thought, he looked at Hu. “There is one rather forceful approach. But it's a matter of drawing the bow and holding the threat. It won't produce an immediate result.” “Never mind. Tell us.” “Qiu has already handled the collection and transport of foreign guns, so he can claim experience. He might submit a memorandum saying that a British merchant has approached him and is willing to sell guns to Zhejiang on exactly the same terms, but at only twenty-five taels apiece. Let's see what they say.” “An excellent plan!” Qiu, despite his promise of silence, could not contain himself. “With that memorandum before him, Governor Huang couldn't shield them. Otherwise a censor might impeach him and the court send a high official to investigate secretly. I'd come forward then and see how he explained himself!” “It won't go that far. Once the memorandum is submitted, the Gongs are certain to approach you. Then there'll be something to discuss.” Ji turned to Hu. “Any benefit will come after the New Year.” Not understanding the purpose, Qiu said, “After the New Year, then! It's only a few days away.” Ji slowly raised his eyes to Hu, questioning and prompting him to make some explanation. Hu understood but did not want to reveal his real purpose. It would do no good with Qiu. An excellent fellow in other ways, he could not manage too many threads in serious business. If told, he might only find it troublesome and say, “Make it short! I can't remember all that.” Why invite a rebuff? Hu therefore said only, “Whenever it produces results, this can only benefit Qiu. Let's submit it first and see.” “Very well.” Ji turned back. “What do you think?”

“Ask me nothing beyond the wine cup!” Qiu answered in something like a line of verse, his drunken eyes misty as he reached again for the bottle. “No more!” Ji caught his hand. “You mustn't get drunk when there's work to do. Once it's finished, I'll give you a bottle to take home.” Reluctantly Qiu let go. Ruiyun, alert to the situation from the next room, promptly cleared away the remaining food and wine. She brought a pot of rice gruel simmered with a roast-duck carcass and four accompaniments. With ivory-coloured winter-pickled vegetables, Qiu ate three bowls. “How comfortable!” he said, rubbing his stomach. “Full of wine and food—now for business. Are we drafting the memorandum?” “Exactly. Can you still wield a brush?” “Why not? ‘A measure of wine, a hundred poems from Li Bai.’ Surely I can manage one plain memorandum!” “Good. Have some tea, smoke a couple of pipes, and rest while Xueyan and I discuss it.” They moved to the window and spoke quietly. Some things could not be said before Qiu. First they must consider the consequences for him personally.

“Once the memorandum goes in, Governor Huang will hate Qiu. He may suffer for it later. As his friends, we must consider that.” “Of course. But, Elder Brother, I see it a little differently. Governor Huang has always responded to pressure better than courtesy. This might even make him regard Qiu with new respect.” Ji reflected. “Leave that aside for now. The memorandum must at least look genuine.” “We must be prepared to make it genuine. If we actually take over the transaction, we can certainly supply guns at twenty-five taels each and still make a profit.” Once that was clear, drafting was easy. They moved the inkstone towards the lamp. Ji and Hu considered each point and dictated it, and Qiu wrote each one down. Then they reviewed the whole and settled on a final text. Qiu would have three fair copies made, one for the governor and one for the provincial treasurer. This must be done the next morning. “Very well. Leave all that to me. Now, the next step: once it's submitted, what should I say if Governor Huang sends for me?” “He won't,” Ji replied with complete confidence. “It will be the Gongs who come.” “I still need something to say.” “There's no hurry. When they come to you, come to me.” “By the time I come to you, your New Year expenses will be provided for,” Hu added. Feeling this was ill-judged, he immediately laughed. “I'm joking. Whatever happens, don't worry about this New Year. Leave everything to me!” “Thank you, thank you!” Qiu beamed. “To tell the truth, with friends like you two, I had no need to worry anyway.” He rose to leave. Hu did not detain him and left Ji's house with him. They agreed to meet there again at supper the following evening, whether there was news or not.

Moved by their appreciation, Qiu exerted himself unusually hard. Back home, he did not return to bed. Fortunately, the effects of the foreign liquor wore off readily. After washing his face and drinking two cups of strong tea, he felt clear-headed. He trimmed the lamp, rubbed the ink, and resolved to copy the memorandum properly so that he could take it to the governor's yamen first thing in the morning. His activity disturbed his stout wife's sleep. “You wretch!” she scolded affectionately from behind the bed curtains. “In the middle of the night, in weather this cold, why don't you drag yourself to bed? What are you up to?” “Go back to sleep. I have official business.” This was news indeed. Qiu spent his days being busy about nothing and had never put brush to paper on official work. To claim it at such an hour was extraordinary. “Tell that to the ghosts! What official business? You're playing some foolish trick for amusement again. New Year's nearly here. Think about that! How are you going to get through New Year's Eve?” “It's precisely to get through it that I'm working myself to death. Stop bothering me. Once I finish, I still have to go to the governor's yamen.” That could hardly be a prank. Half disbelieving, and unwilling to let her old companion work himself to death alone, she rose and came over. Seeing “Memorandum” on the cover of the plain folded paper, she finally believed him. “Go to bed,” he said, rubbing his hands. “Why stand here freezing with me?” “How could I sleep alone while you're doing official work?” Her words made him feel delightfully light. He reached out and gently pinched her cheek, soft as starch paste, then took up his brush and wrote rapidly with his head bent. His small regular script was both swift and elegant. He finished by the fifth watch. His wife urged him to sleep a little, but he refused. After an early cup of liquor to keep out the cold, he walked, fortified by it, to the governor's yamen, found Liu Er, and explained his errand.

Qiu's amiability made him well liked. Liu Er was accustomed to joking with him, punning on “Master Qiu” by calling him “Uncle.” “It's winter already,” he laughed. “The autumn wind has long passed. These two memoranda of yours won't do any good!” “Does submitting a memorandum always mean coming to cadge money? I haven't troubled you once this year. You must help me this time.” “How?” “Put it into the governor's hands at once—and ask him to read it at once too.” “Is it that urgent?” Liu was doubtful. “Tell me what it's about first.” From Ji and Hu, Qiu knew that Liu resented the Gongs. There was no need to conceal this from him. He beckoned him aside and whispered, “I have a client who wants to sell foreign guns. If it succeeds, there'll naturally be a share of the commission for you.” “Foreign guns!” Thinking it over and taking Qiu's connection with Hu into account, Liu understood. He answered without hesitation, “I see. Where shall I send word if there's news?” That stumped Qiu. What sort of news? If it meant a summons from Governor Huang, Ji had said none would come. Perhaps the prediction was uncertain after all, and he ought to prepare. “You mean the governor might send for me?” he asked after a moment. “I'm hard to find. I'll come again this afternoon.” “Very well. If there's news and I'm out, I'll leave a message. If there's none, you can go home.” With that settled, Qiu returned to make up his lost sleep. He did not wake until two in the afternoon, when his wife handed him a letter from Hu asking him to come to Fukang at three. They had agreed to meet at Ji's that evening. There must be a reason for the earlier appointment. Qiu hurriedly washed, rinsed his mouth, and went out.

The first person he met at Fukang was Chen Shilong, an old acquaintance. After a few words of greeting, he went to Hu and found him looking radiant and delighted. “Well!” Qiu exclaimed. “You look like a bridegroom today!” Hu smiled, ignored the remark, and asked only, “Has that thing been submitted?” “When I got home last night...” Qiu described the whole business, with no intention of boasting, simply to show how sincerely he had worked for his friends. “Excellent! Matters settle better when we take our time. Trouble yourself to go once more and see whether there's news. After that, we'll drink at Ji's this evening.” “Certainly. I'll go now. But there's something I don't understand. Did you specially ask me here now just to ask that?” “Yes. If you hadn't sent it in, I meant to hold it back until I'd been to Shanghai and learned more about that Prussian. Since it's already gone, that's fine too.” Qiu was more puzzled. “Why this sudden thought of Shanghai? When are you leaving?” “No date is fixed, but within the next day or two. Oh!” Remembering something, Hu took a red envelope from his pocket and pushed it into Qiu's hand. “Hurry home and report to your stout wife, so she can start buying for the New Year.” Qiu opened it and found a banknote for four hundred taels. Gratitude mingled with a sense of unworthiness, and his eyes reddened. Hu would not let him say anything. Gently urging him away, he said, “Off you go! I won't keep you. See you at Ji's later.”

Chen's unexpected arrival had surprised Hu, but the explanation was simple: Liu Bucai had pressed him into service. Negotiations at the Pang household had gone fairly smoothly, largely because of Hu's own earlier efforts. His two letters had naturally led Wang Youling to treat Pang with special consideration and instruct his legal secretary to mediate the inheritance dispute. The plaintiff, a distant uncle of Pang's, saw that with the prefect intervening, continuing the lawsuit would bring him no advantage. It would be foolish to reject a cup offered courteously only to drink one imposed as punishment, so he accepted mediation. Pang had already said that he did not mind spending a little, provided he could avoid litigation and appearing in court. That would put his mind at rest. After reading Hu's letter and hearing Liu's explanation, he agreed at once to help. But with the year's end so close, and himself in Nanxun, assembling a large sum in cash immediately was difficult. “I'll find a way. I certainly will! Don't trouble yourself. Let's enjoy a game first.” Exactly as Hu had predicted. Liu thought that refusal would offend Pang and might undo the good result. Yet sitting down to gamble would delay Hu's business. Then he hit upon a way to satisfy both. “Brother Pang, I've been entrusted with something and must see it through. I ought to hurry back. But since you want me to stay and play, I'm reluctant to leave too. Let me enjoy it properly, without something weighing on my mind. How about this...” He paused as though considering, then spoke as though nothing could be taken on trust and this was the only arrangement that would do. “Settle what Xueyan asked you to do, and I'll get someone from Huzhou to take the news back!” “Good!” Pang agreed readily. “Sit here. I'll ask in the counting house.” Liu would not waste time while he was away. He wrote at once and asked the Pang household to send someone to Huzhou for Chen Shilong, who was to come and await instructions.

“There is some silver in the house,” Pang said, “but I must keep it for New Year gambling and for poor friends and relations who come to borrow. I can't give that to Old Hu. I have several hundred thousand owing to me in Shanghai. I'll assign him two hundred and fifty thousand, but he'll have to collect it himself. Two debts may prove uncollectable. That will depend on his ability.” “Excellent!” Liu was thoroughly satisfied. “On his behalf, thank you for such a great favour. But how is this money to be treated? Do you want goods for it, or something else? Tell me so that I can notify Xueyan.” “What goods? It's a loan. He can repay me when he's sold that consignment of silk.” “Yes. And the interest?” “None!” “Surely that's too much to ask...” Liu hesitated. Pang lowered his voice. “Old Liu, we get on well, so I'll speak frankly. Two of those debts, amounting to perhaps seventy or eighty thousand, will be hard to collect. I hear Old Hu is very close to You Laowu of the Songjiang Grain Transport Brotherhood. If he asks You to collect them, we should recover seventy per cent, even if we can't get it all. I'd be deeply grateful for that. I'll thank You separately, of course. I'll discuss it all with Hu when we meet.”
