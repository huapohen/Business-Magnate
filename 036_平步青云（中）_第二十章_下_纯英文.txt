INSTALLMENT 036
A Swift Rise to Prominence, Volume II — Chapter Twenty, concluding part

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

Chen Shilong was eager to oblige. The moment he received the letter, he went to Nanxun. Liu Bucai was already at the pai gow table and could spare no time to write. Drawing Chen aside, he explained everything, gave him Pang Er's documents authorizing collection of the debts, and made sure he understood every detail. Chen then took the fast boat Liu had hired and travelled through the night to Hangzhou. A weight lifted from Hu Xueyan's heart. Yet matters were still complicated enough to require a journey to Shanghai, while miscellaneous business in Hangzhou also needed his attention. The unexpected discovery of the foreign gun transaction was a particular concern. Properly handled, it could become a substantial piece of business, and closer dealings with the foreigners would help his silk trade. Handled badly, it could offend Governor Huang and the Gongs. If their resentment spread to Wang Youling and Ji Heling, the consequences would be grave. He could hardly leave it unattended. Seeing his dilemma, Chen volunteered. “Mr Hu, if it's something I can manage, let me make the journey.” Hu considered this. It was a possible solution. “You couldn't do it alone. You'll have to ask You Laowu.” His decision made, he continued firmly, “Go to Songjiang first. Whatever happens, get him to stay with you and help. I'll entrust the other business to Old Gu.” They talked throughout the evening, until Hu had made every instruction clear. Chen left early the next morning. That same day, Qiu Fengyan's memorandum produced a response. Early in the morning, a large sedan chair covered in blue woollen cloth arrived outside Qiu's house. An attendant took a visiting card from his card case and handed it to the doorkeeper.

The doorkeeper had already been instructed. One glance at the card, and he replied that his master was out, although Qiu had only just risen. The visitor left, but his card remained: Gong Zhenlin, executive director of the Cannon Bureau, had called. Highly excited, Qiu hurried straight to Fukang Bank. “Heling reads the signs like a master diviner!” he told Hu. “Look—the elder Gong has come to seek my counsel, just as he predicted.” “Did you see him?” “Of course not. One meeting would have ruined everything. If he had asked me anything, I wouldn't have known what to say. Truly, ‘Let them question me, and my life is forfeit!’” “It isn't as terrible as all that. Don't be frightened. Come, let's go to Heling's.” There was little to do at the Sea Transport Bureau at the end of the year, so Ji was enjoying a quiet time at home. Seated by a sunny window on this winter day, he was teaching his little son to recognize characters. At the sight of Qiu's expression, he smiled. “There must be news.” “Indeed there is! All the way here I've been worrying. I've never tried a bluff like this before. I don't know whether I can carry it off.” His concern was that neither the proposed transaction nor the supposed merchant behind it actually existed. A question about the foreigner's whereabouts would be enough to put him in difficulty. Under Hu and Ji's guidance, however, this was easy enough to manage. They carefully coached him in what to say, and at last a real smile appeared on his face.

“I must return his call. I'll need to borrow your chair and your steward.” Ji had yet to answer when Hu objected. “No! That would give the game away.” “Quite right, quite right. Never mind. I'll manage.” Qiu had plenty of friends himself. He borrowed a chair elsewhere, took his own doorkeeper along as an attendant, and thus equipped went to return the call at the Cannon Bureau. As soon as his card was presented, Gong Zhenlin opened the main gate to receive him. Gong's home lay behind the bureau. To show his friendliness, he conducted Qiu into his private quarters and first summoned his son, Gong Zhitang, to pay his respects. The young man addressed Qiu repeatedly as “Uncle” and behaved with the greatest deference. “Brother Fengyan, I've long admired your fame as an immortal of the wine cup. I'm fond of a drink myself and have some excellent vintages. Now that two kindred drinkers have met, you mustn't leave sober.” “I really ought not to impose on you like this!” “You're treating me as a stranger.” Gong told his son, “Go and see where Uncle Qiu's steward is. Have his bundle of clothes brought in.” “No need, no need. I'd intended to stay only a moment, so I brought no informal clothes.” “In that case...” Gong glanced from his guest to his son. “Zhitang, you're much the same size as Uncle Qiu. Fetch one of your fur robes and help him change.” Drinking in official dress would be rather stiff, Qiu reflected, so he dispensed with polite refusals. When Zhitang had a maid bring the robe, he put it on. It was a new lambskin robe of the kind commonly called ‘shredded radish’, exceptionally light and warm, and it fitted perfectly.

Before the wine came tea: Wuyi tea from Fujian, prepared to perfection and served in exquisite, spotless utensils. Qiu was at ease wherever he found himself. Though he and Gong had previously been no more than nodding acquaintances and this was his first visit, he behaved like an old guest, tasting the tea and admiring the utensils in complete comfort. Father and son deliberately avoided business, concentrating instead on their attentions to him, as though they could become intimate friends in a few moments. Before they had finished their tea, two servants carried in a jar of wine. “You're a great connoisseur,” Gong said. “Come and have a look.” Judging from the ceremony, Qiu expected a rare vintage. He rose gladly and accompanied Gong into the corridor. It was a twenty-five-jin jar of huadiao wine. The painted designs had faded badly, and dust had turned the clay seal grey. A faint inscription appeared beneath the dust. When this was brushed away, it read: “Made in the twelfth month of the thirteenth year of Daoguang.” “Oh!” Qiu exclaimed. “A full twenty years!” “Yes. I've had it myself for five or six. There were two jars altogether. We opened one for my mother's seventieth birthday the year before last. This one has waited for a guest worthy of its opening.” Naturally touched, Qiu bowed deeply in thanks. Yet he felt uneasy. Hu and Ji had not anticipated such lavish kindness. Once he owed a return for all this generosity, it would be difficult to deal harshly with his host.

While he hesitated, the servants removed the clay seal and the bamboo covering beneath it. “Have a look, Mr Qiu.” He leaned forward. Light fell directly into the jar, revealing that only half the wine remained and that its surface was covered with white growth. This confirmed how old it was. Qiu knew his wine: he nodded. “That's how it should be.” With a copper ladle, a servant carefully skimmed away the white growth, then poured the wine with equal care into a large green porcelain jar, leaving the sediment behind. He opened a ten-jin jar of new wine and added it. Immediately, the fragrance of fermented grain filled the air, and Qiu swallowed involuntarily. They went back inside and took their places. The Gong household's Fujian dishes were even more elaborate than those served at Wang Youling's. Qiu was in his element. Encouraged by father and son together, he drained three cups in succession. A springlike warmth stirred within his chest, and comfort spread through his whole body. When young Gong urged a fourth cup upon him, he declined. “It tastes mild, but it has a powerful kick. One shouldn't drink it too quickly. It wouldn't do to get drunk.” “You're far too modest, Uncle. You must have one more. I'll drink first, as a mark of respect.” Zhitang gulped down his wine in one draught and tilted the empty cup towards his guest. Qiu had no choice but to follow suit. The Gongs understood what he meant, of course: he wanted to discuss business before he became drunk. And it was indeed time to begin.

“Yesterday, when I called at the governor's yamen, His Excellency mentioned a memorandum of yours,” Gong began casually. “He praised it most warmly. Men as zealous and capable as you, he said, are exceedingly rare in official life these days.” “His Excellency is too generous,” Qiu answered calmly. “He is a man willing to get things done. Otherwise, I shouldn't have ventured to submit it.” “Quite so. Our governor certainly has determination. But accomplishing anything is difficult. Take this gun purchase: it comes with powerful backing from the capital. Who knows what connections that Prussian has, to reach a Grand Councillor himself? Naturally, that makes a difference to the price. His demands were outrageous. The governor entrusted this thankless commission to me, and it took endless bargaining to bring the price down to its present level. I've made myself the villain, and people outside still criticize me. Neither side is pleased. Others may not understand what I've suffered, but you surely will.” Qiu reflected that Gong was trying to overawe him with a great name. Whether the story was true he could not tell, and there was no point in challenging it now. Fortunately, Hu had given him a simple guiding principle: neither agree nor disagree. He nodded and answered with a single “Oh!” He did not even ask which Grand Councillor was involved.

“Now, may I ask whether the British merchant named in your memorandum is Hudson?” This required an answer. “Yes.” “That's rather curious.” Gong looked at his son. “Hasn't Hudson gone home?” The remark was meant for Qiu, and it alarmed him. Even the wisest man could overlook something; for all Hu's ability, he would never have guessed that Hudson was no longer in China. If the whole invention were exposed at one stroke, what a predicament he would be in! Fortunately, three things helped him. First, wine had already reddened his face, concealing his embarrassment. Second, he had escorted a shipment of guns, visited Shanghai and dealt with foreigners; he was not wholly ignorant of the subject. Third, there remained one excuse he could use. “There must be some confusion,” he replied. “Many foreigners have the same first and last names. It was probably another merchant called Hudson. As for me, I haven't actually met Hudson over this transaction. The approach came through a comprador.” The word was a foreign borrowing. Chinese managers employed by foreigners to negotiate business on their behalf were called compradors. It was an impressively fashionable expression, and hearing that Qiu knew it made the Gongs regard him with new respect. “Perhaps so,” Zhitang said. Being younger, he was more candid. “It was the Prussian who told us. He must have said it deliberately, out of jealousy of a rival.”

“Exactly.” Gong turned back to Qiu. “When bargaining with this foreigner, I naturally cited Hudson's price to try to beat him down. If he would match Hudson, we could both oblige our superiors and justify the purchase on its merits. My only concern was Mr Hu. He had taken such trouble to introduce Hudson, and by the rules his offer ought to have had priority. Giving the opportunity to someone else wasn't right. But backing from the Grand Council cannot be ignored. We had no choice, so I made a particular point of mentioning this to the governor. His Excellency said at once that Mr Hu understood the larger interests involved and was always considerate of other people's difficulties. Though this occasion might do him a slight wrong, there would be others on which to make it up to him. In wartime there are many military procurement contracts; he must be accommodated next time. His Excellency also said, ‘Mr Hu has many business interests. Perhaps some other project could provide him with a transaction. In any case, there's no need to contest this particular opportunity.’” Gong expounded his account at leisurely length. Qiu drank, but his thoughts stayed on the matter at hand; he missed not a word. The supposed subject was bargaining with a foreign merchant, yet they had ended up talking about Hu Xueyan. Quite a departure from the point! There was no way to establish whether the governor had actually said these things, but the intention to placate Hu was unmistakable. Qiu could make no commitment himself. He could only keep to his rule of neither agreeing nor disagreeing, murmuring polite acknowledgements.

“So may I ask again: is this Hudson you know the same Hudson who sold Mr Hu the guns last time?” There was no evading that question. Qiu decided that admission was better than denial and nodded. “Yes.” “Then why has the price fallen, when last time he charged thirty taels apiece?” “Last time we approached him. This time he's coming to us for business. Naturally, he can't hold out for a scarcity price.” Pleased with his answer, Qiu decided to add fuel to the fire. “To tell you the truth, I could bargain him down another three or five taels.” “Oh! Oh!” Gong had maintained his composure throughout, but this remark seemed to leave him at a loss. Apparently realizing that it was unwise to let Qiu see his discomfiture, he rallied, recovered his calm and took a sip of wine. “There's something you may not like hearing, but I must tell you. Hudson's goods aren't necessarily very good. The bureau tested the guns you escorted back last time, and their aim was poor. I wonder whether the guns he's offering now are the same.” Qiu could not judge whether the allegation about poor aim was true or deliberately invented, but the latter part was easy to answer. “My memorandum states it quite clearly: goods identical to the Prussian's.” “That makes it harder to understand,” Gong replied. “No one else can obtain that particular stock.”

This was dangerous. Qiu thought that if they continued, every flaw in his story would show, and however good the performance, he could not keep it going. Instead of answering, he put a direct question to father and son. “May I ask whether the Prussian is here? Could I speak with him face to face?” This was a delaying tactic; Qiu merely wanted to stop discussing the matter with the Gongs. Gong, however, thought he really intended to question the foreigner, and grew anxious. There were so many concealed tricks in the business that one conversation would expose the whole deception. “The foreigner is in Shanghai at present,” he replied. “If you have anything to say, tell me and I'll certainly pass it on.” After a few more cups, Qiu declared loudly, “I want to ask what entitles him to demand such a price!” His words and tone were aggressive enough to make Gong's expression change. “As I told you, there is powerful backing in the capital. That makes things very difficult here.” His voice became conciliatory. “In everything, I can only ask for your understanding and Mr Hu's.” There was little more to say. Qiu could neither accept nor refuse. He simply nodded. “I understand what you mean.” They had reached something of an impasse. Gong could hardly compel him to abandon his plan and withdraw the memorandum. All he could do was play the attentive host, repeatedly urging wine upon him in the hope that he would depart in good spirits.

The drinking lasted four hours. Well advanced towards drunkenness, Qiu arrived at Ji's house, where Hu happened to be. His detailed account surprised Hu. “So a Grand Councillor is behind it! No wonder they dare act so openly. Elder Brother, what do you think we should do?” Ji knew official life far better than Hu. “There may be no powerful patron at all—only a powerful name to intimidate us. Don't let it crush you before we begin.” “Exactly,” Qiu agreed. “I find it hard to believe that a dignified Grand Councillor would introduce business for a foreign merchant.” “Even supposing there is such a patron, they can't behave as they please. We have ways of dealing with powerful backing, too, though then we'd be fighting in earnest.” “How, Elder Brother? What means have you in mind?” “The most direct is to have a censor submit an impeachment memorial. Then let's see whether they still dare talk about their powerful patron.” This was a ruthless move. Once such a memorial had been submitted, even if Huang Zonghan's connections in the capital protected him personally, the transaction would at least be certain to collapse. But it would also create an irreconcilable enmity. For as long as Huang remained in Zhejiang, there would be trouble. Wang Youling, too, would inevitably be implicated and could no longer get along in the province.

Of course, Ji was only saying this for the momentary satisfaction it gave him. It was Qiu who drew inspiration from it and produced an excellent idea. “We might ‘draw the bow without loosing the arrow’. Have someone make an inquiry: we've heard of such a matter but don't know the details—could you enlighten us? Then see what Gong says.” Ji was puzzled. “Whom would we ask to inquire?” “Why, a censor, naturally.” Now Ji and Hu understood. Drawing the bow without loosing the arrow meant appearing ready to investigate. Gong and Huang would be frightened into making a definite offer. “An excellent idea. But where shall we find a censor? A letter from the capital would come too late.” Qiu would not have proposed it without a man in mind. An investigating censor surnamed Xie had taken leave to return home for a parent's burial. His leave had expired, and he was waiting only for the New Year to set off again. He and Qiu were close friends through literature and wine. There was every reason to expect that he would oblige.

“What do you think?” Ji asked Hu. “I resent the Gongs' behaviour. They act without the least restraint, as though certain that no one would dare speak.” “I'm not nursing an idle grievance, and I'm not trying to make money out of this. But I'm dealing with foreigners now. My standing is involved.” Ji understood. He reflected that if Hu could overturn a transaction already settled with the governor's approval and bring in a different foreign merchant, the news would enormously enhance his influence in the foreign settlement. On the other hand, it would undoubtedly displease Huang and the Gongs. Once they acted, they were bound to make enemies. In those few moments, Hu had decided. “This is what I suggest. We go ahead, and the profit goes to Qiu. First, he's done most of the work. Second, it will put something aside for his old age. Or he might pay an additional contribution for an actual official appointment—a grander title with a real post—and satisfy his taste for office. Only we must settle things amicably when the time comes.” “You can't, however amicable you try to be,” Ji said. “Snatch food from someone's mouth, and how can he not resent it?” “I've considered that, of course. A man of the world doesn't cut off another's livelihood. If we succeed in doing this business, besides Qiu's share we must naturally look after the Gongs and Governor Huang.” “That's more like it.”

The matter was settled. In practice, it had become Qiu's affair, and he did the running about while Hu attended to his own business. Thanks to You Laowu's help and Gu Yingchun's skill, matters in Shanghai went fairly smoothly. Hangzhou's affairs were also brought into order. By the twentieth day of the twelfth month, almost everything was ready, and Hu could spare time for New Year preparations. On the day the Kitchen God was sent off to heaven, Qiu came eagerly to Fukang with good news. Gong had spoken frankly with him: the gun transaction would be handled jointly. His proposal was to divide the purchase between two contracts. Five thousand guns would be supplied by Hudson, with Qiu handling the transaction. The governor's yamen would take two taels per gun for expenses; the remaining benefit would be Qiu's. Hu calculated. There had originally been twelve taels of padding on each gun. Now they were retaining only the odd two taels. In other words, surrendering five thousand guns meant sacrificing fifty thousand taels. This was no small sum. How could Gong willingly hand it over? He was yielding under pressure and making a painful sacrifice. Surely he would bear a grudge and wait for a chance to retaliate. Having to shield Qiu from the consequences would hardly be worthwhile for Hu. With the memorandum already submitted, Gong had to offer something. He could hardly object to letting others drink the broth while he ate the meat. But this proposal cut into his very flesh; it went too far. Yet Hu had promised Qiu all the benefit. To protect Gong's interests now would be to cut into Qiu's flesh instead, and Qiu might resent it. Caught between the two, Hu felt that they had acted too lightly.

“What's the matter?” Qiu asked, puzzled by his expression. “Congratulations on your windfall,” Hu said tentatively. “I owe it all to your support. Naturally, everyone has a share. I'll leave the distribution to you.” This reassured Hu. So long as Qiu had no intention of keeping everything to himself, matters would be manageable. “Heling and I won't take anything. But money must be comfortable to hold. Money that burns the hand is no use. We need to consider Hudson's contract very carefully.” After a moment's thought, he asked, “Tell me honestly, Qiu: how much money would you like?” It was a difficult question. Qiu did not understand what Hu meant and had no idea how much he might have. “As I said, I'll leave it to you. Whatever you give me will be enough.” “Then I must explain from the beginning. They're sacrificing fifty thousand taels by giving up those five thousand guns, but we can't make that much from Hudson. Why not? Let me show you the figures.” The memorandum offered identical guns at twenty-five taels each. Their actual cost was twenty, leaving only five taels of padding per gun, or twenty-five thousand altogether. After deducting ten thousand for the governor's yamen, fifteen thousand remained.

“Fifteen thousand divided three ways,” Qiu volunteered when Hu had explained this much. “Five thousand each.” His expectations were so modest that Hu felt almost guilty. “Five thousand would be too little after all your trouble. Take ten thousand. Heling and I want nothing.” “And the other five thousand? Surely you don't mean to give it to Gong?” “Exactly. And besides giving him that five thousand, we must ask how much more he wishes to add on top. Only then will your money cease to burn your hand.” At first Qiu objected, but after Hu had explained his reasoning repeatedly, he saw the point and agreed to discuss it with Gong on those terms. Naturally, they needed a way to present the arrangement. Hudson, they would say, was willing to reduce his price by another tael per gun. Together with the two taels already allowed for the governor's yamen, that made a deduction of three from the quoted twenty-five: the guns would thus be reckoned at twenty-two taels apiece. This was the net amount their side would receive. If “the higher authorities have other expenses and need to add something on top, that is quite acceptable.”

Hearing these terms, Gong's view changed. He knew Hu stood behind Qiu. He had thought Hu excessively ruthless; now he discovered a man who handled matters most handsomely. Friendship mattered, but weighing interests mattered more. Gong understood the strength of Hu's circle quite well. Wang Youling had earned a reputation as an able official and enjoyed considerable favour with the governor. Ji Heling, too, was a name that carried weight in Zhejiang official circles. Both relied on Hu as their principal adviser. Hu was adaptable and resourceful, and his greatest advantage was that everyone gladly worked for him. To miss an opportunity to befriend such a man would be a pity. Having made up his mind, Gong told Qiu, “To be frank, this affair has put me in a very difficult position. I needn't explain all the complications. You and Mr Hu are perceptive enough to understand them. In a word, I am deeply obliged for your consideration and concessions, and shall certainly repay them.” Qiu found this very gratifying. “We're making friends,” he replied generously. “There's no need to speak of repayment.” “Indeed, indeed! The proverb is perfectly true: ‘At home, rely on your parents; away from home, on your friends.’ Whenever one can make a friend, one should. When this is settled, I shall thank you separately. And naturally I must also pay my respects to Mr Hu. What would you advise?” “If you offer him a gift, he'll certainly refuse.” An idea occurred to Qiu. “I can suggest a kindness that will cost you nothing.”

“Excellent. Please tell me.” “You know Fukang Bank, of course—a name of real standing among Hangzhou's major banks. You could do business with Fukang and give him your support.” “Nothing could be easier!” Gong repeated eagerly. “I'd also like to invite Mr Hu for a visit. Would you kindly make the introduction?” “Certainly. Hu loves making friends. He'll be glad to accept.” “Then that's settled.” Gong returned to the official business. “We'll handle Hudson's side as you propose. As for any expenses above us, I must consult the governor before I can tell you.” He lowered his voice, assuming the air of a man confiding in an intimate. “Think what the governor faces! At New Year he must attend to everyone, from the capital down to our own province, the garrison general and the education commissioner among them. He can't get through it on less than three hundred thousand taels. People truly ‘see the monk eating his porridge, but never see him enduring his vows’!” From this, Qiu knew that the amount added on top would not be small. He said no more and returned to Fukang to give Hu a detailed account. Before he had finished, Liu Qingsheng came in smiling, evidently with something pleasing to report.

“Mr Hu, an unexpected sum has come our way. There's no need to worry about New Year now. Mr Gong of the Cannon Bureau wants to open an account and deposit eighty thousand taels!” Qiu, too, was delighted. “Well?” he asked with a smile. “Don't celebrate yet.” Hu felt caution rather than pleasure. He told Liu, “This money isn't unexpected. If he wants it back at any moment, we must have it ready. We can't put it to use.” “No! He's specified three months, with the interest left to us.” “Then that's another matter.” Hu considered. “Naturally, we'll give him favourable interest. Issue a receipt to the person who brought it and say that I'll call on Mr Gong at once, bringing the passbook myself.” Liu agreed and went to see to it. Only now did Hu's pleasure show. With evident satisfaction, he told Qiu that the outcome was unexpectedly complete. His greatest fear had been that mending one thing would damage another: this affair might spoil Wang Youling's relationship with Governor Huang. Now, instead of damaging that relationship, they had added another bond. How could he not be pleased?

“Still, we mustn't get carried away,” Hu added. “We can't even call this speculative buying and selling. We've only been selling what we haven't got! We've undertaken everything, and still don't know what Hudson will say.” “Never mind. Didn't you say Hudson had agreed to twenty taels a gun? We now have two taels' leeway. At worst, I'll do the job for nothing. Surely we can settle at twenty-two.” It was a generous declaration. Hu smiled and patted his shoulder, then took the passbook to the Cannon Bureau to call on Gong. Naturally, they began with mutual expressions of admiration and found themselves in perfect accord. After they sat down and tea was served, Hu first handed over the passbook and expressed his thanks. He then spoke of Wang Youling's recent circumstances. Once this connection had been established, they felt closer still, truly like old friends at their first meeting. “Over Mr Qiu's memorandum, you've shown such understanding of our difficulties and done so much to help resolve them,” Gong said. “I'm deeply obliged. I've reported to the governor in person. He, too, was very gratified and specifically asked me to convey his regards.” Gong did not say precisely what those regards were, but he meant that Huang appreciated the favour as well. Hu smiled with reserve and said little.

“Though I hold this post at the Cannon Bureau, my knowledge of foreign affairs is limited. I shall need much guidance from you.” “You flatter me.” Hu abruptly turned to the point. “May I ask where delivery is specified in your contract with the Prussian?” “Hangzhou. He's undertaken to arrange the transport.” “Brother Zhenlin, have you considered conditions along the route from Shanghai?” “I know it isn't very peaceful, so I've already asked the governor to send troops to meet the shipment at the border.” “Once it reaches Zhejiang, there'll be nothing to worry about.” “Oh!” Gong's attention sharpened. “You mean the waterway through Jiangsu is unsafe?” “Yes. The Small Sword Society will certainly covet that shipment. I'm not trying to alarm you, but the foreigner's undertaking to transport it cannot be relied upon.” Gong drew in his breath, plainly troubled by doubts. He stared at Hu for some time without speaking. “Brother Zhenlin,” Hu said frankly, “if anything happens, the foreigner can disclaim responsibility, or ask the authorities to pursue the robbers. How would you fight that case?” “Ah!” Sweat covered Gong's forehead. Rising, he bowed deeply. “Thank you for pointing this out. I nearly made a grave mistake. The contract must be amended. We cannot leave transport to the foreigner. He cannot guarantee it.”

“Exactly. You understand now.” “I understand, but I'll still need your guidance on what to do. We may also have to consult Mr Qiu. He's escorted a shipment and knows the route better.” “There's no need to ask him. I can be of service.” “Excellent!” Gong bowed again. Hu hastily returned the courtesy. At this point, there was no need for further circumlocution. He explained his friendship with You Laowu directly and offered help if Gong could use it. “I certainly must rely on you!” Gong's joy and gratitude were unmistakable. “Without you, this could have become an endless disaster.” He next asked Hu to provide someone to work for him. In official circles, a request of this kind was the most concrete expression of a relationship. Unfortunately, Hu was short of men himself and had to decline with thanks. He did, however, offer another suggestion. The guns supplied by both merchants could travel together. Governor Huang could issue a commission appointing Qiu as escort commissioner again, with responsibility for liaising with You as well. Qiu would be doing familiar work, and they could rest easy. This arrangement would save both time and freight charges, so Gong naturally agreed. They found more and more common ground and talked until late into the night. The following day, Gong returned the visit at Hu's home and insisted that Old Mrs Hu be invited into the main hall so he could kowtow to her. That afternoon, Mrs Gong arrived with gifts. The two families quickly became close enough to visit one another's private households.

Hu did, however, see Gong in a different light, and not a favourable one. From his words and manner, Hu judged that self-interest mattered more to him than affection. As the saying went, he knew a man when he needed him and forgot him when he did not. Gong could not be placed alongside Wang Youling, You Laowu, Yu Si or Ji Heling. Hu therefore warned his wife to take particular care in dealing with the Gongs. No courtesy should be omitted, but anything with consequences for their affairs should be left unsaid, lest it cause trouble. And trouble did indeed come through the Gong household. On New Year's Eve, after the ancestral rites, the family sat down to their reunion dinner. Wearing a newly made coat of thick fur, Old Mrs Hu occupied the place of honour. As she looked at her daughter-in-law, she spoke with mingled happiness and emotion. “I never imagined we'd see a day like this! We owe it to our ancestors' virtue, but also to the truth that harmony in a family makes everything prosper. Xueyan, you must always remember the old saying: ‘Never forget the wife who shared your poverty.’ Keep your conscience in its proper place.” Hu found it unpleasant to hear this on New Year's Eve, but could only answer, “I know.”

His wife said nothing. She attended to everyone, old and young, until they had finished eating, then asked her husband, “Are you going out?” “No. Naturally, I'll stay at home to see the New Year in.” At this, Mrs Hu had a few delicate dishes prepared for his late supper. Husband and wife sat drinking quietly beside the brazier. When she saw that no one else was in the room, she said something that startled and alarmed him. “You heard what Mother said. Xueyan, you must keep your conscience in its proper place.” “This is extraordinary. What have I done to wrong you?” “Very well. Those are your own words. Once New Year is over, dismiss that woman in Huzhou.” Hu's heart gave a jump. He steadied himself and played the fool. “Which woman do you mean?” “Hmph! Still pretending innocence? So you intend to deceive me to the end. Must I say her name?” “Go on, then.” “Furong!”

“Oh...” Hu assumed the air of a man reminded of something long forgotten. “One has the occasional passing amusement. It's all in the past. Why bring her up? Tell me, who said this to you?” “Someone did, naturally!” His wife pressed him. “What do you mean, a passing amusement, all in the past? Are you saying she's no longer in Huzhou?” “How should I know whether she's in Huzhou?” Even as he spoke, Hu ran through his wife's usual relatives and friends one by one. Who among them could know of Furong? After considering them all, he could think of only one: Wang Youling's wife. Yet Mrs Wang was capable and discreet. Nothing would induce her to tell Mrs Hu out of mere gossip, unless... Suddenly he saw it. The Wangs and the Gongs came from the same home district, and their womenfolk often visited one another. Mrs Wang must have let the secret slip in casual conversation, and Mrs Hu had heard it from Mrs Gong. With her husband resolute in his denials, his wife could only let the subject drop for the time being. But an uneasiness now lay between them, and the New Year was less happy than they had imagined.
