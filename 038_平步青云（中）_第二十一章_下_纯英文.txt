INSTALLMENT 038
A Swift Rise to Prominence, Volume II — Chapter Twenty-One, concluding part

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

When the day of departure came, Hu Xueyan set off with his daughter and a maid. That night they moored outside Beixin Customs. Father and daughter sat by the lamp, eating snacks and chatting. Hu deliberately set out to win his daughter over, indulging Meiyu so thoroughly that she could not bear to leave him and kept refusing to go to bed. “Meiyu,” he asked when he judged the moment ripe, “do you know what makes life difficult for your father?” She nodded. “You're away all year round, Father. Of course you have a hard time.” “The hard work is the least of it. The worst thing is having no one to look after me wherever I go. This journey will be different, though. With you beside me, I shan't suffer.” “Then... I'll go with you whenever you travel.” “That would be lovely, but I'm afraid it isn't possible. Meiyu, will you be angry if I say something?” “No, Father. Say whatever you like.” “To tell the truth, a daughter is best at home, but a son is best on the road. If you were a boy, I'd take you wherever I went. Unfortunately, you aren't. Even if neither of us can bear to part, I can't take you on every journey. First, Grandmother and your mother would worry. Second, people would gossip. Whoever heard of a girl roaming the world? Third, it wouldn't be convenient for you, and you couldn't bear the hardships. So it can only be an occasional treat.” Meiyu said nothing, merely looking at him with troubled eyes.

“Let me ask you this. Suppose there were someone in one of the places I visit who could look after me in your place. What would you think?” Meiyu did not grasp his meaning. She simply answered instinctively, “Of course that would be good!” “Good girl!” Delighted, Hu patted her shoulder. “You're truly a good daughter to me.” Accordingly, the next day he told the boatman to make a detour to Huzhou before proceeding directly to Songjiang. “But Father,” Meiyu asked in bewilderment, “why have you suddenly decided to go to Huzhou?” “It's for your sake that I must go there.” This only puzzled her more. “For my sake?” At fifteen, Meiyu was beginning to think of love. Suddenly she wondered whether they were going to Huzhou because her father meant to arrange a marriage for her. The thought made her painfully shy: her face flushed, her heart raced, and she could scarcely get her words out. Now it was her father's turn to be surprised. Only after recalling what he had said did he realize that she had misunderstood. It was an amusing mistake, but he dared not laugh. Nor was this the moment to explain at length. Meiyu was too unsettled to consider his words carefully, and he would not achieve the effect he wanted.

“It's about something of my own,” he said. “I want you to help me decide.” So that was it! She had been entirely mistaken. She felt a little ashamed and a little disappointed, without quite knowing what to make of her feelings. One thing alone was clear: she dreaded her father's discovering her mistake. Fortunately, she could detect nothing unusual in his expression. Reassured, she composed herself and asked, “What do you want me to decide, Father?” “It's a long story. After we've eaten, I'll tell you everything.” They took a nap after the meal, and by the time they woke it was almost dark again. Heavy clouds covered the sky, promising snow. Hu ordered an early halt and sent the boatman ashore for a fresh fish and a large piece of mutton. Someone happened to pass with game from a hunt, and Hu bought a pheasant and a wild duck as well. Their supper was therefore sumptuous. “Today hasn't been bad,” he said slowly, his wine cup in his hand. “Don't imagine that travelling is always so comfortable. I'm in better spirits today because you're here. Sometimes, when I'm in a hurry, we pass the last place where we could have stopped and find ourselves beside some deserted village or desolate bank, with nothing to be had. Then I have to mix a bowl of soup from soy sauce and eat cold rice.” Was this how her father suffered on his travels? Meiyu's heart filled with pity. She said nothing, but her chopsticks slowed, picking at the grains without carrying them to her mouth. “Go on, eat!” Hu placed a piece of braised mutton in her bowl. “A thousand days at home are pleasant; a single hour away can be hard. Your mother doesn't know what I suffer when I'm away. Now you understand, don't you?” Meiyu nodded. She had felt no hardship herself, but since her father spoke of suffering, she too began vaguely to sense how difficult travelling could be.

“Meiyu!” Hu abruptly came to the point. “You're my eldest daughter, but I treat you as a son. There's someone in Huzhou I want you to meet. If you approve of her, I'll keep her. If you don't, I'll tell her to leave.” For a moment Meiyu did not understand. Then she realized what sort of “someone” he meant. She had heard vague reports that her father had married a woman in Huzhou. When she asked her mother, she had only been scolded and told to mind her own business. Now her father's words seemed almost unbelievable. “Really?” She meant: could this woman's staying or going truly depend on a word from her? Hu understood. “Of course,” he replied solemnly. “I can't make your mother understand. I can only discuss it with you.” “I...” Meiyu did not know what to say. She wanted only to help him, but could find no way to express it. After staring blankly for a while, she asked, “What sort of person is she?” “Her name is Furong.” He then spoke at length of Furong's goodness and the hardships of her life, until Meiyu could think of nothing else.

When he had finished, Hu asked, “Well, Meiyu, what do you think of her?” “This woman... Father, how did you come to know her?” Her father was unwilling to explain all the circumstances. “Someone made the match. They said that whenever I went to Huzhou, I had nowhere to rest and no one to see to my daily needs, so I ought to establish a household there. Men with large businesses all do it. There's nothing unusual about it. I thought she seemed a good person, and what they said was true, so I brought her to live there. But I made one thing clear beforehand: nothing was settled until I'd spoken to my daughter and obtained your agreement.” Hearing this again left Meiyu overwhelmed, both grateful and frightened by the honour. “Why should you need my agreement?” She shook her head. “How would I dare interfere in your affairs?” “You may not dare, but I insist that you do. Why?” Hu sipped his wine and explained point by point. “First, of course, I must tell Grandmother. If she agrees, your mother must agree too. And even after your mother agrees, I still want to ask you. I don't want anyone in our family to be at odds with her. Do you understand?” “I do. If people seem friendly but don't feel it, everyone is unhappy.” “Exactly! And why must you take a hand? Because Grandmother listens to you more than anyone, and your mother can't ignore your opinion either. If you speak for us, things will go smoothly.”

No one had ever attached such importance to Meiyu before. She felt that the responsibility was hers alone and answered at once, “Since you say so, Father, I'll speak to Grandmother as soon as we get home.” “What will you tell her?” She considered. “I'll say she's a good person and very unfortunate.” “Good in what way? What will you say if Grandmother asks whether you've met her? That's why I must take you to see her before we discuss it further.” By now Hu was confident that his daughter was his unwavering ally. Yet affection for her father was one thing; what Meiyu would think of Furong was another. His thoughts therefore left Meiyu for the moment as he considered how to arrange matters so that the two would take to one another at their first meeting.

After another night, they would reach Huzhou before noon. Beforehand, Hu carefully explained his friends and connections there and how Meiyu should address each of them. Once the boat was moored, he took her to Yu Si's house to settle her there for the moment. She greeted Yu as “Fourth Uncle” and A’qi as “Auntie Seven.” A’qi was particularly astute in such matters. Seeing that Hu had brought his daughter to her house, she knew there must be something he wished to avoid, and never mentioned Furong. Instead, she entertained Meiyu most attentively. Warm-hearted and eloquent, and bound to Hu by a deep friendship, she treated his daughter as someone infinitely precious and went out of her way to please her. Meiyu naturally knew whose standing had earned her this welcome, and admired her father all the more. “Stay here a little while. Auntie Seven's house is just like your own. Make yourself at home. I must call at the prefectural yamen first, and then I'll come straight back for you.” But Hu did not go to see Wang Youling. He went directly to Furong's and knocked. She was delighted to see him, though she could not quite conceal the hurt in her expression. Ushering him into the sitting room, she first asked where his luggage was. “On the boat. I'm only staying a day. I've brought someone especially to see you—my eldest daughter.” “Oh!” Furong's eyes shone as she looked at him. “Where is the young lady?” “At the Yus'. I'll bring her presently. She's a child. Coax her a little.” Furong understood: he wanted her to humour the girl and win her over. There was nothing difficult about that. “I can manage,” she said. “That's a small matter.”

What was the great matter, then? Furong felt that Hu's attitude and intentions must first be made clear. The message her Third Uncle had conveyed was vague: he had merely told her not to worry, without saying what grounds she had for reassurance. This was the first thing she asked. It could not be settled in a few words. Hand in hand, they sat beside each other on the edge of the bed, and Hu first asked what had happened when his wife came to the house. “I was making New Year cakes when someone said a Mrs Hu had called,” Furong began in an aggrieved voice. “As soon as we met, she said, ‘My husband's name is Hu Xueyan.’ I panicked. Having no clear standing like this is really unbearable. Ah!” She sighed, and her eyes began to redden. Hu grew anxious at the sight. This was no time for protracted complaints and exchanges of sentiment. Time was short; they needed practical arrangements. Yet he had to comfort her, so he patiently said, “Don't be upset. Bear it all for my sake. Don't worry. I'll certainly arrange everything properly. First tell me what she said.” Furong blinked twice, took out a handkerchief and blew her nose. Controlling her feelings, she described her embarrassment. “Your wife said, ‘Forgive me for coming uninvited, but I truly have no alternative. I know you're a girl from a respectable family and he's deceived you. People who understand each other needn't spell everything out. I only ask you to take pity on me.’ Her words were sharp enough, but her manner was fairly civil, so I said, ‘Mrs Hu, please tell me plainly what you mean.’ She said nothing, but took a red envelope from her handbag and put it before me. ‘These are the little private savings I've put aside over the years. If you'll accept them, I'll be endlessly grateful.’ Of course I refused, but she thrust it into my hand. Then she said, ‘Xueyan won't be coming for some time. Has he left any account books or contracts here? I can take them back with me.’ I said no. She seemed doubtful. After a pause she said, ‘Xueyan and I have shared hardship. There's nothing we don't discuss. To put everything in a few words: we're both women. You must understand my position and take pity on me. You're still young and so good-looking. There's really no need to accept an inferior place.’” Furong paused and turned her face away. “When I think about it, there's truth in what she said.”

Watching her expression and listening to her tone, Hu knew that even if she meant what she said, half of it was resentment. He therefore did not take it too seriously. Turning her by the shoulder, he lightly tapped the tip of her nose and smiled. “You're far too simple and helpless! If you hadn't married a man like me, you'd have plenty of suffering ahead. You say she was right, but answer me this: she said I wouldn't come back, so why am I here? Not only have I come, I've brought my daughter. Doesn't that make her utterly wrong?” “Some of what she said was right,” Furong replied. “It's so difficult for me. You two have shared hardship as husband and wife. What am I?” If they went on like this, nothing would be settled. Hu thought for a moment and decided to cut through the tangle. “Then tell me plainly: what do you want?” “Haven't I told you? It's so difficult for me!” There was no need to ask further. “Since you're in difficulty, let me suggest a solution.” Deliberately, he asked, “Would you like that?” “Tell me.” “My suggestion”—this time he tapped her forehead—“is that you continue to bear my name, Hu.” “Only if I'm allowed to!” “Why shouldn't you? Hu is my surname. I decide who bears it. Who dares say otherwise?” After that, whatever else Furong might have said, she could no longer find a way to say it. Hu had another question in mind: how would she feel if she had to return to Hangzhou and share a home with his principal wife? But as it reached his lips, he saw that it would be unwise. This was no time to introduce another complication. The immediate task was to secure her cooperation and “conquer” Meiyu together. Everything else could wait.

He therefore explained Meiyu's temperament and tastes to Furong. She agreed to everything, with one condition: Meiyu must acknowledge her position in the family, or she would not entertain her. “Don't worry about that. Leave it to me.” With this assurance, he left. Back at Yu Si's, he found A’zhu's mother there too. She had happened to come visiting. Meiyu had met her before and felt no strangeness, so she was talking to her with particular animation. After exchanging greetings with Hu, A’zhu's mother invited father and daughter to stay at the silk firm. He declined. “There's no need for that. But I do have a favour to ask. Make a couple of your special dishes for my daughter.” “Easily done! Whatever the young lady likes, she has only to name it and I'll start at once.” Meiyu was delighted by everyone's attentions, but she was still a girl of fifteen and would not be persuaded to choose. At last her father named two favourites for her. Both were stir-fried dishes and involved little trouble. A’zhu's mother agreed gladly, then asked, “Where will you be eating?” “At Furong's.” “Stir-fried dishes must go straight from the pan to the table. I'll take the ingredients and cook them there.” “Thank you. We'd better be going too.” Hu drew Meiyu aside and asked quietly, “What will you call your auntie when you meet her?”

The question bewildered Meiyu. He had already said “auntie”; what else was she to call her? “What would I call her except Auntie?” she asked. Hu had intended to plant the suggestion. At her answer, he smiled. “I thought perhaps you might refuse to call her that.” “I will!” Meiyu nodded emphatically. “Your auntie has the sweetest temper. In Huzhou, I depend on her to look after me. That means she looks after me in your place, so you ought to thank her when you meet. That's the proper way to behave.” “Yes.” Meiyu thought for a moment, then repeated, “Yes.” Reassured, Hu took his daughter to Furong's without further misgivings. As their two sedan chairs reached the gate, they heard Furong calling, “Bring them inside! Bring them inside!” The chairs were carried through the gate and set down before the hall. Furong went to the second and lifted the curtain herself. Meiyu had already been peeping out. This new auntie was a little dark-skinned, but really quite good-looking. Suddenly she understood: no wonder her father was so unwilling to part with her!

“My dear young lady,” Furong said with a smile, “I never expected you to come.” Meiyu naturally felt shy. She answered with a bashful smile, stepped out of the chair and only then murmured, “Auntie.” At this greeting, Furong was too embarrassed to accept the title without demur. Taking Meiyu's hand, she said, “Come inside and sit down. Are you cold?” She felt the girl's shoulders and upper arms. “You're wearing too little. Let's see whether you can wear my new silk-padded jacket.” “Thank you, Auntie!” Meiyu seized the opportunity to repeat the words her father had taught her. “You've been so kind in looking after things all this time.” She had not expressed herself very clearly, but her meaning could be understood: the person looked after “all this time” must be Hu, not Meiyu on this first visit. Furong naturally felt comforted. Yet she also drew a conclusion about the mother from the daughter: a girl brought up so well suggested that Mrs Hu was an excellent mistress of her household. This made her still less inclined to treat Meiyu as merely a child. She led her into her own bedroom and entertained her most courteously, addressing her as “young lady” at every turn, until even Meiyu found it grating. “Auntie, just call me Meiyu.” Furong was about to demur again when Hu, entering behind them, happened to hear. It was a rare opening, offered by Meiyu herself, and he must not miss it. “Exactly! You're family. All this ‘young lady’ makes you sound like strangers.” Furong took the hint and nodded. “Then I shall. Meiyu, come and try this silk-padded jacket.”

She opened her wardrobe, which held a good many clothes, and took down a new jacket of pale green satin padded with silk floss. Laid over Meiyu's shoulders, it proved rather long, with sleeves too wide to fit her. A grey-squirrel-fur waistcoat faced with rose-purple Ning silk, however, fitted perfectly. Once Meiyu had it on, Furong would not let her take it off. “But it's one of Auntie's good things,” Meiyu said, delighted yet uneasy about accepting it. Looking at her father, she added, “I mustn't keep it.” “It makes no difference,” Hu answered promptly. “Your auntie loves you even more than your mother does.” That single remark bound them closely together. They became inseparable, like two affectionate sisters. Hu felt entirely relieved and decided he need worry no further. His time was as precious as gold, and he would not waste it. He immediately went to the prefectural yamen to see Wang Youling. “How long can you stay?” Wang asked. “I was thinking of leaving tomorrow.” “Why such a hurry? Couldn't you stay a few days longer?” Had he not come, it would not have mattered; now he was here, there was naturally much to discuss, probably more than a single evening would allow. “I'll stay one extra day,” Hu said. Then he explained in detail the purpose of his journey and his family affairs.

“You really are formidable!” Wang said, laughing. “My wife admires yours above all women. Yet in your hands she's like Sun Wukong meeting the Buddha.” “It's too soon to celebrate.” Hu shook his head. “Sun Wukong still has one trick that even the Buddha might find hard to counter.” “What trick?” “She wants to bring Furong home to live with us.” “And what do you think?” “I'd rather leave things as they are.” “See how matters go,” Wang advised. “If there really is no alternative to living together, you'll have to make the best of it.” “It isn't anything else that worries me. Furong is too simple-hearted and no match for my wife. I'm away all year round. I'm afraid she'll suffer.” Wang considered. “If that's your only concern, I have an idea. But we needn't discuss it yet. Tell me, what has been going on between you and the Gongs?”

“Oh, I was just going to tell you. You must have heard something.” “A great deal, though on the whole it's favourable. The Gongs come from my home district, but I don't take their side. To tell the truth, we don't get on particularly well. Father and son can be ruthless, so quite a few people are pleased that you've got the better of them this time.” This made Hu uneasy. His rule was to offend no one and, beyond that, to help anyone he could. Business inevitably involved dealing with officials, especially business on a large scale. The slightest obstruction could affect an entire undertaking, so he was particularly anxious not to antagonize officialdom. He had spent much effort observing and studying these matters quietly and knew how dangerous gossip could be. Two men might have no quarrel at all, but once someone spread stories that the second was hostile to the first, even the first might become confused and begin to think the other had failed him as a friend. This was what people meant by suspicion conjuring ghosts. Hu constantly reminded himself of the danger and would not readily credit malicious talk behind someone's back. But he could not expect everyone else to be equally sensible, and he therefore had to explain. “Then tell me, Mr Xuexuan: do you believe I've caused the Gongs a loss?” “I don't think you're that sort of man.” “You understand me!” Somewhat reassured, Hu continued, “They haven't lost anything through me. On the contrary, I've helped them.” He then told Wang the whole story of the gun purchase.

Wang listened, nodding repeatedly. He felt that Hu had considered every side of the matter and handled it very well. From the guns they turned to Huzhou's militia, and Wang warmly praised Zhao Jingxian's abilities. This subject gave him particular satisfaction. Elsewhere, officials and gentry often disagreed over the organization of militia; Huzhou alone was an exception. Here they worked together without friction, and business could be conducted very pleasantly. “I've thought this over carefully, and there are two reasons. First, Zhao's examination standing is limited. If he were a local gentleman who had once worn a red finial and could not forget the authority he had enjoyed in office, he might expect me to wait upon him and bow to his wishes as though he were still a serving official of the first or second rank. Then we'd never agree. Second, the credit belongs to you. Xueyan, this is no empty praise.” Wang spoke earnestly. “There are capable merchants, but they don't necessarily understand public affairs. You alone understand every difficulty an official faces and everything he thinks. That brings us back to the foreign guns. Zhao admires me for giving attention to them, but he doesn't know that the credit is yours.” “I've neither earned credit nor done any work. With you, Mr Xuexuan, I wouldn't pretend otherwise. All I've done is carry an official letter on a journey I was making anyway. If there's anything you need in Shanghai this time, I can attend to it too.” “That's precisely why I wanted you to stay another couple of days. Huzhou is prosperous, and everyone is eager to help. The militia is well funded. I'd like you to procure a consignment of military equipment. I'll give you the list tomorrow. Please inquire about prices first.” “Easily done. I can attend to it as soon as I reach Shanghai.”

“There's something else, rather more troublesome.” Wang lowered his voice. “There are reports that ‘Jiangxia’ may be transferred. I must think ahead.” “Jiangxia?” Hu did not understand. “Huang of Jiangxia!” Only then did Hu realize that he meant Huang Zonghan. Officials liked to speak in coded references, especially when mentioning great men. They might substitute a place name, an ancestral commandery or the name of an ancient figure. Once explained, these references were nothing remarkable, but without much book learning one really might have no idea whom they meant. This was a weakness of his own. He could hardly start learning again at his age, like a man of eighty taking up the profession of trumpeter and drummer, but he ought at least to consult men such as Ji Heling more often. This was an incidental reflection, and he put it aside. Wang's future was a matter intimately connected with his own interests. He dared not treat it lightly and gave it serious thought.

After a long silence, Wang asked, “What is it? Have you another idea?” “First, may I ask how ‘Jiangxia’ has treated you, Mr Xuexuan?” “Quite well, on the whole.” “Then you'd like him to stay?” “Not necessarily. He's a very difficult man to serve. If someone else could replace him without harming my position, I'd be only too glad to see him leave.” “I understand.” Hu nodded. “The best thing of all would be to have Education Commissioner He transferred to Zhejiang.” He meant He Guiqing. At these words Wang slapped his thigh. “Of course!” he exclaimed. “You've woken the dreamer with a single word! We might ask him.” “Urge him, rather than ask,” Hu replied. “Urge him to start making arrangements as soon as possible. Naturally, that will cost money. We'll find a way to provide it.” Now it was Wang's turn to fall silent in thought. As he considered, he smiled and nodded, plainly delighted by what he saw. Hu felt secretly pleased that he could move a man so deeply.

“That's an excellent idea! I believe He Genyun would gladly agree. First, although an education commissioner holds the second rank, he is still not the equal of a governor in charge of a whole province. Second, Jiangsu is a dangerous province. Zhejiang may be smaller, but war has not yet reached it, and its military arrangements already have a foundation. If he can simply ‘defend the province and keep the people at peace’, his prospects will be splendid. Why shouldn't he do it?” “And then,” Hu said with a smile, exposing the thought behind Wang's words, “it would be easy for you to move beyond the prefectural grade.” “Of course! A transfer to the prefecture containing the provincial capital would also be possible. I ought to go myself, but if I crossed the provincial boundary to promote another man's appointment, it would attract too much attention. ‘Jiangxia’ is narrow-minded and would certainly be displeased. Apart from myself, there is only you. Would you make the journey for me?” Hu hesitated. He truly could not spare the time. Business in Shanghai urgently needed attention, and they did not even know where He Guiqing was. The Jiangsu education commissioner had originally been based at Jiangyin, but since the emergence of the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom he had been unable to remain there and had moved from place to place. With the roads so difficult, finding him might involve a great deal of trouble. Seeing his reluctance, Wang understood and changed his proposal. “Then I'll write a personal letter. Take it to Shanghai and hire a special messenger to deliver it. How would that be?” “Certainly. But where is the commissioner staying at present?” “Presumably Suzhou. Inquire when you reach Shanghai.”

With that settled, they discussed other matters affecting their interests. It was late at night when Hu took his leave. Wang sent him home in his own sedan chair, accompanied by four personal guards carrying lanterns inscribed with his official title. At home, Hu found Furong and Meiyu still awake. “Well,” he asked cheerfully, “what have you two been doing?” “Auntie and I have been tracing patterns for a pair of shoes to give Grandmother.” “Who will make them—you or your auntie?” “I'd like to learn from Auntie, but where would I find the time?” This gave Hu an idea. Finding a chance to draw Furong aside, he urged her to persuade Meiyu to stay. He had thought all along that taking his daughter with him would be an encumbrance. But he had another use for her and had been obliged to invent an entirely respectable reason for bringing her. Now that she and Furong had taken to one another, he was happy to change his plan. “I'm only afraid she won't agree, and I'll be rebuffed for nothing.” “Let her refuse. It isn't such a serious matter. You must look further ahead. If you mean to spend your life in the Hu family, first win Meiyu over. She's the eldest. A word or two from her on your behalf will be very useful in future.”

That was true, Furong reflected. Married daughters and their husbands counted as impartial family arbiters. When property was divided or other family affairs settled, they were invited to attend and uphold fairness. A wife's own relatives were interested parties and could not take such a role. For the sake of the future, it was worth making an effort over Meiyu now. That night, therefore, Hu slept alone. Furong took Meiyu to share her bed and deliberately set out to win her affection. Meiyu was won over: if her father agreed, she would willingly stay there for a while. Though the plan had been his own, Hu affected hesitation when it was put to him. At last he granted permission, promising to stop in Huzhou on his return from Shanghai and take her back to Hangzhou. If he stayed too long in Shanghai and Meiyu grew homesick, Chen Shilong would escort her home. To Hu, Furong's situation was like a game of chess. With Meiyu now placed on the board, victory was assured, and he could leave the matter for the time being. Calculating how much time these domestic affairs had already cost him, he would linger no longer. That day he had a hurried meeting with Yu Si and looked in at the bank. He had no time even to inquire about the silk firm's affairs. Taking Wang's letter and the list of military equipment to purchase, he boarded his boat that same day, ordered extra boatmen hired and travelled through the night, straight for Songjiang.

“You've come at just the right moment!” You Laowu exclaimed when they met. “Silk and tea prices have risen sharply these last few days. It's an excellent opportunity!” “What is it?” Hu asked. “Is there news of a ban on shipments?” “Exactly. Look.” You took a sheet from a drawer. It was a copy of a memorial submitted by Yiliang, Governor-General of Liangjiang. Its substance was that there was evidence of the Small Swords Society's collusion with foreigners. The only course was temporarily to stop shipments of silk and tea from Fujian, Zhejiang and Jiangxi to Shanghai. Deprived of their natural profits, foreign merchants would become eager to see the city recaptured, and only then would they cease supplying the Small Swords Society. “Everyone has been talking about it these last two days. And besides that, harsher measures are coming. The government forces have decided they must recapture Shanghai.” You went on to describe the recent fighting. After Hu left Shanghai, the gentry of Jiangsu had subscribed funds to recruit a thousand Sichuan volunteers. They were commanded by Liu Cunhou, a secretary in the Board of Punishments from Rong County in Sichuan who had been assigned to serve at the Jiangnan Grand Camp, and placed under Ji’erhang’a, Jiangsu's provincial judge. At the same time, two provincial examination graduates, Qian Dingming of Taicang and Wu Lin of Jiading, had organized militia to cooperate with the government counteroffensive. Jiading and Qingpu had therefore been recovered first, followed by Baoshan, Nanhui and Chuansha. Ji’erhang’a and Liu Cunhou were now jointly besieging the walled county town of Shanghai. Whether Liu Lichuan would be defeated at once remained uncertain. Foreign supplies were proving effective: he had food, arms and ammunition, and might well hold out for a year or so.

“We'll have to plan this carefully. Where is Brother Yingchun?” “In Shanghai.” You sighed and seemed about to say more, then stopped. “Brother Five, what's wrong?” “Ah! A family disgrace. Naturally, I needn't hide it from you, but I hardly know where to begin.” You was a man thoroughly at home in the world, brisk and direct in speech. Even when tact was required, he could always explain himself smoothly. He had never been one to hesitate and struggle for words. This occasion alone was different. Hu listened intently, and only after a long time did he understand: the Seventh Lady had run away and was living with Gu Yingchun in Shanghai. The common expression for this was “shacking up”, a profoundly disgraceful thing. In respectable gentry households, even the expression itself was never spoken. No wonder You was so embarrassed to mention it. Hu was astonished, not at the Seventh Lady's boldness, but that Gu should disregard both his obligations to a friend and his own good name. Still more puzzling, Gu had not hinted at it in any of his letters. They were intimate friends; Hu had brought the pair together and was to act as their principal matchmaker. What reason could Gu have for concealing it? Turning this over, he could not help saying, “Old Gu has behaved very badly!”

Now that the matter was out, You found it easier to speak. “We must be fair to people. In truth, Old Gu isn't to blame. It all comes down to our family's misfortune in having a sister like this!” “Oh? Why do you say he isn't to blame?” Once again You faltered, giving an incomplete account of what had happened. Half listening and half guessing, Hu gathered that the Seventh Lady had pursued Gu relentlessly after reaching Shanghai. Then one day she had kept him to drink at her rented lodgings. Whether by design or accident was unclear; at any rate, Gu had become hopelessly drunk, and in his confusion they had become lovers. “Afterwards, Old Gu knelt before me to beg forgiveness. Little Uncle, we must act according to our consciences. Even a sage might have fallen in those circumstances. All I could do was ask what he meant to do. He said he'd go to Hangzhou especially to ask you to arrange the marriage. That would have been a good result reached by a crooked road, and I could have accepted it. Who could have guessed that a Cheng Yaojin would suddenly appear and leave Gu helpless under three blows of his axe?” “How strange!” Hu was full of suspicion. “Does Old Gu already have a wife? Wasn't he honest with me before? If that's so, I must question him properly.” “No, no! It's the head of the Gu clan, a white-bearded man in his seventies who happened to come to Shanghai to visit his grandson. The trouble was that Gu was too punctilious and went to report the matter to him. If only he'd kept quiet! As soon as he spoke, the old man objected fiercely. No woman remarrying had ever entered the Gu family, he said, and he would not allow it. Gu begged repeatedly and sent people to intercede. There was only one answer: he could marry her if he liked, but the old man would open the ancestral hall and expel him from the clan. Now the whole affair is deadlocked.”

“Did Old Gu tell you all this himself?” “Yes. But I've had someone make inquiries, and it's true.” “Then what about Seventh Sister?” You sighed. “A woman's heart turns outwards. She's home fewer than ten days in a month and spends all her time in Shanghai. She and Gu have already set up house together. Yet she herself says he's never stayed at her place. Even as things stand, it's more than enough for me to bear!” “Brother Five,” Hu said soothingly, “who doesn't know Seventh Sister is a woman with a man's courage? You can't judge what she does by the standards applied to ordinary women. I don't believe people will laugh at you. Why keep brooding over it?” “That's true enough, but the matter must be settled somehow.” “Let's discuss it when I get to Shanghai. There must be a way.”
