INSTALLMENT 041
A Swift Rise to Prominence, Volume III — Chapter Twenty-Two, concluding part

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

“Enough, enough!” Hu Xueyan finally said. “Keep this up and someone will lose her temper. I'll take you out for a Western meal, as a farewell dinner for Gu.” Before answering, Gu Yingchun glanced at You. They exchanged smiles and slight nods, apparently understanding each other perfectly, which puzzled Hu. “What are you two plotting?” “Nothing to do with you,” Gu replied. “I'm not leaving today. I'll set off first thing tomorrow.” “What's happened?” Hu persisted. “I still have something to discuss with the foreigners.” Hu was not entirely convinced, but had no reason to disbelieve him. He let the matter drop and repeated his invitation. “What about Sister A’qiao?” Gu asked. “Come with us!” “Thank you!” came a loud reply from inside. Intonation mattered greatly in Suzhou speech, and the sharp, abrupt sound of her voice made the refusal unmistakable. Hu felt a little uneasy, but neither You nor Gu took offence. “Sister A’qiao!” You raised his voice too. “If you won't come, go back for a visit. Second Miss has been missing you.” “Yes, of course!” Her tone softened at once. “I meant to go back anyway.”

As she spoke, A’qiao came out carrying Hu's long gown and riding jacket. He certainly knew how to enjoy being waited on: he merely spread his arms and let her dress him and fasten every button. Then he took out his watch. “Let's go. It's one o'clock.” “Hello!” Sharp-eyed Gu had noticed something. “How did that watch chain come into your hands?” This was what Hu was proudest of. He gave Gu a look promising to tell him later, and at the restaurant he did indeed describe A’qiao's loving thoughtfulness to the two men in delighted detail. “Little Uncle!” You laughed. “Your good fortune is really beginning. Everywhere you go, the women favour you.” Hu's expression grew serious instead. “I don't know what to do myself now. Give me some advice.” You and Gu exchanged another smile. “Give a matter time and it will come right,” Gu replied. “Let's talk when I get back from Suzhou. How about that?” “When will you be back?” “I can't say yet. You have to wait to see those high officials. If I merely leave a letter without meeting anyone, why go myself?” “True enough. Still, I hope you'll return soon,” Hu added quickly. “Not on account of this. I'm afraid that if the foreigners have something to say while you're away, we won't be able to make contact.” “Don't worry. I've entrusted someone with that. Brother Five knows him too. If there's any word from the foreigners, he'll come to find Brother Five. Nothing will be delayed.”

By this time the waiter had brought dessert. When they finished, the bill came to the price of a whole shark's-fin banquet, yet You declared that he had not eaten enough. “There's nothing satisfying about Western food. Expensive and no good!” he said with a laugh. “I'd rather have a plate of pan-fried buns at a street stall. At least they'd sit comfortably in my stomach.” This was only a complaint, of course; there was no question of eating another meal. Outside the restaurant they parted, some to visit friends, others to attend to business, agreeing to meet that evening for a courtesan banquet at Yiqing House.

Hu's thoughts had strayed from work these past two days. Though there was plenty of serious business to attend to, he could not be bothered with it. He went back to the inn, intending to settle his mind and think properly through his public and private affairs. The moment he entered, however, he was astonished to find Chen Shilong waiting. “Why, what brings you here? When did you arrive?” “Not long ago. I went straight from the boat to Yuji Silk Warehouse, and they told me you'd moved here.” “Sit down, sit down. How are things in Huzhou? What have you come to Shanghai for?” “Prefect Wang sent me. I have a letter here.”

Hu opened it and found another appeal for help. Since the tribute grain had been transferred to the sea route, the transport servicemen and sailors formerly employed on the grain boats had lost their rations and were largely without supervision. They frequently gathered to make trouble, and there had even been robberies. Huang Zonghan found the situation a serious headache. During the preparations for sea transport, Wang Youling had proposed making proper arrangements for these men's livelihood, so Huang now made him responsible for finding a way to pacify them. Wang wrote that success would have been relatively easy if funds had been allocated as he originally recommended. Instead, the matter had been allowed to slide. To offer relief now that the situation looked dangerous would seem to be yielding to their threats and pressure, doubling the effort for half the result. It was a most awkward business. Meanwhile, the Huzhou militia was being organised and trained at a furious pace. The defences at Pingwang and Si’an, on the Jiangsu-Zhejiang border, were important too and required frequent inspections. He simply could not attend to this as well. At first he had thought of passing it to Ji Heling; then he realised that Ji would still have to seek Hu's help, so he might as well write directly. He hoped Hu could invite You to accompany him to Zhejiang and use the bonds of their common brotherhood to settle the trouble. “Prefect Wang summoned me and told me himself that he knew how busy you were,” Chen added. “If you really can't get away, I'm to accompany Fifth Master You.” “This could be troublesome. Outsiders don't understand the internal affairs of their brotherhood. We don't even know whether You has any connection with its leaders in Zhejiang. He may refuse to get involved.” Hu added, “What did your Uncle Four say?” “It was Uncle Four's idea to ask Fifth Master You to mediate.” “Oh!” Hu was reassured. “Then that sounds promising. Come along. Let's go to Yiqing House.”

They went together to Second Miss's little house. You had not yet returned, and Hu used the opportunity to question Chen closely about Huzhou. He learned that comparatively few households were raising silkworms that year: the foreign buyers might not enter the market, and the times were unsettled. Chen thought this was only temporary. Anyone with money could make a good business of lending to the silkworm farmers against the following year's new silk. “With money, there are plenty of good businesses to be done. It's too soon to discuss next year,” Hu replied. “When you go back, find out about this year's market first. How many households in Huzhou Prefecture are raising silkworms? Roughly how much silk will they produce? Get reliable figures and write to me at once. This must be done discreetly. Hire people to make the inquiries. Don't try to save a few small coins.”

“Yes.” Chen then mentioned his marriage. His future in-laws had spoken to him about choosing a date around the Dragon Boat Festival. What did Hu think? “Isn't that just when the new silk comes on the market?” “That's what I said. Holding a wedding when business is busiest would be like having both a lantern display and a fair at once. Why crowd everything together? They say that if it isn't around the festival, we'll have to wait until autumn.” “Why postpone it when you could bring it forward?” Hu spoke with the authority of a family elder. “You'd do better to begin married life sooner.” “A’zhu's mother won't do things carelessly. She insists on having the dowry ready first. Unless...” Chen hesitated. “Unless you put in a word.” “That's easy enough. If you'd told me sooner, I'd have spoken sooner. When you go back, tell the old couple this: business matters, but so does a home. Let you have your wedding and set up your household before the new silk reaches the market; then you can concentrate on working hard. Wouldn't that serve both purposes?” Hu went on, “Things will change this autumn. I mean to expand my operations too, and I'll be shorter of people than ever. A wedding then would add to the rush. That calculation doesn't work.”

Chen assented to every point. They had scarcely finished when You and Gu arrived together. After exchanging greetings, they asked what had brought him. Chen could only look towards Hu to explain. “Brother Five, here's trouble for you!” Hu described the Zhejiang brotherhood's refusal to submit to discipline, the gatherings that had turned into disturbances, and Wang's request. “It's a difficult business,” You said, then added immediately, “But I'd go even if I had to climb a mountain of blades.” “Fifth Master You, you're a true friend!” Chen's manner showed unbounded admiration. Hu himself was rather surprised by this ready and generous agreement. But if You went to Hangzhou and Gu to Suzhou, he would be left alone in Shanghai, unable to speak to the foreigners. True, Gu had someone here to help if anything happened, but Hu had never met the man. Besides, there were matters only Gu and You understood. In effect, he would be isolated and helpless. That would never do. He decided at once. “Gu, you must stay here to hold things together. Let's exchange places. I'll go to Suzhou to see Education Commissioner He, and you remain in Shanghai.”

This change overturned the two friends' “secret plot” completely, and was a considerable disappointment. It had been You's suggestion. What Yu Si and the others had done in Huzhou to help Hu marry Furong had been a fine act of friendship; why should they not follow the same example? Gu had deliberately postponed his departure by a day to observe Hu and A’qiao. If the affection was mutual, he intended to take advantage of his journey to Suzhou to visit both her husband's family and her own family at Mudu, and spend freely to bring the couple together. They had just come from Yiqing House, where Second Miss told them that A’qiao had not only agreed but supplied a great deal of information and explained how to proceed. Given enough fire, even a pig's head would cook tender. Three to five hundred taels at the very most would buy her freedom. Now Hu's proposal had spoiled it all.

“What's the matter?” Hu noticed their altered expressions and pressed them. “Gu, is there some difficulty?” “Ah!” Gu sighed with a smile. “The course of a good thing never runs smooth.” “What do you mean?” “Some matters are more urgent than others,” You said quickly to Gu. “Stay and guard the home camp. It won't hurt to make a special trip later.” “I suppose that's what we'll have to do.” “What are you talking about?” Hu was thoroughly puzzled. “Why not tell me so we can discuss it?” Gu shook his head. “If we told you, it would spoil the pleasure.”

You too merely smiled and said nothing. That made it plain enough. Hu still did not know exactly what they were concealing, but it must concern him and A’qiao. Realising this, he reconsidered the amorous good fortune that had fallen into his lap. Certainly, A’qiao had a charm unlike any woman he had known. One could truly say, “Die beneath the peony blossoms, and even as a ghost you remain a lover.” But some people were born fortunate, with wealth and comfort assured and nothing to do. They had abundant leisure to spend on a captivating woman like her. He was different. He had been born for business, and big business at that. He could appreciate her attractions, and could make a woman like her willingly submit to his wishes, but that was not his proper occupation. He must not waste time on her, still less lose his head and forget what he was meant to be doing. At this thought his palms grew damp. He was deeply uneasy. Ever since coming to Shanghai, it seemed, he had been infatuated with A’qiao and had not properly attended to a single serious matter. His smile vanished. “I can guess something of what you're thinking. I'm not speaking against my conscience, but this is only a passing romance. It depends on opportunity; things must take their natural course. We mustn't force them. I acted a little rashly in that Huzhou affair, and there's trouble still. To boast a little, I'm afraid of no trouble at all—but dealing with it takes time, and time is what I lack.” This was a warning to You, and Gu's expression changed too. “We understand,” Gu said. “We'll do as you wish and make no move for the present.”

“That's best. When I feel the time is right, I'll certainly ask you both to take the trouble.” Then something occurred to Hu. “Brother Five, why not take Seventh Sister with you this time and settle that matter we discussed?” He meant asking Wang Youling and the Seventh Lady to become sworn brother and sister. It was a good opportunity, but everything ought to be settled beforehand, and Hu should be present at the ceremony. To bring her along and arrive at Wang's door asking to form the relationship would be too presumptuous. You explained this, Gu agreed, and Hu said no more. It did remind You, however, that he ought to take plenty of gifts to Hangzhou and cultivate the Wang family, preparing the way for the future bond. From this they turned to his journey and decided he would leave the day after next. “What about you, Little Uncle?” “I can leave at any time. If nothing comes up, I'll go tomorrow. The sooner I go, the sooner I'm back.” “No,” You said. “The route isn't very peaceful. I'll have someone make inquiries, hire a boat for your exclusive use, and send men to escort you.” “Don't worry.” Knowing how many small arrangements You already had to make, Hu did not want to trouble him further. “I don't know the route, but Gu does. I'll ask him to help. You needn't bother with it.” “Exactly!” Gu immediately agreed. “Leave it to me. I guarantee everything will be properly arranged.”

With this settled, they parted. Chen was staying at Yuji Silk Warehouse, and Hu took him there first, giving him many instructions, chiefly about You. Wang had invited You as an honoured guest to resolve a difficult dispute, but his status in official society was another matter. Since they also hoped to form a sworn family relationship, neither side must lose face, and You must on no account be slighted. Hu told Chen to explain all this to Wang in person. “The most important point is that Fifth Master You is coming entirely as a personal favour. He is Prefect Wang's guest alone. There's no need for him to form connections with other Zhejiang officials. Make that perfectly clear to the prefect, so Fifth Master You won't be put in an awkward position.” Chen understood and repeatedly agreed. When Hu had finished and was about to leave, he could not resist a question. “Mr Hu, what exactly is the situation with Sister A’qiao?” “A long story. You'll learn in time.” The question reminded Hu of something. “There's no need to talk about it when you get back.” “Of course, of course. I know what should and shouldn't be said.”

Back at Daxing Inn, A’qiao was cleaning bird's nest by lamplight, so absorbed that she did not notice Hu's arrival. She had removed her make-up and undone her hair, gathering its black abundance into a loose, thick braid cast to one side, which exposed a length of snow-white neck. Unable to resist, Hu bent down to breathe its scent. She jumped up in fright, her face turning white. Only when she saw who it was did she let out a long breath, patting her chest and glaring at him. “Surely not such a fright!” He smiled apologetically. “If I'd known you were so timid, I wouldn't have teased you.” “People can frighten each other to death! Feel!” She took his hand and pressed it to the left side of her chest. Her heart was still racing. “What am I to do with someone so timid?” he asked. “I'm going to Suzhou the day after tomorrow for two or three days. I meant to leave you here alone, but perhaps you'd better return to Yiqing House.” Her answer astonished him. “I'm not going back.” Her voice was calm, but every word had hard, unmistakable edges. “Why? What's the reason?” “I've already settled my wages. Once you leave a place like that, you don't go back.” “Good for you!” Hu praised her, but inwardly grew anxious. If she was determined to leave that life, she had committed herself to him. This affair could only move forward; there was no room for him to withdraw. More trouble seemed likely.

“Go to Suzhou,” A’qiao said serenely. “I can stay here alone. It's people pretending to be ghosts that frighten me. Real ghosts don't.” “That's easy to say now. If a ghost really appeared, you'd probably be half dead with fright.” “I don't believe in ghosts. I'll have to see one before I believe.” “Some people certainly have seen them.” Sitting opposite her with his chin in his hands, Hu stared at her. “Let me tell you two ghost stories.” “No, no!” She hurried to her feet. “The way you're staring is frightening enough. Have some bird's-nest congee and go to bed.” On the tea table, a few embers glowed in a small “fifth-watch cock” stove beneath a bowl of bird's nest. She had picked it so thoroughly that not a single feather remained. Whatever its nourishing virtues, the care she had taken alone made it seem infinitely delicious. Once they were in bed, she clasped him tightly. “Now you can tell me your ghost stories.” “How strange!” He laughed. “Why didn't you want them before, but want them now?” “Now? Now I'm not afraid!” She held him still closer. This was a new experience for Hu. His wife remained a model of propriety even in bed; Furong's amorous playfulness went only so far. With A’qiao, every night seemed fresh. He embellished two ghost stories until she burrowed against his chest in alarm. To be afraid yet unable to resist listening was common enough in those who heard ghost stories, though few were as contradictory as she. He knew she was putting it on a little, but her performance did not annoy him.

After a night of tenderness, Hu again slept late. He knew You had many miscellaneous arrangements to make before leaving for Hangzhou, while Gu was busy hiring a boat and finding escorts for him. Neither would visit Daxing Inn. With no urgent business himself, he felt too lazy to go out and preferred to linger beside A’qiao's dressing table, watching the play of her eyes. “Will anyone be coming to eat?” she asked. “We're starting our own cooking today.” “Why take all that trouble? We can order from a restaurant.” “That isn't keeping house.” She picked up a basket. “I'll go to the food market and hire a maid while I'm out.” Hu did not want her to leave, but could find no reason to stop her and agreed reluctantly. Alone at the inn, he felt utterly bored, without the slightest interest in anything. Forcing down his restlessness, he sat quietly and thought. Suddenly he realised that he had never felt like this before. Even in his worst days, when Wang had gone to the capital and he had been dismissed from the bank and sat idle at home, he had never been so dispirited. “What can it mean?” he murmured, secretly alarmed. “How have I suddenly lost all my drive?”

Perhaps he was simply too tired. Years of rushing about and dealing with countless difficulties had overdrawn his reserves of energy; he really needed a good rest. But he could not rest amid all the glitter and bustle of the foreign settlement. Best of all would be to take A’qiao to a borrowed villa by West Lake and live quietly for two months, doing nothing, worrying about nothing. They could row a boat or look at the hills at leisure. In the evening they could get a fresh fish, cook the middle section in vinegar sauce, make soup from the head and tail, warm a jin of Bamboo-Leaf Green wine, and drink together by lamplight. That would be a life fit for immortals. He contemplated it longingly, then suddenly laughed at himself. His business had grown, yet his ambitions had shrunk. In his first flush of success he had thought of buying all the land around his home, building a fine garden, giving daily banquets, keeping his seats filled with guests, and making a splendid display. Now he wanted only to withdraw quietly with A’qiao. Why? Putting his loss of drive and his shrinking ambitions together, he easily saw their common source: both feelings had appeared only in these past few days with A’qiao. People said that a woman's tender embrace was the easiest place for a man's ambition to waste away. There seemed to be something in it. Recognition brought alarm, and alarm gave way to defiance. He decided to put A’qiao out of his mind and think about serious matters. That thought brought him out in a sweat. There was no end of important business, yet somehow he had forgotten it all. To continue like this would be dangerous indeed.

When A’qiao returned, he said, “Just make us something simple. I have to go out after eating.” “Listen to you!” She laughed. “When you're extravagant, every meal must come from a restaurant. When you're stingy, you won't even go out for a bowl of noodles.” This reminded him, and he laughed too. “It's all because you talked about keeping house. I kept thinking I had to eat lunch at home before going out.” He began to walk away. “All right, all right. I'll eat outside.” “Wait!” She caught hold of him and pointed to the basket. “What am I to do with all this food?” “We'll eat it tonight.” This pleased her greatly. “Invite them all,” she said. “There's more than we can finish.” “Good. You might as well make a little more and give Fifth Master You a farewell dinner.”

Once outside, however, Hu felt at a loss. His real purpose was to struggle against himself, refusing to sink into the comforts of love. There was much to be done, but some matters were not yet ripe, and others depended on news from other people before he could decide his next move. At this moment he could do nothing. Where should he go? After some hesitation, he decided to try Yuji Silk Warehouse and find Chen first. Unfortunately, Chen had just gone out. The clerk was most courteous and insisted that Hu stay, pressing tea and tobacco on him with every attention. Hu could hardly refuse, but felt no inclination to make conversation. “Please don't trouble yourselves over me. I'll have some tea and wait. Go about your business, or I shan't dare impose on you.” The clerk understood that he did not want to sit among miscellaneous visitors and led him into a small room where You also held private conversations. The furnishings were nothing special, but there was a finely made, very comfortable reclining wicker chair, ideal for lying back and thinking. “This is excellent,” Hu said happily. “I can have a nap here.” That made his wish not to be disturbed still clearer. The clerk agreed at once, had a young apprentice bring in a freshly brewed covered bowl of tea, four dishes of fruit, and a water pipe, and arranged them on a square stool serving as a tea table. Everything ready, he withdrew and closed the door.

Hu lay back in considerable comfort. As his mind grew quiet, he became aware of voices in the next room, every word distinct. Listening closely, he found they were discussing the land business. He too had considered entering it, and now let nothing escape him. The two men knew a good deal about the foreign settlement and the foreigners' intentions. They said that foreigners and Chinese did things differently. In China, roads emerged from use: people gathered, a market formed, and naturally a track was worn. By the time anyone planned a proper road, shops and stalls hemmed it in on both sides, leaving no room for widening. The foreigners built their roads first. Roads brought people, people brought houses, and trade naturally flourished. That was why Chinese commercial districts could not grow on the same scale. One had to admire the foreigners' breadth of vision and boldness. Hu had never heard anything like this. The more he considered it, the more sense it made. Wang had told him about the capital, and now he understood why its commercial streets were different. Its great population under the emperor's very feet explained only half of it. The other half lay in its planning, which resembled the foreigners' method: broad roads laid out first, land divided into districts, separate places assigned to government offices, homes, and shops. Commercial areas were further distinguished: theatres and teahouses could open here, cattle, sheep, donkeys, and horses could be sold there. Such planning naturally produced an impressive result. “Look at Shanghai's layout,” one of the voices continued. “With First Road, Second Road, and more laid out on that pattern, you can see how the northern and southern districts will prosper. But there's even greater promise to the west. A man with foresight should buy all he can now, whether reed marsh or paddy field. Once the foreigners' roads reach it—good heavens!—he can sit at home and grow rich.”

How could Hu lie there quietly after hearing that? Yet he had said he wanted a nap, so abruptly taking his leave would seem odd. He could only restrain himself and wait, though this kind of business talk was impossible for him to put out of his mind. He did not know Shanghai's geography well enough to make plans. What he longed to do was find Gu, climb into his Hengsimei carriage, and drive west as far as the area around Jing’an Temple. Only an inspection on the spot would satisfy him. Fortunately, Chen soon returned. Hu thanked the clerk warmly and left. The first thing he said in the street was, “Shilong, do you know the area to the west?” “If you don't know it, Mr Hu, how could I?” “Never mind. Let's get a carriage and go for a drive.” They hired a clean carriage and set off west from the mud city wall, taking whatever roads offered themselves. After turn upon turn among paddy fields and stretches of water, Hu could scarcely tell which direction they were facing.

As they drove without a fixed destination, Hu told Chen what he had overheard. Now Chen understood. “Mr Hu, there are two ways to approach this. I'm afraid the first isn't practicable.” “Never mind that. Tell me.” “The first requires money to spare. Land is cheap, so even if it earns nothing, you buy it and leave it there until the price rises, then decide what to do. It would make sense if you were providing for future generations. But your money has to keep working, Mr Hu, which is why I say it isn't practicable.” He paused. “The second would depend on Mr Gu. Find out which road the foreigners intend to build, get ahead of them, and buy the land nearby. Then you could make a fortune in no time.” “Yes! That's right!” Hu considered his suggestion, then suddenly saw something further. “Though not entirely right. The cleverest course is to get the foreigners to build the road you want.” “But...” Chen understood, yet thought it impossible. “Why would the foreigners let someone else direct them? Would they build whichever road you told them to?” “It depends on what men do. There must be some way. Fortunately, there's no hurry over this. We can think it through.” That was Hu's way. While he lacked information, his urgency to obtain it was like a house on fire. Once he understood the facts and had a direction, he grew calm. Chen knew that his talk of taking time meant neither delay nor abandonment. Anyone working for him had to understand this and quietly make preparations. One day Hu might finish his thinking and produce a plan worked out in every detail, ready to put into effect at once. If Chen had made no preparations, he would be unable to match Hu's pace or meet his requirements.

“I must find some more men,” Hu said on the journey back. “Keep an eye out for me when you return.” “Yes.” After a moment's thought, Chen asked, “What work do you intend to give me eventually? I don't want to stay shut up in Huzhou.” “I know. You like travelling about. Don't complain when the time comes.” “Why?” Hu considered without replying. After a long silence, he asked, “Do you think the Shanxi remittance houses can be beaten?” “You can't bring them down. They've built up credit over many years. But if our banks were more flexible, instead of clinging to old rules as the remittance houses do, we ought to be able to win the market in the five southern provinces.” Hu liked this answer. Chen might look rather glib and superficial, but his words were sound. He therefore told him what was in his mind. “I've thought things over properly today. Banking is still my foundation. But I must change how I work. People speak of power and profit together: the two can't be separated. With power comes profit. So for now, I needn't pursue profit. I must gain power.” “Power?” Chen thought carefully. “Do you mean influence, Mr Hu?” “Exactly. Influence in business and influence in official circles. I want both. Even both won't be enough.” “And influence among the foreigners!” Chen completed the thought. “Good!” Hu raised his thumb excitedly in sincere approval. “If you can see into my mind like that, you're getting there.” Chen too was delighted, though he answered with restraint. “How could I compare with you? I'm not a tenth as good. But I do understand something of how you work.”

“As long as you understand!” Hu replied. “Times are changing. You're a few years younger, after all; you can't compare things as I can. Officials used to despise merchants—truly despise them. Even the great salt merchants of Yangzhou, who bought official rank as high as circuit intendant, would submit meekly to every order from a man who had risen through the examinations. Only after the five ports opened to trade, when people saw that foreign merchants and officials were treated much the same, did their ideas begin to change. In this past year or so, I'd say the position has shifted further: officials have to depend on merchants. Why do I want influence among the foreigners? Because official power doesn't reach into their sphere. The authorities need men like me to make the connections. Once I have influence in official circles and among the foreigners, extending my influence in business will naturally be easier.” Chen listened, nodding and considering each point. Hu's words gave him much to understand.

As they talked, they found themselves back among crowded streets without noticing the time. Only then did Hu remember A’qiao's request to invite You and Gu home for dinner. It was already late, and he feared they might have other engagements. He hurried to Yiqing House, but nobody was there, not even Second Miss. There was, however, a message. The male attendant told him that You had said, “Ask Mr Hu to wait. I'll be back at six sharp.” And what would happen when they met at six? Suppose You had another engagement, or was entertaining guests at Yiqing House himself? How could Hu explain that to A’qiao? The thought made him visibly restless. Chen had seldom seen him in such a state and was surprised, though still more concerned. When he asked and learned the reason, he was inwardly amused. A proverb came to mind: “Even a hero finds a beauty hard to resist.” Men of the highest ability often became helpless and utterly at a loss in such matters. Here another saying applied: “The onlooker sees clearly.” “There's no difficulty. Either I can go and let her know, or I can stay here to wait.” “Yes, yes!” Hu interrupted before he had finished. “Go and tell her I'm waiting here for them and will return as soon as they arrive. If I can't bring the guests, I'll certainly come home for dinner myself.”

Chen went on this errand and found A’qiao sitting quite at ease. She greeted him politely, heard his message, and replied without concern, “That's all right. There aren't many dishes. The steamed ones can go on steaming; I'll wait until everyone arrives before putting the others in the wok.” Her calm self-possession had a different charm from Furong's gentleness. Mr Hu was certainly fortunate in love, Chen thought. Another difference was A’qiao's remarkable fluency. Chen considered himself a good talker, but beside her he felt at a disadvantage. As the conversation continued, he suddenly realised that he might have said too much. Through indirect questions she had drawn a great deal from him about Hu's family. Fortunately, he had not mentioned that Mrs Hu was both formidable and highly capable. Now on his guard, and feeling that he should not stay too long, he rose to leave. A’qiao knew that he was Hu's trusted man and naturally wished to win him over. She repeatedly urged him to stay, finally saying, “I only dispense with ceremony because you're one of Mr Hu's own people. Otherwise I wouldn't insist. Unless, of course, you don't regard me as one of his own.” She was plainly speaking as Hu's woman established in a separate household. Chen could see how deeply Hu liked her, but the eventual outcome was still entirely uncertain. If he insisted on leaving, it might indeed suggest that he refused to accept her as belonging to Hu, and arouse her suspicions. That would be unwise. He changed his answer. “I'd better go back and give Mr Hu your message first. Then we'll come together.” “Well, you must be sure to come back!” “Certainly, certainly.”

Chen left Daxing Inn and walked at an easy pace. He had gone only a few steps when a woman called, “Shilong!” Looking up, he saw the Seventh Lady. Gu was driving the carriage himself and had also noticed him. He stopped. “Where are you going?” “Back to Yiqing House.” “No need. We've come specially to fetch Sister A’qiao. We're all gathering at our place tonight. You must come too.” Chen therefore turned back, and the three went into Daxing Inn together. The Seventh Lady and A’qiao had never met. One was punctiliously polite, the other warmly affectionate; both were talkative. Holding hands, they ranged far and wide over all they had heard of one another. The Seventh Lady said that Gu had told her how capable and pretty A’qiao was. A’qiao replied that Second Miss had described this frank, lively lady who dared to visit a place like Yiqing House. They took to one another at once and seemed to regret not having met sooner. Gu, however, grew impatient. “My dear lady, you've talked for ages. Say something about why we're here!” They had come specially to invite her. As Hu and You were about to leave for Suzhou and Hangzhou, the Seventh Lady had ordered a farewell banquet from an Anhui restaurant. Hu, meanwhile, wanted everyone at Daxing Inn to try A’qiao's cooking. The result was a deadlock. “It makes no difference whether we eat at your place or mine,” the Seventh Lady said. “But first, I can't cancel the banquet, and it would be a pity to waste several taels. Second, my place is more convenient. The weather isn't hot yet. Even if your dishes are already cooked, they'll keep overnight. I'll come and eat them tomorrow!”

A’qiao naturally agreed without hesitation. Saying that she must change, she asked them to sit in the outer room, then quietly drew Chen aside. Taking out four foreign silver dollars, she said, “Young Master Chen, may I ask a favour? It's my first visit to the Seventh Lady. I can't arrive empty-handed. Please buy four kinds of food for me and bring them to her house.” “I don't know where she lives.” Then Chen had an idea. “But never mind. Leave it to me.” Once she had changed, all four rode together to the Seventh Lady's door. Now that Chen knew the address, he said, “I'll be back in a moment,” and turned away. At the mouth of the lane were a confectioner's shop and a fruit stall. He bought a basket of American oranges, a basket of Tianjin duck pears, and a large box of pine-nut candy. Two dollars remained. He asked the shopkeeper to take down an aged ham, and when the total came to forty cents more than he had, he advanced the difference himself. “These are Sister A’qiao's gifts to the Seventh Lady,” he said with a smile. “I'm the junior here, so today I shall eat for nothing.”

“Why stand on ceremony?” the Seventh Lady said. “Sister A’qiao, we're like family. I'll open the skylight and speak plainly: I don't care for all this, and I'm no good at it myself.” Gu could not help reproaching her. “Listen to yourself. She means well, and that's all she gets from you. Sister A’qiao knows your nature, but wouldn't anyone who didn't think you unfeeling?” “Not at all!” A’qiao put in quickly. “I know the Seventh Lady dislikes empty formalities. But we're so used to them that we can't change. Fortunately, Young Master Chen chose well. These are all useful things. Even if I hadn't brought them, she'd have to spend money buying them.” “That's true enough,” the Seventh Lady said, smiling. Then she added apologetically, “I always say whatever comes into my head. Don't take offence if I say something wrong.” Nobody else found these two sentences remarkable, but Chen was astonished. She had never spoken like that before. It seemed that Gu's quiet influence was having its effect.

Chen was about to make a teasing remark when he heard footsteps on the stairs. You and Hu appeared together, still talking as they walked. They offered brief greetings and continued their conversation. Chen gathered that they had been visiting someone who could be very useful in settling the trouble within the Zhejiang brotherhood. They were now discussing whether to invite him to Hangzhou as well. “Can't your conversation ever end? Talk later. Dinner's ready.” Then the Seventh Lady had another thought. “There aren't enough of us to make it lively. Why not invite Second Miss?” “No need, no need!” You waved his hands. “She's gone out on a banquet engagement. She'll come later.” Dinner was served in the main room. Six people sat at a round table that seemed rather too large. Unable to resist the Seventh Lady's insistence, A’qiao shared the place of honour with Hu. Men and women sitting interspersed was a complete departure from custom, and A’qiao had never occupied the seat of honour before. She was therefore rather constrained and kept well away from Hu. After the first round of wine, he asked her, “What have you and the Seventh Lady been talking about?” “All sorts of things. She's so frank. It was delightful.” “Why don't you come and keep her company more often?” You coughed. Only then did Hu remember that You was urging his sister to return to her family home. Wouldn't frequent visits from A’qiao give her an excuse to stay in Shanghai?

The Seventh Lady paid no attention to these considerations. “Little Uncle is right. Why don't you come to Songjiang with me for a few days?” “Excellent!” You was slightly surprised and hastened to encourage the idea. “Sister A’qiao, do go and stay there. It's easy to travel back and forth. You can return to Shanghai whenever you please.” “I should feel awkward imposing on your household.” Though she said this, her bright eyes kept turning towards Hu. His attitude would determine what she did. He naturally expressed approval, but Gu had a different opinion. “I don't think she need go to Songjiang or stay in Shanghai. Why not simply accompany Little Uncle on a visit to Suzhou?” “That's another possibility.” You looked at them both. “What do you think?” Hu genuinely found it hard to decide. On one hand, A’qiao's company would make the journey warm and delightful. On the other, after sharing a bed and living together, travelling together as well would hammer in the nail and bend its point: the case would become unassailable. He would be able only to go forward, never retreat. It therefore depended on what A’qiao herself wanted. She had come from misunderstanding Hu to understanding him, and from understanding him to accepting Second Miss's advice. Her mind was already made up. But she had known many people and understood the world thoroughly. She would never force his hand at every turn and make him wary of her, keeping a respectful distance. For the moment, she said nothing.

“Well?” the Seventh Lady pressed. “Songjiang or Suzhou?” Put like that, A’qiao could naturally only answer Songjiang. Gu was more perceptive in such matters than the Seventh Lady and spoke before she could reply. “Suzhou, of course.” “Suzhou it is,” Hu decided, though he still had to ask the woman concerned. “Will you come?” Here A’qiao showed her skill. She said neither yes nor no, but merely, “The Seventh Lady has been so kind...” She meant to accept, while still acknowledging the Seventh Lady. Though only a verbal courtesy, it made a favourable impression. “Never mind, never mind!” the Seventh Lady replied. “Once you're back from Suzhou, I'll come and take you to Songjiang.” That settled the matter. Everyone had packing to do, so they broke up early, agreeing to lunch the following day in Second Miss's little house and then board their separate boats.
