“This is a most presumptuous visit,” Hu Xueyan said with a deep bow. “May I have the honour of your name?” “My courtesy name is Shuya,” Pan replied. “I already know why you have come. I'll ask her to join you so that you can speak face to face.” “Thank you, thank you. I'm most grateful, and very sorry to disturb you so late.” “Excuse me for a moment.” Pan Shuya turned and went inside. Hu took the opportunity to examine the reception room and was astonished by the difference between the house's exterior and interior. The narrow, worn entrance suggested a family in decline, yet the furnishings were extraordinarily valuable. The pictures and calligraphy alone dazzled him. A set of four landscape scrolls on one wall represented the “Four Wangs,” one apiece. On another wall hung four calligraphic scrolls by Wen Zhengming, covering all four scripts: regular, cursive, clerical, and seal. There was also an enormous central scroll stretching from ceiling to floor, covered in wild cursive characters as big as bowls. Apart from the character for “one,” Hu could not read a thing. But the sheer size of the sheet of xuan paper, over two zhang long and seven or eight chi wide, was enough to leave him staring.

“Mr Hu, please have some refreshments.” A servant in an exceptionally neat blue cotton gown brought a silver box wrought in the shape of a plum blossom, its stalk forming the handle. Beneath the lid were five fine porcelain dishes, each with a different delicacy. Red, green, yellow, black, and white were all represented in brilliant colour. Hu recognised only the red one, which he took for rose-flavoured New Year cake. Using ivory chopsticks mounted in silver, he lifted a piece to his mouth. Smooth, glutinous, fragrant, and sweet, it grew more delicious as he tasted it—but the flavour was not rose. “What is this made with?” “Peach juice mixed into the flour and steamed.” Hu had never heard of such a thing. “Your household certainly knows how to do things finely!” The servant smiled with restraint, stepped back two paces, and stood silently by. As Hu ate, he thought that when he made a great fortune, he must live even more exquisitely than the Pans. That would make life worth living.

In the midst of his admiration and wandering thoughts, he heard voices behind the screen. Looking up, he saw A’qiao approaching with a maid. “I've been waiting so long for you,” she said at once. “Please sit over here.” The tactful servant led them to a couch farther inside, brought covered bowls of tea, and gave the maid a glance. Both withdrew to the veranda. “What's happened?” Hu asked. “One visit to your family, and all this trouble! If I'd known, I'd have sent Zhou with you.” “That wouldn't have helped. It's very strange...” The strange thing was that on the day before A’qiao returned, someone had called at her husband's home and announced openly that she had sent him to discuss ending their marriage, which now existed only in name. If her husband was willing to divorce her and marry again, he could receive a substantial payment. Her husband was an honest, simple man and did not know what to say. But an elder cousin on his father's side, called Xiaogouzi, was a scoundrel who enjoyed stirring up trouble. Seeing a valuable opportunity, he immediately said that everything could be discussed, but A’qiao must come and settle it herself. The visitor repeatedly sounded him out, and Xiaogouzi said that he wanted only two or three hundred taels. “A lie! He meant to lure me home and use me to extort money from someone. As luck would have it, I came back the very next day. Fortunately, word reached me in time. When he came to fetch me, I refused to go. My mother told me to leave quickly too. Who would have thought he'd get a boat and follow me down with two local ruffians? When I saw that, I daren't return to the inn. I warned the boatman not to reveal that Jinchang Inn had hired his boat for me. I went ashore, hired a little sedan chair, and came straight here. I still don't know whether Xiaogouzi knows I'm here.”

Hu nodded gravely as he listened. By the time she finished, his mind was made up. “You did the right thing. Don't worry. I'll handle it.” “How?” “Are you very close to the concubine in this house?” “We've been friends since we were children. I wouldn't have come to her if we weren't close.” “Good!” Hu was relieved. “Then stay here for the present. Stay a few more days.” A’qiao was surprised. “A few more days?” She frowned. “Until when?” Hu would have preferred her to remain until He Guiqing left for the north, but this was neither the moment to say so nor something he could easily explain. Then he considered that, close though the women were, her hostess was not the household's principal mistress. A’qiao's position as a guest was awkward too. Both sides had their difficulties. A brief visit was harmless; a long stay might invite gossip. “Let's proceed as circumstances suggest,” he said. “If you're imposing on them here, naturally I must show my appreciation. I'll send a gift tomorrow. If the master of the house kindly invites you to remain, stay on. Otherwise we'll find another way.”

“There's no difficulty about staying. I only want to know until what day. You've so much to do in Shanghai that you'll have to go back in two or three days at most. Surely you don't mean to leave me here alone?” “Of course not. I have other arrangements...” “What arrangements?” she interrupted, intensely serious. Another man might have felt unable to withstand her persistent questioning, but not Hu. “Don't be anxious. Naturally, the arrangements will be perfectly satisfactory.” He continued, “To put it briefly, I'm keeping you here rather than bringing you back to the inn because I don't want to give Xiaogouzi anything to use against us. Next I'll find out who visited your husband's family.” “Yes, you must find out. All the way on the boat I kept wondering why they had used my name and said I'd asked them to negotiate. Could it have been someone I know?” Her words struck Hu like a flash of lightning. Almost certainly this was You and Gu's doing. He had told them to wait for his instructions and keep out of the matter for the present. They must have disregarded him, and their cleverness had backfired, producing unexpected trouble. But he knew A’qiao was unsettled and ought not to be told too much just then. “Don't go guessing. Leave everything to me. If this goes smoothly, it may be settled very quickly. Perhaps everything will be clear by tomorrow or the day after. Stay here a few days with an easy mind. I'll send your clothes trunk over.”

“There's no need. My friend and I are much the same size, and she has more clothes than she can wear. But...” A’qiao returned to her point. “You must give me a day. How long exactly am I to stay? Then I can be at ease and have an answer if they ask.” “Well...” Hu saw that keeping her here until He Guiqing left, around the Dragon Boat Festival, would be impossible. In that case, he should settle the matter sooner. After considering briefly, he answered decisively, “Three days. On the fourth day I'll certainly come to fetch you.” She was satisfied, but added, “Do remember!” “I won't forget.” He took out a thick sheaf of banknotes and selected several of smaller denominations. “Here are two hundred taels for your expenses. While you're a guest here, don't stint over small sums. Give the servants whatever gratuities are appropriate, and don't be mean about it. Understand?” Of course she understood. She nodded. “Don't worry. I won't let you lose face.”

Hu then asked to see the host, thanked him, and took his leave. Back at Jinchang Inn, he kept Zhou Yiming with him and explained the situation in detail. The story was complicated, with many twists of cause and consequence. Even though Hu omitted much that did not need telling, Zhou listened with relish and rubbed his hands, eager to take part. “Country ruffians can't manage much. Let me send them packing.” “We don't even know where they are. Don't rush,” Hu said. “Let's agree on a plan before acting. Getting rid of those two fellows would be easy enough. It's Xiaogouzi I want to deal with.” “Oh!” Zhou settled down to listen. Apparently, there was more to come. “I want to teach Xiaogouzi a lesson and make him grateful to me as well. See if you can think of a way.” This was a difficult problem, and Hu had partly intended it as a test. After careful thought, Zhou proposed a plan. Hu considered it workable, and they began preparations that day.

The first step was to locate the two men. Country people were country people, with a rustic air they could not shed, and early the next morning Zhou found them near the Pan house without much difficulty. At the mouth of the Pans' lane stood a little tea shop of the kind popularly called a “tiger stove.” Its customers were mostly ordinary people from the neighbourhood. On arriving, they took a wooden basin and scooped up water to wash their faces and rinse their mouths, greeted one another, then drank tea, ate snacks, and talked loudly. Only two furtive, rustic-looking customers, sitting at the table behind the flatbread stall by the entrance, greeted no one. Their eyes followed every passer-by, paying particular attention to women. The signs were plain enough. “Xiaogouzi!” Zhou called out on a guess. Xiaogouzi had no idea that, while the mantis stalked the cicada, an oriole waited behind it. He turned and found Zhou smiling at him. “Were you calling me?” “Yes. When did you come into town?” “Yester... yesterday.” Xiaogouzi faltered. “I don't know you.” “How can you not know me?” Zhou looked puzzled too. “Let me ask: don't you live in Mudu?” “Yes.” “Then that's right.” Zhou sounded utterly certain. “An important man forgets easily. You may not recognise me, but I can't be mistaken. We once attended a dispute-settling tea meeting together. You were a great help to my friend.” This was another fabrication. Zhou reckoned that a man like Xiaogouzi was bound to have taken part in such meetings and settlements, so he lied boldly. Suspecting no trick, Xiaogouzi considered. “Which friend?” “His surname is Wang.” “Oh! You must be Fat Wang's friend. Yes, he got slapped for making advances to Widow Liu, and I intervened for him. How is he now?” “Quite well, quite well,” Zhou answered readily. “He often mentions you and says what a loyal friend you are. Come, let me treat you to a little breakfast.” He called to the stall, “Bring some crab-shell pastries and oil buns!”

“You shouldn't trouble yourself,” Xiaogouzi said, but his eyes kept turning outside. A woman was passing whose graceful figure from behind looked very much like A’qiao's. It was as good as writing his own confession. Zhou laughed inwardly and ceased to regard him as any sort of opponent. When the figure disappeared, Xiaogouzi reluctantly looked back, his face showing both annoyance and doubt. Zhou went straight to the point. “Are you waiting for someone?” “No, no!” “That woman...” Zhou pointed into the distance. “Her back looks so familiar. Where have I seen her?” Xiaogouzi could not imagine that he was being deliberately baited. Surprised and delighted, he began, “You... Ah, how foolish of me! We've been talking all this time and I haven't asked your surname.” Holding him in such contempt, Zhou could not even be bothered to invent one. “Zhou.” “Brother Zhou, you too thought you recognised that woman who just passed?” “Yes. I seem to have seen her in Mudu, and perhaps in Shanghai too.” He shook his head. “I can't remember.” This performance deceived Xiaogouzi completely. Rather than reply, he called to his companion, “Old Wu!” and jerked his chin towards the street. Old Wu dashed off, leaving Zhou still more amused. Lowering his voice, Zhou asked, “Are you following someone?” With an air of concern, he warned him, “Suzhou isn't the countryside, especially this past year or so. The governor-general, governor, military commander—there are any number of great officials with red hat buttons here. You'd better be careful.” “Well...” Xiaogouzi faltered. “It's nothing. Someone we know.” “Who? Do you mean that woman?” “Yes.” Thinking Zhou worldly and experienced, and remembering his claim to know her too, Xiaogouzi stopped concealing the truth. “You're right about both Mudu and Shanghai. Perhaps you'll know her name. She's called A’qiao.”

Zhou put on another performance. Straightening his back, drawing his face a little away, and lowering his eyes, he paused for some time. “So that's who it is! A’qiao from the high-class courtesan house. No wonder her back looked familiar.” “Yes, yes! Brother Zhou, you know she's at one of those houses.” Feeling a further explanation necessary, Xiaogouzi hurried on. “It's all her own family's fault. She ran away secretly from her husband's home. Carrying on like that has disgraced his family. What rotten luck!” “Why are you following her now? To catch her and take her back?” “Not catch her. She's neglected her duties as a wife. We want to persuade her to return.” “Xiaogouzi, if I may say so, this is just like your Suzhou saying: ‘Hang a salted fish under your nose—smell it, but forget your hopes.’ How can you expect to talk sense into someone like that?” Xiaogouzi nodded, opened his mouth, then swallowed his words. Zhou knew they had reached the critical point. His original plan had been to find a young runner from the detention quarters and have him recruit a colleague. With a chain and a pair of handcuffs, they would wait until Zhou discovered Xiaogouzi's lodging, then pin some arbitrary charge on him and haul him into custody. Hu would then use the visiting card He Guiqing had given him for the magistrate of Changzhou County to secure Xiaogouzi's release. That was how they meant to frighten him and make him grateful as well. Now it seemed there was no need for such elaborate measures: direct negotiation would suffice. Zhou had no doubt he could mould Xiaogouzi like dough, round or long, into whatever shape he wanted. But he had to consider Hu. Neither his standing nor the closeness of their acquaintance entitled him to act independently. He was merely running errands: Hu gave instructions, and he followed them. Carrying them out exactly was the best possible result. If he disobeyed, even an outcome better than expected could make Hu think him unreliable, because a man who would not obey was a man difficult to control.

For this reason, Zhou changed course. “Everyone has his own affairs, Xiaogouzi. I won't ask any more.” He paused. “It's a coincidence, but I have a chance to make a little money today. I can't manage it alone. Passing by and seeing you, I thought I'd ask you to help. Have you any time?” The proposal came so abruptly that Xiaogouzi did not know how to answer. Of course he welcomed any chance to make money. But first he must decide whether Zhou could be trusted, and second whether he could do the job. He needed an explanation before making up his mind. “If you've chosen me, Brother Zhou, naturally I'm willing. But what's involved? Could you tell me first?” “It's a long story. You're distracted at the moment, and I have something else to do. Let's see...” Zhou pretended to consider, then asked urgently, “Where are you staying? I'll call at noon.” “With a friend outside Chang Gate. But I may not go back at noon.” “Then let's arrange somewhere to meet. I'll invite you for a drink. Bring your friend Old Wu.” “Good.” Xiaogouzi agreed without hesitation. “You choose the place. We're brothers; it makes no difference who treats whom.” “Exactly. Yuandachang on Guanqian Street, at noon sharp. Whoever arrives first waits. We must meet.” With this settled, Zhou left first. He was careful enough not to forget to pay the stall for their breakfast.

He hurried to Wuyuan Tea House, where Hu was waiting by arrangement from the previous night. “That Xiaogouzi is completely in the dark. He knows nothing! To think he'd try making money this way—he has no idea of his own limits.” Zhou proudly described how he had found him, then added, “Why use an ox-cleaver to kill a chicken? A fellow like that isn't worth your trouble. We need only tell him straight. I'd meant to speak plainly on the spot, but since it was your business, I didn't dare take the decision myself.” “You do me too much honour.” Well pleased, Hu responded with equal courtesy, clasping his hands. “You've done me a great favour.” “Not at all. No need for formalities, Mr Hu,” Zhou answered earnestly. “Just tell me what should be done, and I'll do the legwork.” “Your method is excellent already. To settle it within a day or two would be easier than I could have expected, to tell the truth. Keep your noon appointment, then bring him to my inn so we can talk together. It would be best to leave the man named Wu out of it.” “Easy enough. I'll find a way.” “There's one more thing, and it's important.” Hu thought briefly. “Never mind. I'll do it myself. Just bring Xiaogouzi. Once you've got him there, leave the rest to me.” Those words reminded Zhou that Xiaogouzi might grow suspicious and deliberately fail to appear. Then he would have no satisfactory account to give Hu.

Without further talk, Zhou hurried out of Jinchang Inn. To save time, he hired one of the horses used for excursions into the hills, set his foot in the stirrup, swung into the saddle, and turned it towards the city. “Hey, sir! Where are you going?” The groom hurried to catch the bridle and looked up at him. These horses normally went out with a groom leading the way, and knew the route thoroughly. They advanced one slow step at a time on the outward journey, but moved very differently when home and their feed troughs were in sight. All were old, so none could go particularly fast. They were used only for hill excursions and never entered the city. Hence the groom's astonishment. Zhou knew the rules. Seeing that no exception would be made, he said curtly, “I'm going into town. Will you hire it to me or not? If not, I'll get down.” “I'm in business. Why wouldn't I? Only...” With no time for discussion, Zhou jumped down, tossed away the reins, and turned to go. His manner was poor, and the groom had a temper too. Spitting on the ground, he muttered, “Rotten luck! I've met a ‘master’ today.” In this use of Suzhou speech, “master” meant a ghost or devil. Zhou was already furious at failing to hire the horse. Now he could contain himself no longer. Turning, he strode back two paces and pointed. “Who are you cursing?” Alarmed by this menacing approach, the groom was afraid. Yet the elaborate preparations of Suzhou men for a fight were famous. In a dialect that would never sound fierce, he repeatedly demanded, “What d'you want? What d'you want?” while backing away, baring his fists, rolling his sleeves, gathering his skirts, and winding up his queue as though preparing for a fight to the death. Suzhou people also loved a spectacle, and a ring of onlookers soon formed. Encouraged by the support of his fellow townsmen, the groom raised his voice and accused Zhou of behaving unreasonably in rapid Suzhou speech. Even in a quarrel he remembered to add a few witticisms, which drew laughter from the crowd. Zhou was now in an awkward position. He was not afraid, but feared becoming so entangled that he would miss Xiaogouzi and spoil Hu's important business. He regretted his conduct but could find no way to retreat with dignity.

Fortunately, help arrived in the shape of Hu. Pushing through the spectators, he asked, “Zhou, why are you quarrelling with him?” “I wanted a horse to get into town quickly. His horses are particular about where they'll go. Very well! Even if a bargain falls through, civility remains. There's no need to curse me.” “Who cursed you?” The groom hurried forward to argue, but Hu stopped him. “No one speaks pleasantly in a quarrel. There's no need to debate who's right. I ask only this: has he cost you any business?” “Even if he has, I'll have to put it down to bad luck.” “Then there's nothing more to say. Go and attend to your customers.” Taking Zhou by the arm, Hu walked away. There was no need for further soothing or questions. They hired two sedan chairs into the city, got out at Guanqian, fixed their meeting at Jinchang Inn for noon precisely, and separated to attend to their respective tasks.

Hu had intended to find a silver foundry, but inquiries showed it was too far away. He would have to use a bank instead. Entering Yongxingsheng, whose premises looked suitably imposing, he asked at once, “Have you any official ingots fresh from the mould?” These were large ingots of fifty taels each, cast under the supervision of the provincial treasury for remittances to the capital and other official purposes, hence their name. A bank might not have newly cast ones on hand, but could exchange for them at a foundry or even have them made. Anything was possible if the customer would pay the premium. A clerk at Yongxingsheng, full of his own importance, braced his hands wide apart on the counter, tilted his head, and asked, “How many?” “Twenty.” Twenty meant a thousand taels. The clerk pulled over an abacus, rattled through the calculation of the premium, and said, “Not until this afternoon.” “I need them urgently. I'll pay extra for transport. Please arrange it.” They agreed on that charge too, and Hu handed over two Fukang notes payable on demand, one large and one small. The clerk gave them a sidelong look. “We don't accept those notes.” “Why?” “Their credit is unreliable.” Had he said they had no dealings with Fukang and could not accept its notes because they knew nothing of its position, Hu would have had no objection. But to call its credit unreliable was close to slander. Anger rose in him. He reached inside his gown for his thick sheaf of notes, which included those of the capital's celebrated “Four Hengs” and Rishengchang, with its head office in Hankou and more than twenty branches. He meant to put them on the counter and let the man choose. His hand had already touched them when he reconsidered. There was no need. Suppressing his anger, he asked, “Does your bank issue notes?” “Of course.” “Good. Its own notes must be perfectly reliable, then?” “Need you ask? Bring as many as you like. We'll redeem them all.” “I have none. Since you won't accept Fukang's, I must go elsewhere.” Hu clasped his hands. “My apologies.”

Outside Yongxingsheng, Hu found the insult hard to swallow. He would have liked to retaliate immediately, but that could not be arranged in a moment. Putting it aside, he found another bank, obtained twenty official ingots, and had one of its outside clerks deliver them to Jinchang Inn. The silver had scarcely been stacked when Zhou arrived with Xiaogouzi. Once they were introduced, Hu came straight to the point. “I'm a patron of Sister A’qiao's. She has asked me to speak for her. If her husband's family will release her, she's willing to pay a thousand taels so that her husband can marry again and buy a few mu of land. He could live comfortably enough. Zhou tells me you're involved in the matter. An emperor doesn't send hungry soldiers on a mission. On Sister A’qiao's behalf, I'll give you a hundred taels. What do you say?” The proposal was plain, yet Xiaogouzi found it abrupt. What puzzled him most was how this “Brother Zhou,” who claimed to be Fat Wang's friend and to have attended a tea settlement with him, could know so much about his affairs. Looking first at Zhou and then at Hu, he rolled his triangular eyes, which showed more white than black, and could find no answer. As he hesitated, a flash of brightness struck him. Hu had pulled away a sheet, revealing twenty large ingots beneath, all brand-new and glittering with silver light: a truly lovely sight. Beside them lay another pile, several medium-sized ingots and some pieces of refined silver, perhaps a hundred taels in all. Plainly, that was the reward intended for him. “Money moves the heart,” as the saying went. Hu, who had turned countless somersaults through the eye of a coin, understood this better than anyone. That was why he had gone out of his way to obtain official ingots. Knowledge of the world, insight into human nature, and command over other men had given him this unfailing prescription. Xiaogouzi knew, too, that A’qiao had become a valuable commodity. Storytellers always said a beauty was beyond price. If he held firm, eighteen thousand taels would be easy enough to get. What was a mere thousand? Such were his thoughts, yet his eyes refused to obey. They would not leave the gleaming pile of ingots. Naturally, he said nothing. Zhou moved his lips to press him, but Hu stopped him with a gesture.

Hu was patient and let Xiaogouzi think. Silver was like beauty: “Let a man see nothing to desire, and his mind remains untroubled.” Even if he caught a glimpse and was abruptly cut off from it, that could be endured. But when it remained visible and just beyond his reach, each look intensified his longing. Xiaogouzi was gradually reaching precisely that state. “Nothing need be forced,” Hu said at last. If he did not speak, Xiaogouzi could not, and they would be deadlocked. “If there's a difficulty on your side, say so.” “A difficulty?” Xiaogouzi asked blankly. Seeing how the money had bewitched him, Hu lifted a corner of the sheet and flicked it upwards like a conjuror. It spread through the air and settled exactly over the ingots. Only then did Xiaogouzi recover his senses. “I know you're a man of the world who understands how things are done,” Hu continued. “That's why I've spoken plainly. I'm acting for someone else. Whether it succeeds or fails makes no difference to me. I only need an answer to take back.” The money had effectively been put away. One word from him, it seemed, and the whole matter would be dropped. Such an opportunity was too painful to surrender lightly. “I'll find a way,” he answered involuntarily. “That's the spirit. We're both intermediaries. We can speak frankly and discuss any difficulties together. What way do you have in mind?” “I can't decide it myself. I can only do my best to persuade them. I can't guarantee success.” Xiaogouzi added, “And if there's some difference over the amount, what should I say? Please give me something to go on, Mr Hu.”

They had reached the bargaining stage, which meant the main issue was effectively settled. Hu considered briefly. “I'm very busy in Suzhou and have no spare time for haggling. Make things easy for others, and they're easier for you too. If you don't waste my time, I'll pay for promptness. If the documents can be signed first thing tomorrow, I'll add four large ingots myself.” “Tomorrow morning may be too soon.” “Noon tomorrow at the latest. If it can't be done by noon, there's nothing more to discuss. Someone else has a use for a thousand taels.” As Xiaogouzi wondered what this meant, Zhou supplied the perfectly timed response: “I've invited Clerk Zhang of the criminal office to drink with me tomorrow at noon.” Together, the two remarks implied that the thousand taels might go to Zhang. Money for a clerk in the criminal office naturally meant a lawsuit. Xiaogouzi grew still more alert. “Yes, yes,” he said, nodding repeatedly. “I'll certainly bring the husband here by noon tomorrow. I'll act as intermediary for the documents.” “Zhou will act for our side.” Hu took the hundred taels and put them before Xiaogouzi. “Take this now.” Xiaogouzi was delighted beyond expectation, though he offered the required polite objection. “That isn't the custom.” “People make customs. This has always been mine. Take your hundred taels first. It'll give you more energy for running about.” Hu added a brand-new wrapping cloth, silk outside and cotton within, bundled up the silver himself, and handed it over. Xiaogouzi calculated that if the affair succeeded, the twelve hundred taels would yield him another three hundred through the open intermediary's fee and the concealed twenty-per-cent kickback. Such a windfall had dropped out of nowhere, and he owed it to Zhou. After thanking Hu, he beckoned. “Brother Zhou, please come outside with me.”

Zhou accompanied him out. When he returned, he carried two medium-sized ingots and was smiling broadly. “The fellow has a conscience after all. He said one should remember the source when drinking water, and since the opportunity had come through me, he wanted to give me twenty taels. I was happy to accept, so I could return them to their rightful owner.” He set the ingots before Hu. “Nonsense. He gave them to you. What have they to do with me? Keep them. Tomorrow, when the papers are signed, we'll follow the custom for property transactions: ‘three from the buyer, two from the seller.’ The intermediary's commission is five li, divided between the two sides. You'll have another thirty taels. That's your rightful benefit.” Zhou had thus gained fifty taels out of nowhere. Overjoyed, he revered Hu as though he were a god and volunteered to fetch A’qiao. “No hurry. Let her stay at the Pans' a couple of days,” Hu replied. “There are two other matters I'd like to discuss.” The first was the insult he had suffered at Yongxingsheng that morning. He wanted redress. Did Zhou have a clever suggestion? “No mind is better than yours, Mr Hu. You think of the plan, and I'll do the legwork.” “I do have a plan, but I'm afraid it may be too harsh. Let me tell you a story about a remittance house first.”

The four largest remittance houses in the capital all had the character Heng in their names and were commonly called the “Four Hengs.” Great businesses could bully their customers, and their competitors too. A rising house called Yiyuan specialised in copper-cash notes. Courteous and accommodating in its business, and sound in credit, it prospered rapidly. Its notes circulated chiefly among ordinary townspeople. Once its good name was established, word spread from one to ten and ten to a hundred, until even official circles knew its reputation. Jealous of a competitor, the Four Hengs set out to damage it. They secretly collected its notes and presented them for redemption while spreading rumours that it was about to fail. A run followed. Yet for three days Yiyuan redeemed every note on sight, without even making people wait. On the fourth day calm returned, and its reputation was stronger than before. The Four Hengs naturally investigated its resources, only to discover an unshakable opponent. Yiyuan had four million in cash reserves. Whenever it issued a note, it set aside the full corresponding amount in a separate reserve. It never issued an unbacked note, so it could not be embarrassed. Zhou understood the point. “Mr Hu, you mean to collect Yongxingsheng's notes and give it a lesson?” “Exactly. But I'm afraid of repeating what happened between the Four Hengs and Yiyuan. One man makes a move today, and the other answers tomorrow. If I fail to bring Yongxingsheng down and it turns on Fukang, wouldn't I be asking for trouble?” “Yes, we must guard against that. Let me find out how strong it is. If its resources are thin, we can put it in check. Otherwise we'll need another method.” “Just what I mean. Make inquiries first. Fortunately, there's no hurry. Now for the other matter.”

The other matter was a gift for Pan Shuya. Hu wanted both to thank him for lodging A’qiao and to cultivate his friendship, for a man like that was well worth knowing. If he merely wished to discharge a debt of courtesy, the gift would be easy: spend a few dozen taels on four kinds of presents, and the obligation would be met. Friendship required something Pan would value, even something that would touch him. The family was rich; no costly object was likely to impress it. Local products from Hangzhou might have left an impression through their rarity, but Hu was in Suzhou and could not obtain them. Explaining all this posed another difficult problem for Zhou. “A gift should be something the recipient wants but can't get,” Zhou said. “The Pans have money. What they lack is prestige. Could you give them that?” “Beautifully put!” Hu clapped in approval. “Let's think of some distinction we can give him.” Encouraged, Zhou concentrated, blinking as he worked his mind, and came up with an idea. “See what you think of this. Your friendship with Education Commissioner He is close enough. Ask him to honour the Pan household.” Hu nodded gravely. “The principle is excellent. But how exactly?” “You could write to him. Whether to explain the circumstances fully is for you to decide. If you'd rather not, you can be vague. Just say that Mr Pan has been a great help on this visit, and ask the commissioner to call and thank him on your behalf. A second-rank official paying a visit with his full ceremonial escort—wouldn't that confer considerable prestige?” “Excellent! A brilliant idea. So brilliant, Zhou, that you don't know yourself where its brilliance lies.” This was a strange remark. Zhou was thoroughly puzzled and could only stare. Hu offered no explanation; the time had not come to reveal his intentions. He had decided to follow a common practice in official circles: purchase a concubine as a gift, arranging for A’qiao to become He Guiqing's secondary wife. Since she was staying with the Pans, He too would owe them gratitude. Thanking them for Hu would really be thanking them for himself. Zhou's suggestion contained this further significance, which made it exceptionally clever, though he himself did not know it.

“That's certainly what we'll do.” Hu was pleased, yet regretful too. “Zhou, you're very capable. It's a pity you can't come and work for me.” Zhou was stirred. He too felt that working for Hu was brisk and satisfying: every move seemed to give them the upper hand. Yet the lijin post in Yangzhou was an official appointment, however modest, with a good income. It would be a pity to abandon it. He could only express regret. “Yes. If the opportunity arose, I'd very much like to serve you.” “We can discuss that later.” Hu rose cheerfully. “I've nothing more today. I'll go and look round the city. Find out how matters really stand at Yongxingsheng. We'll meet at Yuandachang again tonight.” Hu visited Xuanmiao Temple, drank tea, and listened to a long storytelling performance. When the session ended, he strolled to Yuandachang, chose a good seat, and began drinking alone while he waited. By the time he had finished a jin of Huadiao wine, Zhou arrived, wearing a secretive smile. Hu smiled back. “It looks as though Yongxingsheng may be in for a headache.” “An extraordinary coincidence!” Zhou said eagerly. “An acquaintance of mine works there as an outside clerk. I invited him for tea, and now I have the whole ‘Romance of the Western Chamber’ in my head.” “Excellent! Have some wine first.” Hu poured him a bowl of warm wine himself. “We can drink and talk.”

“Yongxingsheng deserves a lesson. Its very origins are crooked...” Zhou began with the bank's history. The original owner, surnamed Chen, had built his business through thrift, enduring hardship for half his life. Yet he had scarcely been a proprietor for a year when a fever carried him off. He was barely into his forties and left a wife and a son. A widow and orphan were easily preyed upon. One of the clerks, also surnamed Chen, was deeply calculating. He lavished concern on the widow and attended to her every need, eventually gaining both the woman and her money. Nominally Yongxingsheng's manager, he was in fact its owner. “One turn deserves another!” Zhou took a long drink. “This present Boss Chen has a daughter. A clerk in the bank became involved with her. By the time the raw rice was cooked, there was nothing to do but take him into the household as a son-in-law. His nickname is ‘Skyrocket’—the fellow who offended you.” “No wonder he's so full of himself. He has the standing of a man awarded an imperial yellow jacket.” Hu asked, “This Boss Chen schemed against a widow and orphan, and his son-in-law behaves like that. The other men in the bank must resent them. How can it prosper?” “Exactly!” Zhou put down his cup and struck the table. “No weakness escapes you. If that weren't the case, why would my friend act like Zhang Song presenting a map and reveal their secrets?” According to Zhou's information, Yongxingsheng was already in a dangerous position. Chen had been too greedy, lending several sums at high interest that he could not recover. Cash flow was therefore strained. Its capital had only ever amounted to a hundred thousand taels, yet it had issued notes for more than two hundred thousand. Resenting the wrong done to the dead Boss Chen, the clerks all longed to see the living one collapse. Hu knew the business. A brief calculation showed him that bringing Yongxingsheng down would not be difficult. Present fifty thousand taels in notes, and it would suffer; present a hundred thousand, and it would have to close. A man of Chen's character could expect little support from other banks. Even where fellow bankers felt a duty to help, it was easy to relieve a temporary emergency and hard to cure insolvency. Yongxingsheng's difficulties were more than a passing shortage. Any money advanced would be lost. Nobody would willingly be so foolish. But then Hu reconsidered. What benefit would he gain by destroying it? None—only harm. Word would spread that Hu Xueyan of Hangzhou's Fukang Bank was ruthless. The Suzhou bankers might be outraged and unite against him, shutting Fukang out of the city for good.

“Let it go,” Hu said with a smile. “I don't like beating a drowning dog. We'll spare him.” Zhou, however, was dissatisfied. “Mr Hu, he ought to be taught a lesson. Besides, Fukang could make a name for itself.” Hu considered. “That's possible. Let me think it through.” They dropped the subject. Hu drank with an easy mind; such moments of relaxation were rare. Without noticing, he drank too much and became so thoroughly drunk that he could not remember how he got back to Jinchang Inn. When he woke the following day, his whole body felt weak. Too languid to go out, he sat resting at the inn, drinking strong tea alone and reviewing the previous day's events. One remark of Zhou's had considerable point. A quarrel with Yongxingsheng was not worth the trouble, but making Fukang's name in Suzhou was an excellent idea. Suzhou had become the most important centre in Liangjiang, handling immense flows of military funds and assistance remitted by other provinces. If Fukang was to spread branches everywhere and operate on a grand scale like Rishengchang of Hankou, Suzhou was a market it must enter. There were only two ways to establish oneself. One was literally to fight one's way in, compelling submission by force—the method of a street ruffian making his name. Hu could do that too: crush Yongxingsheng and let everyone see his power. But he would not. His unchanging principle was the Hangzhou saying, “People lift one another as they carry a flowered bridal chair.” That principle had brought him to his present position, and he would continue to follow it. The other course was to win acceptance through goodwill. Remembering Skyrocket's face still filled him with disgust, but business must never become a contest of temper. Considering matters calmly, he saw that Yongxingsheng might serve as a stepping-stone into Suzhou. The question was how to place it.

Boss Chen did not seem an easy man to deal with. Hu had seen many of his kind: half-wits, as men of the world called them, or “candles,” as the people of Shanghai said—no light until someone set a flame to them. To bring such a man under control, one had to begin harshly and turn kind: let him taste hardship before sweetness, and he would become perfectly obedient. With this in mind, Hu quickly formed a plan. He could influence two streams of official payments between Zhejiang and Jiangsu: the shared expenses of the Sea Transport Bureau, and the military funds for Huzhou's joint defences, together with assistance remitted directly from Huzhou to Jiangsu. For both streams, Yongxingsheng notes could be collected and sent straight to the Jiangsu provincial treasury and military finance office. Public funds would naturally be redeemed in cash. This was borrowing another man's knife, and it would immediately put Yongxingsheng in difficulties. Once it could no longer meet its payments, Fukang would step forward to support it. Then Yongxingsheng would be meat on the chopping board, to be cut as he pleased: he could either keep it going or take it over. There were risks, of course. The bank was a mess. Supporting it might be like jumping down a well to save a drowning man; taking it over might bring endless entanglements. This was the point on which the whole plan would succeed or fail. Hu thought hard. The safest course seemed to be to judge the situation as it developed. If it could be managed, he would intervene. If not, the Jiangsu authorities would be the ones demanding payment, and his own side would have nothing to do with it. Yet safe though that might be, it could leave him with nothing for his effort, or damage others without benefiting himself, merely destroying Yongxingsheng. At this thought he realised that it was too early to decide. Before doing anything, he must investigate further: what were its real profits and losses, what other property did Chen own, and if the bank failed, what proportion of its debts could be recovered in a settlement? Only with that information could he reach a decision.

When Zhou arrived, Hu asked, “How much does your friend at Yongxingsheng actually know about its affairs?” “I can't say. But inquiries would be easy. Most of the clerks are at odds with Boss Chen and Skyrocket. If they know the details, they'll certainly tell.” “Good. Ask your friend to investigate. It must be done secretly.” “I understand. But it will take more than two or three days. I'm afraid you may not be able to wait.” “There's no hurry. Write when you've found out.” “Yes.” Zhou paused, then added, “Once I've finished your business, I'll go to Yangzhou and see how things are. If there's nothing worthwhile there, I'll come and join you in Shanghai.” “I hope you will. If Yangzhou really doesn't suit you, you're welcome. But there's no need to force it.” Hu returned to Yongxingsheng. “What's your friend's name?” “Zheng Pinsan.” “What sort of man is he?” “Very honest, and quite capable too.” “That's rare. Honest men are often useless, and most capable ones are slippery.” An idea occurred to Hu. “If he's that sort of man, could you bring him to see me?” “Of course! He knows of you too.” “How?” “I've told him about you. But he'd also heard that the owner of Fukang in Hangzhou was a Mr Hu. When would be convenient? I'll bring him over.” “You're leaving tomorrow. Bring him tonight.”
