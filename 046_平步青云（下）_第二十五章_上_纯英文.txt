CHAPTER TWENTY-FIVE

Once the men had gone, A’qiao could come out of hiding. Hu Xueyan felt that the time had come to explain everything. After a moment's concentrated thought, he asked, “A’qiao, how would you like me to find you a husband?” He had deliberately chosen this abrupt opening to turn her thoughts in a new direction at once. That could not be achieved so easily. Her lively eyes blinked as she tried to understand his meaning before deciding how to answer. “What do you mean?” She shook her head, setting her jade earrings swinging. “I really don't understand.” “Do you think I'm joking?” “I don't know. I've had some experience of you now. You have so many tricks that even Zhuge Liang couldn't see through them.” Hu laughed. “Is that praise or abuse?” “Neither. It's the truth.” “I'm telling you the truth too.” His smile vanished and he spoke with complete seriousness. “I guarantee you'll be happy with the match I arrange. In future, remember that I've done you some good, and speak for me when you have the chance.” This was frank enough, and clear enough. A’qiao understood quickly. The promise that she would be happy struck a particularly pleasing chord. But who was the man? Hu answered before she could ask. “You've met him. Education Commissioner He.” Her face grew hot and her heart beat rapidly. She tried to conceal it and appear unmoved, but her eyes could not deceive Hu's penetrating gaze.

“Well?” he asked deliberately. “Commissioner He is a true fair-faced scholar, famous in official circles for his good looks. He'll soon go to the capital and then receive a provincial appointment. I hear his principal wife is unwell. If anything should happen to her, he might even make you his lawful wife. Then wouldn't you be a great official's lady, riding in a sedan chair with eight bearers?” What a delightful prospect! A’qiao's heart blossomed with joy, and a smile appeared at the corners of her mouth. It vanished almost at once. Suddenly on her guard, she thought the proposal too unexpected. Most likely Hu was testing her. If she believed him and the trick was exposed, she would have given him endless grounds for ridicule. Other matters could be joked about; this was no joke. Being too innocent and trusting could leave her in an impossible position. Her expression darkened. With a cold little laugh, she withdrew to the narrow space behind the bed curtains to change. Hu was naturally surprised by the sudden alteration, but soon understood. He could hardly blame her. “You don't believe me, do you?” he asked calmly. “Tell me what would convince you.” That was precisely what A’qiao was asking herself. She could think of no way to establish whether it was true. For now, her safest position was to show complete indifference. Deliberately sounding impatient, she replied, “I don't know. Stop bothering me.” Her defence admitted not even a drop of water. Hu paced slowly, considering how to reach her. Later, something he said could convince her that he was serious. Truth was truth and falsehood falsehood; the facts would emerge in time. He might as well say what needed saying now. She would remember, and once she believed him, his words would take effect.

He therefore went on “talking to himself,” describing He Guiqing's circumstances and the steps he intended to take. Inevitably, he ended by asking, “Now it depends on what you think.” A’qiao had long since finished changing, but deliberately remained seated behind the bed. His orderly, vivid account began to stir her hopes again. Still, she would not disclose her attitude. Whatever course she took, the essential thing was to keep up appearances. “What do I think?” She emerged gracefully and began folding her clothes. “I don't know.” Hu knew that further pressure would achieve nothing. Reversing his approach might be more effective. He assumed a disappointed expression. “If you're unwilling, nothing can be done. Other things may be forced, but this requires consent on both sides. Fortunately, I haven't spoken plainly over there yet. Otherwise I'd have offended everyone.” A’qiao feared that the cooked duck might fly away—that her cleverness would cost her the opportunity. Yet honestly admitting that she was willing would undo all her elaborate pretence. Caught between the two, she desperately found another tactic. She searched her memory for the sufferings that had begun when she became a child bride at seven: her mother-in-law's cruelty, washing coarse cotton clothes in icy water despite winter chilblains, and summer nights in the woodshed when mosquitoes kept her awake until dawn. Gradually her heart ached and her eyes grew hot. She began to sob.

A pretty woman's tears possessed immense power. Hu had a way of dealing with almost anything, but he feared tears like these. Alarmed, he asked, “Why, what's happened? Tell me if you've been wronged. What are you crying for?” “Where can I speak of my wrongs?” A’qiao seized the opening, filling her voice with resentment. “People like us are only playthings for rich men, like a cat or a caged bird. When they fancy us, they spend money to buy us; when they're tired of us, they give us away. Told to go east, we daren't go west. What is there to say?” “That's an ungrateful thing to say!” Hu was exasperated. “I'm doing this for your good.” “Who knows whether it's good or bad? Think about yourself. You decide everything without consulting anyone, and then say it's for my good!” She meant something specific: the business Zhou had gone to settle. Hu felt himself somewhat in the wrong and could only remain silent. Silence brought calm, and calm made it possible to understand. Thinking over her words, he perceived compliance beneath apparent opposition, pleasure beneath apparent resentment. Inwardly, she was perfectly willing; she merely had to retreat before advancing. That was human nature, and could even be taken as proof that she still had a conscience. To force her to say outright that she wanted to become He's concubine was impossible—and even if it were possible, what would be the point? Once he understood this, her tears no longer seemed formidable. What he must do now was win her affection and leave her feeling grateful, so that in future she would always consider his interests with He Guiqing. He remembered Ji Heling telling him the story of the First Emperor's parentage. He himself was rather like Lü Buwei. The thought brought an involuntary smile. “I cry, and you laugh!” A’qiao continued her performance, rolling her eyes and pouting. “Men have no conscience. One can see right through them.” “Quite right,” Hu answered in the same spirit. “I admit it. But men are clever too. They seldom drive things to the point of leaving no way out. When conscience is called for, they still have one.”

A’qiao did not answer. Wiping her tears, she poured herself a cup of hot tea. Just as she raised it to her lips, it struck her that this was not proper. She placed it before Hu and poured another for herself. Drinking at his ease, he asked, “A’qiao, who exactly is there in your family?” “Haven't I told you? An old mother and a younger brother.” “How old is he, and what does he do?” “Eighteen. He's an apprentice in a cloth shop.” “Is he married?” “He hasn't even finished his apprenticeship. How could he think of that? Besides, marrying isn't easy.” “Not so difficult, either. A’qiao, I'll give you another thousand taels. Deposit it in a sound bank, use the interest to help with household expenses, and leave the principal untouched. When your brother finishes his apprenticeship, it can pay for a wife or a little cloth shop of his own.” She looked at him, blinking without a word. Thinking she doubted him, Hu generously took out a thousand-tael note and pressed it into her hand.

“Do you really want to help me?” “How could there be any doubt?” Hu laughed. “Do you truly think I have no conscience?” “I was only saying that. Everyone has a human heart. If you're good to me, surely I know it.” She added, “But if you really want to help, don't help in this way.” “How, then?” “My brother is clever, and good-looking too. He's famous in our town as a handsome young fellow...” “You needn't tell me.” Hu smiled. “One look at the elder sister tells me the younger brother must have fine features.” “Not in an effeminate way. He's tall and well built, and cuts quite a figure. But never mind that. I was thinking that if you'd look after him, I could bring him out to work for you. Wouldn't that be better than learning business in our little place?” She returned the note. “So that's it! Certainly. I'll help your brother advance, provided he wants to get on. Keep the money too. Consider it my gift towards your mother's ‘coffin fund.’” Though she knew she need not stand on ceremony with Hu, A’qiao still felt uncomfortable accepting it. “I'll kowtow in thanks on my mother's behalf. I'll leave the money with you for the present.” “No. Keep it yourself.” She would not take it, and he would not yield. Taking the note, he slipped it into the inner pocket of her lined satin jacket. The soft warmth he felt as his hand reached inside stirred his desire. Regret returned. He was reluctant indeed to send such a woman far away.

For a while Hu said nothing, his mind in confused agitation. A’qiao could not guess what he was thinking and returned to her brother. “His childhood name is A’shun. When should I bring him out?” Collecting himself, Hu said, “An apprenticeship is governed by a written contract. He can't simply leave whenever he likes. As far as I'm concerned, any time will do.” A’qiao knew that leaving before the term was complete meant repaying the cost of board. But with a thousand-tael note on her, she felt secure. “That doesn't matter. I'll arrange it properly.” “Good. Write and tell him to come.” Besides this, she had much to discuss with her family. Better to go home again herself. “When are you leaving?” she asked. “I've already lost time. Once Zhou gets back, if your business is settled, I'll leave first thing tomorrow.” “Then it's too late,” she said disappointedly. “For what?” “If you were staying another day or two, I'd have gone home once more. Now there's no point discussing it.”

By this time Hu's wandering desires had settled. He had given his word and could not go back on it. That being so, he might alter his original plan for A’qiao to remain with the Pans. “This is what I think. In conducting affairs, one must give people no grounds for criticism. From this moment, you belong to Commissioner He's household. We ought not to stay together any longer; it wouldn't look right. I originally meant you to stay at the Pans', but now you can decide. You may go to your mother's instead.” This surprised her. After considering it, she felt that Hu really did act unlike other men: his thinking was careful and his methods elegant. Since he put it that way, she would have greater standing before He Guiqing later. There was no need to say more. Then it occurred to her that accepting without comment would suggest she felt no attachment, as though she truly had no conscience. She must say something, but what? She could hardly argue that they might continue living together since she had not yet entered He's household. Wouldn't that be the behaviour of a woman in a courtesan house? Even there, once a girl agreed to marry a patron, her nameplate came down immediately. She could not claim that she might still receive customers until the day she left. Yet what else could A’qiao say? At last she found something. “If one's conscience is clear and one's conduct upright, what is there to fear? We know the truth ourselves.” “That isn't the point. Any appearance of impropriety must be avoided,” Hu replied. “I'll ask Zhou to take you home tomorrow. If you grow bored after a couple of days in the country, you can return to the Pans', or even come to Shanghai for a visit. As far as I'm concerned, from this moment I regard you as a concubine of the He household.” His words built a fence between them. Both suddenly felt obliged to observe propriety; their speech and manner grew polite, and distant.

Such restraint made their company uncomfortable. “Go back to the Pans',” Hu said. “I'll take you.” “And what will you do?” “I...” At a loss, he answered vaguely, “I'll look round the city.” She wanted very much to offer to walk with him, but her new position as “a concubine of the He household” made the words difficult to utter. Unable to express her concern, she hesitated. “Well? Let's go before it gets late.” “No hurry. I'll sit a little longer.” Sitting silently was dull, so she found something to talk about: Suzhou's gentry families. From the wealthy Pans they passed to the distinguished Pans, and from them to first-place examination graduates and chief ministers. Suzhou produced great officials in abundance; the subject could go on forever. As dusk approached, Hu felt that calling on the Pans after nightfall would be inappropriate and urged her to enter the city. He escorted her there, arranged to meet the following day, and took his leave without disturbing the host.

Having dismissed his chair, Hu wandered on foot. He reached Guanqian and passed an inn just as a sedan chair stopped before it. A man got out whose back looked familiar. Hu stared for a moment, then called in delight, “Elder Brother! Elder Brother!” The man stopped and turned. Now Hu could see clearly: it was indeed Ji Heling. “What a surprise!” Ji was equally pleased. “To meet here! What brings you to Suzhou?” “I was going to ask you the same thing. Why are you here?” “Making arrangements for this year's sea transport. I've been here several days.” “Then haven't you heard about the disturbances in the Hangzhou brotherhood?” “I have. It isn't my responsibility, but it concerns sea transport all the same, and I've been very anxious. My official business isn't finished, so I can't get away.” Ji asked, “How did you hear of it?” “This is no place to talk. Where are your rooms?” “Oh, this way.” Ji led him to his quarters, which also had a small courtyard. Someone came out to open the door, and Hu was taken aback to find a young woman. “This is Mr Hu, my sworn brother.” “Mr Hu!” Smiling, she welcomed him. “Please come inside.” Unsure how to address her, he merely nodded vaguely, though his eyes remained on her. She seemed not yet twenty, wearing a pale moon-white satin lined jacket beneath a long black satin waistcoat, with loose trousers and unbound feet. A long, glossy black braid hung down her back. Her appearance suggested a maid in plain dress, but her speech and conduct were dignified and composed, quite unlike a servant's. He could not make her out.

Ji ought properly to have introduced her, but said nothing. Hu grew more puzzled. Unable to ask in her presence, he could only speak of the brotherhood's disturbances and Wang Youling's appeal for help. “Good! With You to mediate, everything should be all right.” Ji was deeply relieved. “Now I can stop worrying. But what brought you to Suzhou?” “A long story.” Hu rose. “Come, Elder Brother. Let's eat out and talk over dinner.” Ji readily agreed. “But first, there's one arrangement to make. Why don't you move here and stay with me?” “I'll stay here tonight, but there's no need to move my luggage. I meant to leave tomorrow. Since you're here, I'll stay another day. The day after that I'll board outside Chang Gate, so it's better to leave things where they are.” “Very well. I'll have someone find you a room.” Ji summoned his newly engaged attendant, Changqing, and told him to ask at the inn’s desk for a clean, superior room. Afraid Zhou might return and be unable to find him, Hu also asked Changqing to make a special trip to Jinchang Inn and explain his whereabouts. These arrangements made, they went out together. Yuandachang was only a few steps away. Choosing a secluded corner, they sat over their wine while Hu related the full story of his visit. “You're just like the Old Man under the Moon whom people worship by your West Lake!” Ji laughed. “Always arranging happy matches.” “This is one good deed I have to do. A’qiao's heart has already changed. Why should I insist on keeping her? As for Commissioner He, it's entirely a business calculation—placing a bet, you might say. If I win, everyone benefits.” Ji understood that “everyone” included himself and Wang, and felt more strongly than ever that Hu was a friend worth having. Yet he was a man of some learning, and did not consider obtaining influence through a woman's relationship particularly honourable. He therefore offered no comment.

“Now tell me about the woman in your rooms,” Hu said. “What's the story?” Ji smiled. “What do you think it is?” “How could I guess? Tell me yourself.” “She's Ruiyun's younger cousin. She married in Changshu and was widowed last year. Her parents-in-law wouldn't have her, so she wrote to Ruiyun asking to come and depend on her. Ruiyun asked what I thought. Surely I'm not so mean that I begrudge feeding one idle person? I took the opportunity of this official journey to Suzhou to arrange to bring her back to Hangzhou.” “What a pity!” Hu said with deep regret. “Widowed so young.” His evident sympathy for the young woman gave Ji an idea, but he immediately checked himself. Better not meddle. He merely nodded. “Naturally, she'll marry again eventually. If you know a suitable man—someone like Chen Shilong, for instance—do keep her in mind.” “Certainly.” Hu's answer was earnest. “I'll find someone for her.”

That subject passed, and Ji asked about public affairs. “How are things in Shanghai?” “The Small Swords Society amounts to little by itself, but the foreigners are behind it. I'm afraid the county town won't be recovered soon. The longer things remain as they are, the more the foreigners benefit.” “How?” Ji had lately become very interested in foreign affairs. Looking hard at Hu, he said, “Explain your reasoning.” “First, the settlement was originally waste ground. Land without people is useless. Now the disorder has driven everyone to seek refuge there. People create a market; as trade flourishes, the foreigners' revenues increase. Second, both sides want to win them over. While the snipe and clam struggle, the fisherman profits. The foreigners are happy to manipulate matters between them.” “In what way?” “They speak as men to men and as ghosts to ghosts. If you want their help, you must first do business with them. Governor-General Yiliang of Liangjiang has now decided to cut off their supplies. I don't think that policy can last.” Hu went on to relate many strange stories involving foreigners in the settlement. They made excellent entertainment over wine for Ji and taught him a good deal. Yet he felt that Hu too had certain prejudices. The foreigners were shrewd, but their shrewdness rested on reason. If one had reason on one's side, negotiations need not be difficult. The worst obstacles were one's own suspicions or insistence on preserving the empty prestige of the “great Celestial Empire.” The foreigners wanted a plain, sincere statement, while one's own side kept putting them off with evasions. Under those conditions agreement would never be possible. These ideas, however, remained untested. Moreover, prevailing opinion favoured round abuse of foreigners. Anyone who offered a fair-minded word was certain to be denounced by the guardians of orthodoxy for failing to distinguish Chinese civilisation from the foreign barbarians, perhaps even accused of taking a thief for his father or being a traitor. Ji therefore hesitated to express his view openly, even to so close a friend as Hu.

They turned to Zhejiang's political affairs. Ji too thought Huang Zonghan's transfer was merely a matter of time. The clearest sign was Huang's own preparation for departure. He was not only pressing hard for the customary unofficial payments and benefits that fell to him, but reaching out for gains that should not have been his at all. These were the signs of a man ready to pack his bags at any moment. “Then, Elder Brother, do you think Commissioner He has a chance of going to Zhejiang?” Hu asked with concern. “How could we know? There's no need to worry about it now.” Hu frankly explained his particular interest. After this Shanghai silk business, banking would remain the foundation of his enterprises. That principle was settled. Yet Shanghai's prospects were so brilliant that he was reluctant to abandon it. His idea was that influence among officials and foreigners would strengthen his influence in commerce. If He Guiqing became governor of Zhejiang, Wang's old connection with him, added to Hu's own immediate rapport with him in Suzhou, would give them unrivalled official support. Whatever business he attempted, whether moving capital or passing checkpoints, all would go smoothly. “That's why I must judge which way the wind is blowing and prepare early. If there's no hope of He's appointment to Zhejiang, I must reduce my operations and proceed steadily, securing each step. If the appointment is reliable, I have plenty more plans to bring out. Otherwise, wouldn't I be mad to leave so much business waiting in Shanghai and Hangzhou and come to Suzhou to deal with men like Xiaogouzi?” At this, Ji naturally felt bound to consider the matter seriously. He nodded without answering and thought carefully over his wine.

“I think there's a chance,” Ji began with a conclusion that pleased Hu. “Their yiwei examination class keeps in close touch and stands firmly together. Once they secure a provincial post, they don't easily let it go. Zhang Fu, the governor of Jiangxi, headed the second class in their yiwei palace examination and was one of Muzhang’a's ‘Ten Disciples.’ He was dismissed in the first month of this year. Last month they promptly put forward another classmate, Zheng Dunjin, to become governor of Henan. Both the present governors of Jiangsu and Zhejiang belong to that class. I hear Governor Xu in Jiangsu has lost imperial favour; there have long been reports that he'll be moved. If Governor Huang goes too, they'll lose two important territories at once and naturally seek to recover elsewhere what they've lost. Looked at that way, He's succession in Zhejiang is very possible. There's another consideration: this gentleman would also like one of his own people to follow him.” Dipping his chopstick in wine, Ji wrote the surname Huang on the table. He meant Huang Zonghan. “What makes you think so?” Hu was listening intently. “For the same reason that I succeeded Mr Xuexuan at the Sea Transport Bureau.” “Ah! One word wakes a dreamer!” Hu understood at once. After a moment's thought, he struck the table. “It's more than a chance. It's practically certain.”

He went on to develop the argument further. Huang Zonghan was calculating and devious. Though currently in favour, he had plenty of enemies. With so many things amiss in his administration of Zhejiang, he must worry about who would succeed him. Officials protecting one another was a practice common to all eighteen provinces. A successor generally did his best to cover his predecessor's mistakes. It was not unusual for a popular official's deficits to be cleared by his successor at a superior's instruction. There were only two exceptions: where the successor's own interests conflicted with such protection, or where an old enmity offered a chance for revenge. It was the latter that Huang had to fear. Though Zhejiang fell under the governor-general of Fujian and Zhejiang, that official resided in Fuzhou. Zhejiang's governor was in every real sense the province's highest authority. If his successor discovered a fault, he could impeach him in a direct memorial without consulting the governor-general. There would be no room for anyone to soften the blow. Huang therefore had to find a friend to succeed him, and his friendship with He was beyond question. “Yes. My view is much the same,” Ji agreed. “But if the court means to give He an important post,” Hu objected, “and Governor Xu is to leave Jiangsu, why not appoint He there? He knows the province. Wouldn't that be the natural arrangement?” “No, for two reasons. First, He has often submitted memorials about military affairs in Jiangsu, with remarks that reflect badly on Governor Xu. As members of the same examination class, they must avoid any appearance of disloyalty. Even if the court appointed him, He would fear being accused of writing those memorials in order to replace Xu, and would certainly refuse.” Ji drank, then continued. “Second, the governor of Jiangsu must command troops in battle. Military merit counts for most at present. Ji’erhang’a, the provincial treasurer, is working hard against the Small Swords in Shanghai. In my view, if Xu is transferred, Ji’erhang’a will probably succeed him.” This analysis reassured Hu still further. He now felt sure that He Guiqing would become governor of Zhejiang; only the timing remained uncertain. A delay would disadvantage Hu but help Ji, for it would mean He first serving a term as granary vice-minister. When Ji's sea-transported tribute grain was inspected at Tongzhou, he could then enjoy considerable assistance.

Having worked through all this, Hu felt that either course would benefit them. His appetite for both wine and conversation increased, and he drank until thoroughly drunk before returning with Ji to the inn. The next morning an attendant told him that Ji had gone out early to pay calls, leaving word that he would certainly be back at noon and wanted Hu to wait. Sitting alone was tedious. Hu also needed news from Zhou and had to see A’qiao, so he decided to return to Jinchang Inn, leaving a message that he would visit Ji again that afternoon. Before going out through Chang Gate, he called on A’qiao, briefly explained what had happened, and said he must stay another day. This was good news to her. She decided to go straight to Mudu and bring her brother to meet him. “Good. We might as well settle everything. But you can't manage it alone. When Zhou returns, I'll ask him to take the trouble of going to Mudu once more and accompany you. You should meet my sworn elder brother too.” A’qiao therefore went back with him to Jinchang Inn, carrying a large bundle of clothes. Some were gifts from her friend; others had been newly made during the past two days. The Pans kept a cutting table set up all year and employed two seamstresses by the day. Except during the three great festivals, they made new clothes every day. Being so close to the source was wonderfully convenient. Naturally, A’qiao knew Hu's temper and would not wear before him clothes that were in fact brand-new but counted as second-hand because her friend had given them to her. Her newly made garments were less fashionable than those of the foreign settlement, but they had the virtue of dignity. She changed her hair and ornaments to match, abandoning elaborate cosmetics, dressing her hair to a smooth shine, and wearing only a green jade hairpin in the bun and pearl earrings. No stranger seeing her could have detected a trace of the pleasure quarters.

Just as she had finished dressing at Jinchang Inn and was preparing to accompany Hu to see Ji after lunch, their intended host arrived instead. Hu introduced them. A’qiao's manner was so respectful that Ji took a strong liking to her and praised her warmly to her face. Once she had withdrawn to the inner room, he could not hold back. “Xueyan, I know a little of the Liuzhuang method of reading faces. This beauty past her first youth has considerable good fortune ahead.” “Then I'm doing the right thing.” Hu smiled. “Watch her walk: her skirt scarcely moves. She's so composed, she looks like a great official's lady.” “Otherwise...” Ji stopped abruptly. “Why don't you finish?” Hu could not resist asking. “There's a great deal behind that ‘otherwise.’” After a long pause, Ji waved his hand. “Never mind. Saying it would only unsettle you. In any case, what you lose at sunrise you regain at sunset. It makes little difference.” Hu understood the expression. Giving up A’qiao was a pity, but there would be compensation, naturally through He Guiqing. Because Ji put it that way, Hu began to take those future compensations especially seriously. The autumn harvest depended on spring cultivation. From this moment, he felt, he must make fresh efforts with He. After considering, he changed the subject, though it still concerned He and A’qiao.

“Elder Brother, there's something I meant to ask. I'd like you to write a letter for me.” “To whom?” “Commissioner He. It must be beautifully written, preferably in the four-and-six style...” “How did you think of that?” Ji interrupted with a laugh. “You're setting me an examination. Parallel prose requires reference books. To put it bluntly, one assembles a composition from quotations like an otter laying out its catch. Where am I to find something like the Peiwen Treasury of Rhymes while travelling?” Hu did not understand the explanation, but guessed that he had asked something difficult. He knew that not everyone could compose those elegantly balanced four- and six-character phrases, and regretted having embarrassed Ji by proposing it so casually. Seeing his thoughts, Ji hastened to change his tone. “Never mind. Since it's for you, I'll have to make an attempt. Tell me what you want to say.” Hu was delighted. “First, thank him, naturally with the customary expressions of admiration. Second, explain that A’qiao is staying with the Pans, that I owe them a debt, and ask him to thank them on my behalf.” “Third,” Ji added with a smile, “ask him to take care of the lady.” “That's what I have in mind, but I don't know how to put it.” “I do,” Ji said confidently. “I'll find a couple of allusions to hint discreetly at the meaning.” “Exactly.” Hu spoke with admiration and a touch of regret. “Your brush is a formidable thing. At school as a boy, I remember reading two lines: ‘Others carry treasured swords; my brush is like a blade.’ I used to wonder how a writing brush could be more powerful than a sword. Now I understand the words carved on some brushes: ‘Sweep away a thousand armies.’ They're perfectly true.”

“Not quite so formidable.” This reminded Ji of something Hu lacked. “I've wanted to tell you something for a long time. With your present responsibilities, you really must make proper use of several people. However fine the peony, it needs green leaves to support it. Relying on yourself alone, even with three heads and six arms, there will be times when you can't be everywhere.” The remark touched exactly the point troubling Hu. Slapping his thigh, he exclaimed, “Precisely! I've long meant to ask your advice. I've made plans too. I want two men: one to represent me in social dealings—I have him already, Liu Bucai—and another to handle my writing. There's a man surnamed Huang in Huzhou who was to come to Hangzhou with me, but other circumstances ended that plan. I don't think his abilities are very great, either. What I want to discuss with you...” He struggled to express himself. “I still don't really know how to use such people.” Ji pictured Hu's circumstances and saw his difficulty clearly. He began with the broader point. “To speak frankly, you've read too little to know how to expand your establishment. Money alone is no use. You need people. It doesn't matter if you lack knowledge yourself, provided you respect those who have it. It doesn't matter if the people you employ lack ability, either. Once you're known to welcome men, capable ones will naturally come to your door.” From the story of paying a thousand in gold for a horse's bones, Ji went on to Lord Mengchang's retainers, the imitator of a cock's crow and the petty thief. Hu listened with mounting excitement and found inspiration everywhere. When Ji finished, he exclaimed in admiration, feeling that an obstruction in his mind had suddenly cleared.

“I understand!” Hu nodded repeatedly. “Rushing about like this is no way to carry on. I must find a spacious, comfortable place and support a group of men with nothing particular to do. If they do nothing for three years, never mind. One useful act may be worth three years of their upkeep.” “Now you truly understand,” Ji said with deep satisfaction. “That's what household retainers are. The salt merchants of Yangzhou preserve much of Lord Mengchang's tradition. You might take notice of them.” Hu did not answer. He calculated carefully for a long time, then sprang to his feet. “That's settled. When I return, I'm changing my methods.” “How?” “Employ people!” Hu clapped his hands. “I'll stay at headquarters and go out myself only when there's no alternative.” “Exactly. Only then can your establishment grow. I'll help you.” “Naturally. You're my Zhuge Liang.” “I don't deserve that.” Ji laughed, then seemed a little uneasy. “You're expansive by nature. Keep some limits in mind when following my advice. If you suddenly spread yourself too widely and can't sustain it, that will be far from what I intended.” “Don't worry, Elder Brother.” Only now did Hu feel he had the advantage over Ji. “So long as we're talking about moving sums within a few hundred thousand taels, there will be no trouble.” “As long as you're sure.” Ji rose. “I'll go back and write that letter for you as soon as I can.” “Have you an engagement, or calls to make?” “Neither.” “Then why not begin here?”
