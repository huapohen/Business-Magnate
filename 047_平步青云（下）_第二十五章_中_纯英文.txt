INSTALLMENT 047
A Swift Rise to Prominence, Volume III — Chapter Twenty-Five, Part Two

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

Hearing them, A’qiao came out too and urged Ji to stay for a simple meal. There was nothing objectionable about that, and he agreed. “Don't prepare too many dishes,” he said. “There's only one thing I want. Is there a Lugaojian shop nearby?” “They're everywhere. I'll send someone for pork in soy sauce.” “Not the pork. I want the stuff they use to seal the pot while they cook it.” When a great cauldron of pork was ready to simmer over a low fire, strips of noodle dough were used to seal the lid, keeping the steam and flavour in. The dough was edible, but hardly fit for a distinguished table. A’qiao laughed. “They sell that to beggars!” “Never mind,” Hu put in, knowing Ji's ways. “Just send someone to buy it.” The conversation turned to Lugaojian. Hu and Ji shared the same puzzle: why did all Suzhou's shops selling pork in soy sauce and other cooked meats use that name? Were they branches belonging to one proprietor, or like the Xiaoquan scissors shop in Hangzhou, with only one genuine establishment and all the others imitations? “Most are imitations, of course,” A’qiao said. “But why Lugaojian? What a curious name. There must be a story behind it.”

“There is.” With perfect seriousness, A’qiao told the legend. An ancestor of the Lu family had begun with a little shop selling pork in soy sauce. A beggar came every day, and the owner, a kindly, generous man, always took up his knife and cut him a large piece of meat. Eventually the beggar began sleeping beneath the family's eaves. One day he suddenly disappeared, leaving only a ragged straw mat. It lay discarded in a corner, and nobody paid it any attention. Once, when a batch of meat was almost ready, the owner found that it needed one fierce burst of heat to finish it. The best fuel would be straw, which Suzhou people called “rice firewood”, but none could be found at the moment. The old mat came conveniently to hand. Who could have guessed that burning it would send the aroma of the pork drifting for several li? Business prospered from then on. To remember the origin of their good fortune, they named the shop Lugaojian: Lu's Straw Mat. “Gao means grain stalks,” Ji observed. “A learned man must have chosen the name. But I still don't understand why the mat made the aroma of the pork travel for several li.” “Naturally, it carried an immortal's influence. That wasn't a real beggar. It was Lü Dongbin come down to earth.” “So Immortal Lü was amusing himself among mortals.” “Nonsense!” Hu laughed. “Whenever someone prospers, there's always a fantastic story about him. It's the same when a business succeeds.”

“To inspire such a wildly improbable tale would itself be something to be proud of,” Ji replied. “May future generations tell many strange stories about Hu Xueyan.” “I don't want fame after death,” Hu answered casually. “I want it while I'm alive. If one day Fukang signs are as common everywhere as Lugaojian signs in Suzhou, I shan't have lived for nothing.” “That isn't impossible. It depends on whether you can set yourself the ambition.” Ji was encouraging his sworn brother. Hu replied without thinking, “The resolve is mine; success rests with others.” “Well said!” Ji was full of admiration. “Xueyan, your words are truly extraordinary.” “Don't flatter me, Elder Brother,” Hu said, pleased despite his modest reply. “It isn't flattery. Those two phrases show real understanding. The old saying is, ‘Man makes the plans, but Heaven determines success.’ What sort of man cannot command his own fate? Your way is right: first beware of lacking ambition, then of lacking the right men.” Hu's face grew hot. He felt that he did not deserve this praise. What he had meant was little different from leaving success to Heaven: whether things worked out depended on other people. Ji had interpreted “rests with others” as “finding the right people”. That had not been Hu's intention, though the interpretation was certainly wiser than his own meaning.

“To have ambition without knowing how to recognize and employ people is what we call ‘great ambition and little ability’. Such a man is born to suffer,” Ji continued. “When unsuccessful, he thinks his genius has been buried and is full of grievances. If chance brings him a great opportunity and he rises high, things become worse.” Listening intently, Hu could not help exclaiming, “Why should that be?” “Because the higher he climbs, the harder he falls. He has risen through chance, or because someone deliberately raised him up. Holding the place once he's been put there is no easy matter. When he tumbles, even if he doesn't lose his life, he'll be badly battered. A man whose ambitions exceed his abilities suffers whatever happens. Look through history: many heroes from humble beginnings became kings and emperors when circumstance favoured them, only to wake from their brief dream and lose their lives. Most came to grief through precisely that failing.” Hu understood and learned a great deal. Every long talk with Ji left him feeling enriched and deeply aware of the pleasures of friendship. Yet this pleasure differed greatly from what he felt with Yu Si, You Laowu, or even Wang Youling. “To tell the truth, my understanding falls far short of yours,” he said with complete sincerity. “A man really ought to read.” “‘Understanding the world is learning too.’ A man who rises through memorizing books and writing eight-legged essays may be worth less than an illiterate who understands people and their affairs.” “Now you're airing a grievance again!” Hu knew that officials who had passed the examinations looked down on men who had purchased rank, but able men among the latter equally despised bookish fools among the former. “Call it a grievance or call it the truth. Either will do.”

“Then let me say something truthful—and entirely serious.” A thought had occurred to Hu. “Elder Brother, do you still intend to sit the examinations?” Ji was a licentiate, commonly called a xiucai, so this meant the provincial examination. Naturally, he had considered it. “It isn't so easy. I waited for employment for years and have only just obtained a post. If I resign to take the provincial examination, even if luck favours me, there's still the metropolitan examination. Where would the money come from?” “Why worry about that? Leave it all to me.” “Given our friendship, if I truly had the ambition to try my fortunes in the autumn examination, I should have to trouble you. But when I consider it, there seems little point.” “Why? This year is jiayin; next year, yimao, is the year of the great examination. There's more than a year to prepare. You could study properly.” Ji had long abandoned the idea and shook his head. “In troubled times, a man can win distinction anywhere. Why must he seek his standing in an examination hall? The greater the disorder, the more opportunities there are. I imagine you understand why better than I do.” This was another revelation. Hu saw that it was true. All his business had been connected with the state of the country. In a peaceful, prosperous age, he might not have done so well. Thinking further, he realized that peace would have given his ability to adapt no occasion to show itself. He might simply have led an ordinary life and rotted away like grass and trees. “Only troubled times bring out men of talent!” he exclaimed. “Others have said that too.” Ji sounded approving. “In five thousand years of history, talent flourished most at the end of Qin and beginning of Han, and when Wei, Shu and Wu divided the empire. Those were troubled times.” “And now? This too is an age of disorder. I wonder how many men of talent later generations will find in it.” “Not a few. To speak only of the present, don't undervalue yourself, Xueyan. You're a rare talent yourself.”

Hu smiled without answering. Just then A’qiao came to call them to the meal. The dishes ordered from a restaurant were plentiful, with a large platter of Lugaojian pork besides. Naturally, the humble food unfit for grand tables was there too. “Come and eat with us,” Hu told her. “There isn't any rule permitting that.” She declined with a smile. “There are no outsiders here,” Ji put in. “It's the first visit to Suzhou for both of us. We want you to tell us about its people and customs.” Further ceremony would now seem unfriendly. A’qiao reflected that she would have to attend to the table in any case. Standing would be awkward; better sit. She therefore took a place at the side, pouring wine and serving dishes as hostess while talking with Ji about everyday matters. Suzhou women were skilled in conversation, and she had experience besides. Her tactful words and pleasant voice increased Ji's liking for her. The meal consequently lasted a long time, ending only after four. No sooner had Ji left than Zhou Yiming arrived. The negotiations over A’qiao's affairs had gone smoothly. Only payment remained before the transaction could be completed. “Congratulations!” Hu said to her with a smile. “You've thrown off your fetters.” “Thank you for all your trouble, Mr Zhou.” After thanking him, she added apologetically, “I'm afraid I shall need your help again tomorrow.” Hu explained that Zhou was to accompany her to Mudu once more and bring out her younger brother. Naturally, he agreed without hesitation.

By the time they had discussed everything, it was supper time. Hu asked Zhou to stay and eat. He himself drank tea to keep him company, chatting while privately considering a plan. When he had made up his mind, he asked casually, “Old Zhou, have you ever imagined what your life would be like if you prospered?” Zhou had no answer ready. “I've never thought about it,” he replied frankly. “I get through one day at a time.” “Surely you've thought about what sort of official you might become, or what sort of wife you'd marry?” “I have a wife at home. She came to our family as a child, and she's two years older than me. A very good woman. I've always wanted to bring her here, but never managed it.” “There must be a reason. Why haven't you managed?” “Isn't it easy enough to see? It all comes down to money.” Zhou spoke with deep feeling. “These last two years, I've barely managed on my own, running errands for people and unable to settle anywhere. I daren't do anything so rash.” “What would have to change before you could bring her?” Zhou's expression brightened. “Now there's hope, thanks to your help. If I get a post in Yangzhou with a dependable income of two hundred taels a year, I'll send for her and let her enjoy a few peaceful days.” “That isn't much. In my view, a man like you ought to have at least fifty taels a month to be properly valued.” “Where could I find such good fortune? If anyone gave me that, I'd serve him faithfully for the rest of my life.”

“Do you mean that?” Zhou had spoken without thinking, but now he saw that Hu looked quite serious and dared not answer carelessly. “We're only chatting,” Hu said in a gentler tone. “There's no obligation.” Even so, Zhou had to consider it carefully. He could not quite tell what Hu intended. “If you wanted me, sir, I'd naturally be willing.” “That's not what I mean.” Hu waved his hand. “I don't like anyone to work for me out of obligation.” “I mean it. Working for you is a real pleasure. Never mind fifty taels a month—I'd be delighted with half.” Seeing his sincerity, Hu explained his intention. After that day's long conversation with Ji, he had decided to employ Zhou. He proposed an annual salary of six hundred taels, with a further bonus at year's end according to the profits. Zhou should think it over carefully before answering. If he preferred to go to Yangzhou, Hu would understand: a post at a lijin station did, after all, make a man part of officialdom. “Who wants that kind of official standing? I needn't think it over. I can tell you now: I shall do whatever you ask.” “Excellent!” Hu said gladly. “From now on, you're one of our own.”

The new relationship made Zhou more constrained rather than less. He could no longer sit there drinking by himself and hurried through his meal. After clearing the table himself, he asked about A’qiao's journey the next day. “I'm entrusting her to you. Once the document has been drawn up tomorrow, accompany her to Mudu. When everything is settled, bring her brother to Shanghai. I'll write down my addresses there and in Hangzhou for you.” Hearing this, A’qiao came over. “And what about you?” “I'll see what Elder Brother Ji wants. I'll spend another day with him tomorrow, and I really ought to leave early the day after. With Old Zhou to look after you, you can take your time and stay a few extra days in Mudu. If anything comes up afterwards, I'll send him to make arrangements with you. In short, once you've escorted the Buddha, you must see him all the way to the Western Paradise. I won't consider this great business finished until you're properly settled.” First, Zhou was present, and A’qiao did not want an outsider to glimpse the private understanding between her and Hu. Second, her view of Hu had changed again during the past two or three days. She therefore made her attitude clear. “What's all this about escorting the Buddha to the Western Paradise? I don't understand!”

Whether she truly failed to understand or only pretended, she plainly did not welcome his well-meant promise to see everything through. Hu too realized that his words had been a little indiscreet and wisely dropped the subject. He turned to Zhou. “Go home early. If you have any unfinished business of your own, clear it up as soon as possible. There's something I ought to tell you now. In my work, we have to move quickly. There may be nothing to do for ten days or a fortnight, but once something comes up, we act at once. To say something that may sound unreasonable, there may not even be time for you to go home and tell your family. Naturally, I'll look after them. Whatever difficulty arises, however great, I'll settle it. Anyone I send out on business—to put it in a literary phrase—will have no worries about those left behind. Remember that when you work for me.” “Your Honour...” “Wait!” Hu cut him off. “We must change how you address me. I bought this ‘Your Honour’ with silver. I'm not a real magistrate presiding over cases in court. If a stranger chooses to address me that way, I may feel undeserving, but I do have a board certificate and count as an official of sorts. The court's established forms must be respected, so to insist on refusing the title would be discourteous. In business, we have no first or second official masters, only first or second proprietors. Yet I don't like dividing people into employers and employees either. I have two other ‘friends’, Liu Qingsheng and Chen Shilong, both able assistants. They call me Mr Hu. Do the same. There are other things you can teach me; but in business, if I may boast, you must learn from me. Think of the title xiansheng, ‘Mr’, in its other sense: your teacher in business.” “Oh, dear!” A’qiao frowned in mock distress, with the beguiling petulance of a Suzhou woman. “What a lot of words!” Zhou answered seriously. “Many words, but every one useful. Mr Hu, I am ready for your instructions.” “Then it's settled. Come early tomorrow.”

Just as Zhou was about to leave, an inn servant brought in someone A’qiao recognized: an attendant from the Pan household. “That's Pan Fu,” she told Hu after seeing him through the window. “I wonder why he's come. If it's anything to do with me, don't agree too readily.” She gave him a gentle push. Hu received the man in the outer room. After paying his respects, Pan Fu took a plum-red invitation from his card case and presented it. Pan Shuya, signing himself Hu's humble younger friend and pupil, invited him to lunch the next day. He had added an emphatic request: “Do please honour me by coming.” This was unexpected. Pan was a young master through and through, reluctant to bother with people who did not concern him. For that reason, on his several recent visits to the house, Hu had not asked to see its master. Such a sudden invitation must have a purpose, and Hu naturally asked what it was. “Nothing but admiration for you, sir,” Pan Fu replied. “No other guests have been invited. My master especially wishes to entertain you alone.” Hu could not imagine what Pan intended, but there was no need to puzzle over it now. He would learn at lunch. He took a reply card, confirmed that he would attend and asked Pan Fu to convey his thanks. “My master also asked whether you'll remain here tomorrow morning or go elsewhere. Please let us know where to send the chair.” “Probably here, but there's no need to send one.” “He insists. Without it, he says, he would not be showing proper respect.” In that case, further refusals were unnecessary. After Pan Fu left, Hu and A’qiao discussed the invitation. They agreed that the emphatic request and the offer of a chair must mean Pan had an important favour to ask. Even A’qiao, who had stayed in the house for several days, could not guess what it was.

“Never mind,” Hu said. “Let Old Zhou accompany you into the city. Sound out the Pan mistress while you're there, and find some way to let me know before I meet him tomorrow.” A’qiao still seemed reluctant to leave, but could say little in Zhou's presence. At last she hired a chair and went into the city. The night passed without incident. Early the next morning, Hu went in for a stroll. Ji was out, so he returned quietly to Jinchang Inn without disturbing Ruiyun's cousin. Just after eleven, the chair from the Pans arrived. It was a grand official chair. On inquiry, Hu learned that soon after Pan's birth, his father had followed the example of the Yangzhou salt merchants and spent twenty thousand taels to buy him the rank of circuit intendant. As a third-rank official, he was entitled to a large chair covered in green woollen cloth. He could even have mounted outriders, though he had not adopted that part of the official display. The chair itself was splendid: glass windows on three sides with coloured silk curtains, bright tassels along the eaves, and brass-covered poles polished to a shine. Inside were a covered tea bowl, fruit, snacks, a water pipe and two novels, Flower Shadows Behind the Curtain and An Old Rustic's Unvarnished Words. On a long journey, there would be no lack of diversion.

Hu had never ridden in such a large chair before. Watching the four bearers, he remembered one of Ji's jokes. Each man, Ji said, could be described in four characters. The first held his head high: “head up, spirits lifted”. The second “dared not let wind”, since he was directly in front of the gentleman, who would suffer the first blast. Of the two behind, the nearer man's view was blocked by the chair: he “couldn't tell east from west”. The last simply followed every step: “no ideas of his own”. The Grand Councillors were said to be much the same. Their leader naturally held his head high. Since he alone customarily spoke during an imperial audience, the second dared not let wind. The third was new to the council and still ignorant of its workings; not knowing east from west described him perfectly. The last, commonly called the “curtain-lifting Grand Councillor”, naturally had no ideas of his own. This brought Hu's thoughts to Peng Yunzhang, He's fellow graduate and a Grand Councillor. Where did he stand? If he was the one who dared not let wind, how could he speak on He's behalf? Hu must ask He about it when an opportunity arose.

While his thoughts wandered in this fashion, they reached the Pan house. The chair was carried right up to the eaves of the great hall. Only then did Hu discover that Pan Fu's words were unreliable: besides the host, two other guests were present, both dressed like wealthy young gentlemen. After host and guest exchanged bows, Pan made the introductions. Hu had guessed correctly. One was Wu Jizhong, the other Lu Zhixiang. Both came from distinguished families, with fathers and elder brothers holding posts in the capital; both were themselves licentiates. They exchanged the customary expressions of long-standing admiration, and Pan led them into the garden. It was large, but the buildings looked old. Only a boat-shaped pavilion of nanmu timber was new, and it was here that Pan entertained them. Male servants waited outside. Within, two maids attended them, exemplifying the prettiness of Suzhou girls: matching cotton tunics trimmed with patterned borders, loose-legged trousers, tightly drawn waists, glossy braids and nails dyed bright red. Their voices were as clear as orioles'. Pan, Wu and Lu were all “Young Master”; Hu alone was “Master Hu”. At noon, the meal was served in the pavilion. Pan offered Hu the seat of honour and forestalled his refusal. Hu was the only guest, he explained. Wu and Lu were not even fellow guests invited to keep him company. The three were jointly his hosts, and had something to consult him about. Pan Fu's words had been correct after all.

Hu had already expected a request. After three rounds of wine, he asked first, and Pan explained the circumstances. It had begun with A’qiao's conversation. While passing idle hours with the Pan mistress in the women's quarters, she had entertained her with stories heard from You Laowu, Second Miss and Hu himself. His adventures were unusual enough, and A’qiao's eloquence and vivid embellishments made them especially absorbing. The mistress was fascinated. The stories then travelled by way of the pillow to Pan's ears, changing his view of Hu entirely. Young men of privilege never chose ordinary, upright gentlemen as friends. They wanted “interesting” people: brilliant talkers, striking figures or men who handled affairs with style. Hu seemed to Pan precisely such a man. What impressed him most, however, was Hu's loyalty to his friends. For that reason, he had an important favour to ask. “Brother Hu, Suzhou has never been so unsettled. The government troops do more to disturb the people than to protect them. All three of us would like to see the foreign settlement in Shanghai, and we'd like you to look after us.” “Certainly,” Hu answered calmly. He supposed this meant such small matters as buying houses. “Gladly. Leave it all to me.” “This is what I have in mind. I have some ready money to entrust to you. What could you put it into? It isn't a great sum—about a hundred and twenty or a hundred and thirty thousand taels.”

A hundred and twenty or a hundred and thirty thousand, and he called it a small sum! What an expansive way of talking. Hu was about to answer when Wu spoke first. “My situation is much the same as Shuya's. I have a hundred thousand taels and would also like you to take the trouble to employ them for me.” “Mine is a little different,” Lu said. “I have a chest of things I'm uneasy about leaving in Suzhou. Could you advise me where to put it safely?” “What sort of things?” “A chest of paintings.” “The Lu family's collection is famous,” Pan explained. “Some of its finest pieces have been handed down since the Ming.” That alone showed how precious the chest must be. Hu dared not answer lightly. He merely nodded. “We can discuss it further.” “Discuss it” was an evasion. He had already decided against such a thankless burden. A thankless burden alone might be tolerable; the real danger was that something could go wrong. Antiques and ancient paintings could not be replaced with identical objects. It was a prudent decision.

Lu, however, wanted Hu to guarantee the paintings' safety during their removal from the endangered city to the settlement under foreign protection. He therefore mentioned You Laowu. “I hear you are a close friend of the leaders of the grain-transport brotherhood?” There was no denying this, nor any need to. “Yes. Both generations of leaders in Songjiang think well of me.” It occurred to Hu that Lu must want You's help. He might as well be generous without waiting to be asked. “If there's anything you need, so long as it's within my power, I can intercede.” “Yes, that's what I should like you to do.” They went on to talk at length about the Songjiang brotherhood's influence, leaving their immediate business aside. As Hu maintained the conversation, he observed the three men calmly. He soon understood that their wish to move to Shanghai arose half from fear of war and half from longing for the settlement's prosperity. The first thing to arrange for them there would therefore be a house for each. The plans quickly took shape in his mind: Western houses with all manner of imported furnishings—soft sofas, great dining tables, and fireplaces burning coal or wood. This also suggested a way to make Pan's and Wu's money productive. It would mean flying the kite far out on a long line; if they expected quick gains, there was nothing to discuss. If they took a long view, it could greatly assist his own business too. With two hundred thousand taels available for a long period, why not open another bank in Shanghai? Only then did he realize what an excellent opportunity he had found. He thought it through carefully, then waited for a pause in the conversation. “There's something I'd like to ask.”

“Of course. Please do.” “You both honour me with your confidence, and I must do my best. But you must have some intention for this money. Is it for trade or for lending at interest? If the latter, for a long term or a short one? I need to know your general purpose before I can act.” Pan glanced at Wu, seeking his view. Wu spoke only for himself. “Brother Hu, I don't know what I should do. I can only tell you my thoughts. If we have to flee, our income in Suzhou will stop. We shan't be able to collect the rent rice from our fields. Who knows whether our shop properties will even survive, let alone yield rent? In that case, all eighteen members of my household will depend on this money for their living. That's the truth. Please act accordingly.” “Mine is much the same,” Pan said. “There are only thirteen in my own household, but a great many in my clan. If they all flee to Shanghai and cannot maintain themselves, I'll have to help.” “I understand. If Suzhou falls, who can say when it will be recovered? Six months or a year, three years or five—nobody knows. Since this money must support you in exile, it must provide a steady stream. Security comes first.” “A steady stream—exactly!” Wu said with relief. “That's what I mean.” Hu nodded, laid down his chopsticks and placed both hands on the table, assuming a grave expression. “You're laying a heavy responsibility on me. I can only do my utmost. I think we should make provision on several fronts. First, you need homes of your own in the settlement. Second, we should calculate your monthly expenses and set aside enough to lend at interest. Spend the interest and leave the principal intact. With a house and daily expenses provided for, your home base will be secure. Then we can act more freely with the remaining money, buying land or trading. Even at the worst, if that portion is entirely lost, your living will remain secure and your foundations unharmed. Would that suit you?” “Of course! Excellent.” Wu turned to Pan. “Shuya, Brother Hu is truly a man of foresight. Admirable!”

Since Pan had brought Hu into their circle, this praise reflected honour on him, and he was delighted. In his excitement he called to a maid, “Tell the mistress there's a card case in the iron chest. Bring it here with the key.” “Wait!” Hu stopped her hastily. “Don't give me anything yet.” “It makes no difference. I have fifty or sixty thousand taels in notes at home. I'll hand them over to you first.” “No. In banking, trust comes first and proper procedure second. Once we've agreed the arrangements, you can deposit your funds at the bank and receive passbooks. The interest and the length of the deposits must both be considered carefully.” “Very well. Tell us what to do.” Hu began with buying land and building houses, and continued through to trade in foreign goods. A broad plan emerged. The budget for buying land and building was to be limited to ten thousand taels. The rest would be divided equally: half in a five-year deposit and half in a demand deposit to serve as trading capital. Hu would introduce them to a bank. In practice, the whole amount would be available for a long term, because even the trading half, though withdrawable on demand, would be employed according to his advice. If he was short of funds, he could simply advise against a particular transaction. Whichever way matters went, everything would depend on a word from him.

“Good. Let's do that.” Pan turned to Lu. “What about you? What are your plans?” “After listening to all this lively discussion, naturally I must make plans too. If we build our houses together in Shanghai, it will be easy to visit.” One after another, they spoke of the pleasures of living close together and seeing one another every day. Hu observed coolly. These three wealthy gentlemen differed from Pang Er, Gao Si, Zhou Wu and their circle. Zhou, Gao and the others had at least managed businesses themselves and were comparatively shrewd—which, paradoxically, made them easier to deal with. These three had never known the difficulties of earning a living. Their hopes might be too high and unrealistic; their lack of worldly experience might leave them unable to judge good from bad. While they were talking, everything sounded excellent. Once plans were carried out and fell short of their ideals, ugly complaints might follow. Such thankless effort would hardly be worthwhile. “Have any of you been to Shanghai?” he asked. “I went once, when I was four, and remember nothing. The farthest either of them has been is Changshu.” “Then you've never really seen the settlement.” “Exactly. I'm forty-two now. When I was four, it was still the Jiaqing reign. There was no foreign settlement then.” “People all say how lively it is, but I must tell you this: its real prosperity lies in the future. Only a small area is busy now, and all sorts of people mingle there. It isn't suitable for refined gentlemen like yourselves, with your music, chess, calligraphy and painting. I have thought of a place where you could buy a large piece of land for your homes. It's still like the countryside. Once the foreigners extend their roads that far, it will become a quiet place within a busy district, ideal for living. But that's only my opinion. You must see it yourselves before deciding.”

“If you say it's good, that's enough. Buy it first.” “Third Brother Pan is right,” Hu replied frankly. “But this is our first undertaking together, and we have many years ahead. I want everything to go well the first time. Here's my suggestion: if any of you would like to come, I'll accompany him to Shanghai for an inspection. How would that be?” “An excellent idea!” Lu said eagerly. “I've long wanted to visit, but I was afraid of making a fool of myself without acquaintances or any knowledge of the settlement's customs. With you there, what have I to fear?” This stirred Pan and Wu as well. But Wu was devoted to his mother and reluctant to leave her side, while Pan had some property to dispose of and could not go far. They therefore agreed that Lu alone would make the journey. “When shall we leave?” he asked. “I'd thought of setting off tomorrow.” “Good heavens!” Lu was astonished. “How could I possibly be ready?” For a businessman, a long journey was commonplace; he could leave at a word. For a man like Lu, it was a great event. First an auspicious day for travel must be chosen. Then luggage must be prepared and gifts assembled for friends and relatives. Farewell banquets would follow on successive days, with calls to take leave in between. If he could depart within ten days, that would be quick. Hu understood all this. There was no point in bargaining over the date. Even waiting another two or three days would achieve nothing, while his own time was too precious to waste. “Fortunately, there's no urgency about this particular matter. Take your time. Once you've fixed the day, I'll send someone especially to fetch you, or come again myself. I guarantee you a safe, comfortable journey to Shanghai.” “Nothing could be better.” Lu fetched an almanac and first chose a day near the end of the month. Then, remembering that the Dragon Boat Festival was approaching and that he wanted to spend it at home, he finally settled on the auspicious seventh day of the fifth lunar month.
