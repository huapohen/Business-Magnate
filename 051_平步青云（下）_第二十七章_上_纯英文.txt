CHAPTER TWENTY-SEVEN

On reaching Songjiang, they moored below Xiuye Bridge and went ashore, stopping first at the You household to rest. Mrs You was greatly surprised and busied herself with greetings and arrangements. The family always kept plenty of guest rooms ready. Furong had already agreed to stay with the Seventh Lady; Mrs You now insisted that Hu and Qiu stay in the house as well. Once they had settled in, they went to see the Old Master. Since Qiu Fengyan was a stranger and an official, the old man observed every courtesy. He had his long robe and riding jacket brought out, dressed formally, and sat with them in solemn state. Qiu was deeply uncomfortable, and even Hu felt constrained. Fortunately, the Seventh Lady soon followed. Her easy, affectionate conversation dispelled the stiffness. After some small talk she came to the point. “Old Master, I have something very important to tell you today. Only I find it rather hard to say.” He had already seen that Qiu knew her well, so there was no need to restrain his own remarks. “Now that is something new!” he said, half smiling. “Is there anything you find hard to say?” “You make me sound quite shameless.” She rose with a laugh. “I shall go in and talk to Old Aunt first. Little Uncle can tell you for me.” Old Aunt was the Old Master’s sister, also over seventy, hard of hearing and slow of speech. There was little to discuss with her. The Seventh Lady merely wanted to withdraw so that Hu could speak of her marriage. Her unsettled future had long weighed on the Old Master. Hu’s detailed account delighted him, especially the news that Wang Youling, a prefect, had condescended to take a woman of her background in the brotherhood as his sworn sister. He considered that a great honour. It was all Hu’s doing, and, remembering the source of these blessings, he thanked him repeatedly. Since Qiu was to represent Hu and coordinate arrangements between the You and Wang families, he also entrusted the matter to Qiu and thanked him. His heartfelt sincerity was deeply moving.

“Old Master,” Hu said, finally turning to his own request, “there is something else. Since Brother Five is away, I must trouble you to muster a few men for me.” “Certainly!” the old man said eagerly. “Tell me what you need!” Hu explained that he wanted two men to accompany a shipment. He had expected this to be easily arranged, but the Old Master fell into thoughtful silence. Puzzled, Hu could not help asking, “Is there some difficulty?” “Yes. You are one of us, my young friend, and Mr Qiu already feels like an old acquaintance. This is a most unfortunate coincidence—and a fortunate one too. Never mind the unfortunate part. The fortunate part is that you have told me. Otherwise, I should have done a friend a very serious wrong.” Hu’s shrewdness and Qiu’s long experience both told them that something lay behind these words. They exchanged a glance. Naturally it was Hu who spoke. “Since you regard us as your own people, why not tell us the unfortunate part as well?” “There is no need. The misfortune is that Old Five is away. Had he been here, this would never have happened.” The Old Master calmly asked, “When do you intend to leave, Mr Qiu?”

“My cargo is still in Shanghai. Hiring boats and loading them will take three to five days. I shall follow your instructions.” “I would not presume to give instructions. Please go back to Shanghai tomorrow and make your preparations. Today is the fourteenth day of the fourth month. Be ready to sail on the twentieth. We shall meet in Shanghai on the nineteenth.” “What?” Hu did not understand his use of “we.” “Surely you do not mean…” “Yes. I shall escort Mr Qiu myself.” “How could we allow that?” Hu and Qiu exclaimed together. “No!” The old man made an emphatic gesture. “I must go in person.” His lips moved as though he had more to say. He looked at Qiu, but finally left the words unspoken. Recognising the seriousness of the situation, Hu abandoned ceremony. “Forgive me, Qiu. Would you sit outside for a moment? I must have a word with the Old Master.” “Of course.” Qiu understood and hastily rose to withdraw. “There is no need. Please stay here, Mr Qiu.” The Old Master rose and apologised. “I am very sorry. I must speak to our friend Hu about something within the brotherhood. I do not mean to treat you as an outsider, but, to be frank, there are things you would be better off not knowing.” With this explanation he took Hu to the family’s Buddhist shrine room, the most solemn and secluded place in the house. Hu understood such observances. He immediately assumed a grave expression, joined his palms, lowered his head and eyes, and silently paid reverence to the Buddha. Only then did he quietly take a seat below the scripture table.

The Old Master sat beside him and spoke slowly. “My young friend, I did not know you had a hand in this affair. Had I known earlier, things would have been easier. Now it is like a sore about to burst: there is nothing for it but to squeeze out the pus.” Hu listened closely, still unable to guess what connection the old man could have with the arms Qiu was escorting. One thing was clear: the Old Master was in a very difficult position. Yet Hu could not imagine where the difficulty lay. Among men of their world, consideration for one another mattered above everything. Hu felt that all other questions could wait; first he must make his own position clear. “Old Master, I know you treat my concerns as your own. Let us regard this as something we are both involved in, and work out a solution together. If you shoulder all the difficulty alone, I shall not accept it, even if you succeed.” Stretching out a hand all knuckles and calluses, the old man gripped Hu’s wrist. “My young friend, I have not been mistaken in choosing you as a friend. Let me explain.”

It was indeed just as he had said: an unfortunate coincidence, yet a fortunate one. The shipment of arms concerned one of his fellow initiates, a man named Yu Wucheng, whose territory lay around Yangzhou and Zhenjiang. Though the Taiping forces had withdrawn from Yangzhou, they had left Lai Hanying holding Chenzhou, where he was locked in a stalemate with the Qing naval forces under Lei Yixian, left vice-minister of punishments. Determined to break the deadlock, the Taipings had been dealing with foreign merchants in Shanghai in the hope of buying arms. “The foreign merchants are to blame for this,” the Old Master said. “They had originally promised the rifles Zhejiang has bought to the Longhairs. They had already taken their deposit and agreed on a shipping date, with a British warship to carry the cargo. Then the arrangement changed. I hear that the man negotiating for Zhejiang was extremely resourceful…” Hu interrupted with pleasure. “That was none other than the Seventh Lady’s future husband, Gu Yingchun.” “Oh! I had no idea. Old Five has been in Shanghai these past two months, and I have been cut off from the news. Never mind that for now. Let us return to my brother Yu Wucheng.” Yu knew Lai Hanying well. Appealing partly to their friendship and partly through a substantial gift, Lai had enlisted him to intercept the arms as soon as they left Shanghai and entered the inland waterways. Since this was the territory of the Songjiang Grain Transport Brotherhood, Yu had made a special journey to call on his senior fellow initiate and courteously notify him. “It was my own carelessness,” the Old Master said regretfully. “I have long stopped attending to these matters and left everything to Old Five. Unfortunately, he had gone to Hangzhou. And Wucheng is a brother who knelt with me before the same stick of incense! After fifty years, only three of our fellow initiates remain. I could not disregard that friendship. Who could have imagined the flood would wash into the Dragon King’s own temple? Now there is nothing for it. I shall have to break my word.”

“How can that be right?” Hu replied. “Elder Yu may be your fellow initiate, but you have given him your word. You cannot simply change your face and tell him that the goods belong to friends and must not be touched. A man of our world does not cut off another man’s livelihood. Let me find a way.” “There is no reason for you to rack your brains over it. I have told you what lies within our doors because I regard you as one of us. Under our rules, I ought not to have spoken at all. I ask only for your advice. The responsibility is mine, and I must bear it whatever happens. Tell Mr Qiu he need not worry.” “How can we stop worrying?” Hu said. “For now we must hold our position. Leave the rifles where they are until we have reached an understanding with Elder Yu.” The Old Master did not answer. Leaning back, he stared into space with his lips tightly closed, utterly absorbed in finding a way out. Hu watched uneasily, working through the problem himself. Had Yu not been the old man’s fellow initiate, matters would have been simpler. Had the weapons not been destined for the Taipings, that too would have made things easier. But now they dared not strike at the rat for fear of breaking the precious vessel, nor could they readily let go. Neither firmness nor persuasion offered a clear path. Even Hu could think of no immediate solution.

“It is difficult,” the Old Master said. “After turning it over and over, I can see no way but to stand firm myself.” “That will not solve it. How close do you think Elder Yu is to the people on the other side?” “I do not know. But among men of our world, a promise is a promise. He has promised them, and I have promised him. Whatever the circumstances, if I prevent him from laying hands on this cargo, our brotherhood is at an end.” “Not necessarily!” An idea flashed into Hu’s mind. “I have a way out where there seems to be none. Send a special messenger to invite Elder Yu here, and put everything openly before him. Both sides are friends. You cannot help one at the other’s expense. Ask him himself what ought to be done.” “What sort of solution is that?” The old man smiled. “It would simply mean that I cannot manage the affair and must withdraw from it.” “Exactly!” Hu nodded. “If you decline to intervene, he cannot blame you. And we, on our side, shall be endlessly grateful.” The old man considered. “Have you some other plan? Perhaps you mean to seek protection from government troops and settle things by force with Wucheng?” “How could I do that?” Hu laughed. “If I did, could I ever move among men of the brotherhood again?” “Then what would you do?” “I must speak to Elder Yu first. I shall tell him plainly that if the Taipings were not the intended buyers, I could give way, let his clients buy the cargo, and negotiate another purchase with the foreigners myself. But as things stand, I cannot. I shall ask him to stay out of it. As for the handsome gift they have given him, I shall match it.” “I hear it was ten thousand taels.” “Ten thousand is no great matter. I can afford to bear the cost. I doubt Elder Yu attaches that much importance to it either. What I must persuade him of is that he must never help the Longhairs. A man cannot fail to distinguish loyalty from rebellion.”

At these last words the Old Master looked at him intently. After a long pause he nodded. “Though you are an outsider, you know the origins and history of the Grain Transport Brotherhood very well. To tell you the truth, two hundred years have passed. These are no longer the times in which our three patriarchs, Weng, Qian, and Pan, founded the brotherhood. When the Longhairs first rose, we thought highly of the two Xius, Hong Xiuquan and Yang Xiuqing. Who could have foreseen how disgraceful they would become? Before they had even won the empire, they had swept away all the proper bonds of human relations. Worshipping Heaven and Earth while showing no respect for parents, men’s divisions and women’s divisions, and a whole tangle of other nonsense! Now Liu Lichuan of the Small Swords Society is worshipping Heaven and Earth too. I cannot stomach it. So if you can persuade Wucheng to change his mind and stop helping the Longhairs, there will be no breach of the loyalty we owe one another. But how do you mean to persuade him?” “That must depend on the circumstances. At present I do not know myself what I shall say.” The next step was clear: someone must be sent to find Yu Wucheng. The Old Master did not know where he was, but the brotherhood’s connections were extensive. Once word went out, every wharf, large and small, would hear within a day, and someone would find him. Yu was no obscure figure, which made the search easier still. Only the distance was uncertain. If he was near, he would come quickly; if far away, more slowly. No date could be fixed. “I know you are anxious,” the Old Master said, “but haste will not help. We can settle it in the end. We seldom have a chance to be together. Stay a couple of days.” “Of course. I have delayed this long already. Another two days will make no difference.”

Hu felt that way, and Qiu was still less concerned. That night they drank freely as usual, listening tirelessly until late to the Old Master’s tales of strange encounters from his years roaming north and south. Eventually the conversation returned to Yu Wucheng. “Songjiang is an impoverished branch of the brotherhood, while his is a prosperous one. Among our circle, Wucheng was the dandy. Women, gambling, fine food and clothes—he enjoyed them all and knew everything about them.” The old man sighed. “Who could have imagined that after a lifetime of pleasure, he would suffer in old age?” “The Longhairs are to blame,” Hu said. “Without their rebellion, he could have lived comfortably in Yangzhou and Zhenjiang. How would he have come to this? That is why I cannot understand his dealings with them.” “Be careful with such remarks when you meet him. He has one bad fault: he will never admit he is wrong. Leave him alone and there may be hope; tell him he is wrong and he goes still further down the wrong road. Only his mother can rebuke him, and then he dare not disobey. Anyone else, even the Lord of Heaven himself, could tell him he was wrong and he would refuse to accept it.” “Then he must be a devoted son,” Qiu observed. “Most admirable.” “That is why everyone respects him. He is sixty-seven this year, yet before his ninety-year-old mother he still behaves like a child seeking her affection. It is quite amusing.” “Oh!” Hu asked. “His mother is still alive?” “Indeed.” “In Zhenjiang or Yangzhou?” “Neither. Who could live in those places now? She has moved to Suzhou. Last year she passed through Songjiang on a pilgrimage to Hangzhou and stayed with me for a few days.”

“A lady of ninety, still traveling so far to offer incense? She must be remarkably strong.” “Very strong. In her day she was a formidable woman. Third Uncle Yu—Wucheng’s father—was murdered by an enemy. She took a fruit knife and went to the murderer’s door. As soon as she saw him, she stabbed him. Then she went straight to the magistrate’s office. The magistrate was a good man: he said she had avenged her husband and discharged her in court. She was four months pregnant at the time. The child was Wucheng.” “So Elder Yu was born after his father’s death. No wonder he is so devoted to her.” “He would not dare be otherwise. And it was thanks to this Third Aunt Yu of mine that he later took charge of the branch. When Third Uncle died, he had no son. The members agreed that his fifth brother should manage affairs in his place. If the unborn child proved a girl, there was nothing more to say. If a boy, then when he reached twenty, Yu the Fifth was to yield his throne and surrender his realm. But the man proved black-hearted and refused to step aside when the time came. Once again Third Aunt Yu intervened. She took the case to the Director-General of Grain Transport and won. Only then could Wucheng inherit his father’s position.” “Then this old lady understands affairs outside the home very well?” “Of course. Exceptionally well.” “Does she also manage their branch’s business?” “She used to, though not much in recent years. In the old days she not only managed the branch but intervened in disputes throughout our world. The name of Widow Yu the Third commanded respect everywhere.”

During this conversation Hu had worked out an excellent plan. To carry it out, however, he would need one person’s help, and must consult her before speaking to the Old Master. That person was the Seventh Lady. It was too late to disturb her when he returned to the You house. Rising early the next morning, he washed hurriedly and summoned the boy attending him, asking him to go inside and tell her that he urgently needed to discuss something important. Entirely unselfconscious, she invited both Hu and Qiu to her rooms. Qiu naturally hesitated to enter an unmarried lady’s quarters, especially with Furong there too. “It is quite all right,” Hu said. “We really are intimate family friends. Come and listen, and save me having to repeat it afterwards.” Qiu could only accompany him. A breakfast table had already been laid in the outer room. The people of Songjiang ate firm, cooked rice for breakfast, which he found novel. There was wine as well as rice, putting him entirely in his element. He sat down gladly and took a great mouthful. “Not too much, Qiu. We have work to do today.” “What work?” the Seventh Lady asked. “We have little else to offer him but wine. Little Uncle, do not spoil his pleasure.” “He will be pleased enough once he hears what I have to say. Seventh Sister, do you know someone called Widow Yu the Third?”

“Do you mean Uncle Yu’s mother?” “Yes.” “No one calls her Widow Yu the Third now. Everyone calls her Third Granny. I have met her. When she came to Songjiang last year, she wanted to take me as her sworn daughter. Then we worked out the generations and found they did not fit, so nothing more was said.” “Excellent! Then she likes you. Seventh Sister, you must come to Suzhou with me.” At this Qiu understood. Delighted, he raised a large cup of spirits. “Now I really must drink a full cup!” The Seventh Lady and Furong were still bewildered, so Hu briefly explained Yu Wucheng’s designs on the rifles and the Old Master’s predicament. Before he could go further, the Seventh Lady understood his purpose. “Little Uncle, you mean to bring out Third Granny and make her put pressure on Uncle Yu?” “That is the idea, but it must be done in a particular way. My chief purpose is to spare the Old Master embarrassment. This is my plan. Tell me whether you think it will work.” Hu proposed to prepare a handsome gift, then visit Third Granny Yu with Qiu, both in formal dress. They would explain that the Old Master had already given Yu his word and could not go back on it. With no other course open to them, they had come to ask her advice. What ought they to do? Would she tell them?

“Everyone has a human heart. With Little Uncle showing her such respect, and me putting in a word beside her, Third Granny is sure to intervene. If she speaks, Uncle Yu dare not disobey. Certainly I shall go with you. When do we leave?” “First I must discuss it with the Old Master. Please get ready. We shall leave at a moment’s notice.” “There is nothing I need to prepare. But what about Third Granny’s present? What have you in mind?” Hu had already considered that. He assigned the task to Qiu, asking him to reach Shanghai that day and tell Liu Bucai to buy two roots of old wild mountain ginseng from Jilin, together with three other gifts suitable for an elderly person to eat or use. Qiu would bring them to Suzhou. Once again, Jinchang Inn outside Chang Gate would serve as their meeting place and point of contact.

Qiu accompanied Hu to the Old Master’s house. When they began to take their leave, the old man was greatly surprised and did not understand. “I have thought of a way to spare you embarrassment with Elder Yu,” Hu explained. “Qiu and I shall be like Jiao Zan and Meng Liang, going to enlist the help of Old Lady Yu. But you must play your part in our double act.” The Old Master’s part was simple. Once Yu arrived, he was to tell him that Hu and Qiu had come with an urgent request, but that he had refused because he had already given Yu his word. He must also say that Third Granny Yu had sent someone looking for him, leaving instructions that he return to Suzhou at once to discuss a matter of great urgency. Having heard the plan, the old man bowed deeply. “My young friend, this scheme will be a tremendous help. You have preserved the friendship between two old, white-haired brothers. I am deeply grateful. But though Old Lady Yu can bring him to heel, we must consider his difficulties too. Leave that to me. You need not trouble yourselves.” “There is no reason to burden you with that either. To be frank, there is a commission on the foreign rifles. We shall use it to make a friend of Elder Yu. When I meet him in Suzhou, I shall speak to him myself. I am sure we can settle it. You need only pretend to know nothing.” “That I can do. When do you leave?” “We must make the pretence convincing. We leave tomorrow and shall not call on you again before then. It would be awkward if we happened to meet Elder Yu.” “In that case I shall not keep you. But once everything is settled in Suzhou, you must return to Songjiang and stay a couple of days.” “Certainly!”

They took their leave, and Qiu set out for Shanghai immediately. Hu reflected that an unforeseen turn of events was taking him to Suzhou yet again. Since he had the opportunity, he ought to untangle the affair with A’qiao. Fortunately, Furong was with him and could serve very well as a shield. Back at the You house, he asked, “Would you like to visit Suzhou?” “I cannot be bothered to move. Besides, you will be back in two or three days, and Fifth Sister-in-Law and I get on very well. I might as well stay where I am.” Their hostess also urged her to remain. Unable to explain in Mrs You’s presence, Hu could only give the Seventh Lady a meaningful glance. Its meaning was not easy to grasp, and she spoke with her usual directness. “Little Uncle, say whatever you want. What are you afraid of?” Helpless, he could only reply, “Seventh Sister, come here a moment. I have something to tell you.” Once he explained, she understood. If Furong went with him and A’qiao saw her, the latter could scarcely say anything. It was an excellent shield. She quietly persuaded Mrs You not to insist on keeping her guest. Furong too changed her mind when she heard the reason and agreed to accompany the Seventh Lady to Suzhou. They packed that night and boarded their boat early the next morning. There were four in the party: Hu, the two women, and a young man named A’tu. He had once been sent to Suzhou by You with birthday presents for Third Granny Yu, so they took him as their guide.

Suzhou proved a lively gathering place. At Jinchang Inn they found Zhou Yiming, who had been staying there all along, Qiu, who had arrived afterwards, and Liu Bucai, who had come along to join in the excitement. They occupied two courtyards, but everyone assembled in Hu’s rooms to receive his instructions. “Seventh Sister, you and A’tu will go first. Give Third Granny our regards and tell her that we wish to call. If she asks why we did not come with you, say we feared she might think us presumptuous and decline to see us. Then ask whether we may visit early tomorrow. If she agrees, send A’tu back to tell us.” “I understand. But she is certain to ask why you want to see her. What shall I say?” “Only that we have been unable to find Elder Yu, so we must come to her. If she asks what we want with him, say you do not know, but that we certainly mean no harm.” “Very well.” She set off with A’tu at once. “Zhou, go to Guanqian immediately and get me a full set of seventh-rank official dress, from head to foot.” “Oh dear!” Qiu exclaimed. “I have not brought my formal robes either.” “Easily settled. Get two sets.” As soon as Zhou left, Hu turned to Liu. “Third Master, now for your assignment. Take plenty of money, go into the pleasure quarters, choose the best establishment, and begin patronising it. You must make an impression as a wealthy customer.”

“There you go!” Furong complained at once. “No sooner have we arrived than you set Third Uncle to no good.” “Never mind whether it is good or bad,” Liu said. “Tell me what it is for, so I know what I am doing.” “We shall need to entertain a guest in a few days. I hear Yu Wucheng is an old hand at pleasure: women, gambling, fine food and clothes—he knows every one of them thoroughly. Once he arrives, I shall leave him in your care.” “Then I came at just the right time! Do not worry. I shall look after him when he comes. I guarantee he will be perfectly amenable.” Liu went off delighted. His forces deployed, Hu turned with a smile to Furong and Qiu. “There is nothing more to do today. Where shall we go for a look round?” “Never mind that,” Qiu said. “There will be time enough once the business is settled.” “Why, you never worry even if the sky falls. Why are you so anxious this time?” “Different times call for different responses. I have thought this through from beginning to end. It is not simply a matter of brotherhood affairs. The Longhairs are involved. Elder Yu may not be free to act as he wishes.” Hu sprang up in alarm. “You are right. We must guard against that.” He considered, then added, “There is no time to lose. We must write to Songjiang at once. Qiu, you must write the letter.”

This was plainly Qiu’s responsibility. He brought out the writing materials himself and asked what the letter should say. They asked the Old Master, once Yu reached Songjiang, to discover exactly what arrangement he had made with Lai Hanying and how the interception was originally to be carried out. Most important of all, was Yu himself under Lai’s control or coercion, unable to act freely? Just as they finished, A’tu returned to the inn, breathless from running. “The Seventh Lady sent me to tell you at once: Third Granny’s grandson is coming to call. He is a ‘Captain.’” The Green Standard forces had junior ranks called qianzong and bazong, and civilians accordingly addressed even ordinary soldiers as “Captain.” Hu attached little importance to the title. What pleased him was Third Granny’s courtesy: it plainly meant she was willing to receive them. “Good. I understand.” On reflection, A’tu was no longer needed in Suzhou and could carry the letter back. “A’tu, please return to Songjiang at once and deliver this to the Old Master. Tell him to reply immediately when he has an answer to the questions in it. I must trouble you to make another journey.” He then called Furong and had her give A’tu ten taels for his traveling expenses.

Just then an attendant brought in a military officer, followed by four mounted orderlies. His bearing suggested more than a mere “Captain.” Sharp-eyed Hu quickly told Qiu, “He has a crystal button on his cap.” That denoted fifth rank. “Ah, a garrison commander!” Qiu exclaimed. “This is awkward. Receiving him in ordinary dress seems discourteous.” It was too late to remedy the omission. Holding the visiting card high, the attendant announced in a drawn-out voice, “Master Yu calls!” More familiar with official ceremony, Qiu tugged at Hu, lifted the curtain, and walked out with measured steps to receive their visitor in the outer room. Master Yu and his orderlies were standing beyond the doorway. Qiu averted his gaze and took the card from the attendant. It read, “Your junior, Yu Shaowu, bows his head in greeting.” Clearly this was Yu Wucheng’s son. “We are unworthy of the honour! Please ask Master Yu to wait. Traveling away from home, we have brought no official dress and dare not receive him with such disrespect.” Before the attendant could answer, Yu stepped in. With a sweep of his hands he lowered his horseshoe-shaped cuffs and made the formal kneeling salutation. Military ranks might count for little, but they still felt unworthy of receiving such respect. Both Hu and Qiu were considerably embarrassed.

Fortunately, Yu appealed to old family ties rather than official rank. Rising, he addressed them as “my honoured uncles.” “My grandmother has specifically sent me to pay her respects. She feels she cannot presume to trouble you to visit her. If you have any instructions, please give them to me.” “Certainly, certainly!” Qiu replied with clasped hands. “My young friend, please sit and let us talk. My surname is Qiu. This is Brother Xueyan.” They exchanged fresh courtesies and sat down. Qiu made the customary official inquiries about Yu’s qualifications. He learned that Yu had passed the highest military examination, been appointed a garrison commander, and been assigned to the Governor-General of Liangjiang’s headquarters forces. The commander of those forces knew that he came from the Grain Transport Brotherhood. Impressed by his fine appearance and ready speech, he had specially recommended him to the governor-general, who wrote to the Board of War and secured his appointment as capital dispatch officer. Stationed in Beijing, he handled the submission of memorials from the Liangjiang governor-general’s office. He had recently taken leave to visit his family and would remain for another month or so. “So you earned your rank through the examinations! I have failed to show you proper respect.” Qiu raised a thumb. “Heroes arise among the young, and these are times for heroes. Your prospects are splendid. My congratulations!”

When the courtesies were over, Yu repeated the purpose of his visit and asked what they required. Now that they had reached the real business, Qiu gave Hu a glance, leaving him to answer. “There is something on which we need your father’s advice. Since his movements are uncertain, we have come to ask Third Granny. We cannot properly begin without first paying our respects. Please tell your grandmother that Qiu and I will certainly call early tomorrow. We beg her to allow us, her juniors, an opportunity to put our case.” “You do us too much honour.” Yu rose. “My grandmother says that even here in Suzhou she is living under another’s roof. She fears she cannot receive you adequately and would not trouble you to come. Please give me your instructions now.” “That is her consideration for us. As juniors, however, we must know how to honour age and virtue. Brother You of Songjiang and I are as close as brothers by birth. He does not regard me as a man outside the brotherhood’s doors. We are virtually one family. How could we fail to pay Third Granny our respects? Let that be settled: we shall call early tomorrow. Though we have a matter to put before her, we certainly shall not cause her anxiety or put her in a difficult position. Please be assured of that.” Hearing this, Yu could only reply, “Then we shall respectfully await you both tomorrow morning.” He made his salutation and took his leave. Hu and Qiu escorted him to the inn’s main entrance and gave gratuities to the four orderlies. Watching him ride away, they began talking at the doorway.

“I never imagined Yu Wucheng had such an excellent son,” Hu said admiringly. “With a mother like that to shelter him as well, I truly envy his good fortune.” “Never mind that.” Familiar with official conventions, Qiu reminded him, “When we visit Third Granny tomorrow, we must observe particularly solemn courtesies. In my view she must be a lady holding an imperial honorary title.” This reminded Hu of something. Ever since purchasing his rank, he had wanted to obtain honorary titles for his parents, a filial act that would bring distinction to the family. Qiu’s words therefore interested him greatly. “I have been meaning to ask you about that. How does one apply for such honours?” “Let us go inside and discuss it.” Once indoors, they put aside the Yu family’s affairs while Qiu explained. Under the Collected Statutes, civil and military officials of the third rank and above could obtain titles for three generations: their wives, parents, and grandparents. Those of the seventh through fourth ranks could obtain them for two generations, their wives and parents; those of the eighth and ninth ranks only for their wives. Officials outside the graded ranks could obtain none. A son wishing to honour his elders could surrender the title intended for his wife and request its transfer to an earlier generation. Thus officials of the third rank and above could seek titles for their great-grandparents, and those of the seventh through fourth for their grandparents. In the Yu family’s case, Shaowu must surely have obtained such a title for Third Granny.

“Such honours are distinctions conferred by the court. In the old days they were granted very carefully. Since the war began, they too have become indiscriminate. Like official rank, honorary titles may now be purchased.” “Oh?” Hu grew still more interested. “How?” “An untitled commoner cannot buy one. Once you have an official position, however, you may purchase additional rank.” “Good! What would it cost to obtain ‘Lady of the First Rank’?” Qiu laughed. “That cannot be bought. There are limits to the additional rank you may purchase. Shaowu, for instance, is of the fifth rank. He could buy his grandmother the third-rank title of Shuren, Lady of Virtue.” After a pause he added, “When we visit tomorrow, we shall have to wear official dress and kowtow. The ritual manuals do not specifically prescribe it, but by analogy with the courtesies a subordinate owes a superior, that is what we ought to do.” “Not only that,” Hu replied. “People in the brotherhood care greatly about face. I should like to honour the old lady still further. Suppose we borrowed a ceremonial escort and made a splendid procession to her house. Would that do?” “There is no reason why not, though it might be considered vulgar. If you do not mind people laughing behind your back, I can borrow one.” “Whose?” “The county magistrate’s, of course. I know Magistrate Sun of Wu County. He will certainly lend me his escort. But if we arrive with gongs beating and men clearing the way, and her house is too small or the lane too narrow, we may block everything and embarrass our hosts. That would defeat the purpose.” “True. Let us wait until Zhou returns.”

Zhou had not yet returned when the Seventh Lady came back from the Yu house. Third Granny had sent her specially to invite Furong. She told Hu that the old lady had at first been suspicious. She had thought her son might have become entangled with the Zhejiang authorities, who had sent two officers to investigate. Only after Shaowu returned and reported Hu’s assurance that they would cause her no anxiety or embarrassment did she understand that they meant no harm. “When I mentioned Aunt Furong,” the Seventh Lady said, “Third Granny told me, ‘Since these two gentlemen have come expressly about Wucheng, they are honoured guests of our family. We ought to act as proper hosts. But I am a woman and cannot conveniently receive them myself, while Shaowu belongs to the younger generation. We must do this instead: invite Mr Hu’s lady first. That will at least express our wish to entertain them.’ Little Uncle, I think she means it most sincerely. Let Aunt Furong go.” Hu readily agreed. “We must accept such kindness.” Turning to Furong, he said, “Go with Seventh Sister for a visit, and take our gifts along.” Furong hesitated. She was poor at social dealings, and hearing what a formidable figure the old lady had once been filled her with an unfamiliar dread. The Seventh Lady saw this and encouraged her. “Never mind. I shall take care of everything.” “Exactly!” Hu understood too. “With Seventh Sister protecting you, what is there to fear?” “Very well.” Furong finally nodded. “I simply shall not leave her side.” “Look at her!” The Seventh Lady laughed. “Our Aunt Furong is so honest and timid that one cannot help feeling for her. Enough talk. Change your clothes, and we shall go.”

While Furong was changing, Hu explained their plans for the next day to the Seventh Lady. Such displays of ceremony required someone to direct the courtesies, drawing the threads together so that two parties who had never met could respond to one another in perfect accord. With her quick and perceptive mind, she understood immediately. She readily promised that neither hosts nor guests would suffer an awkward moment over etiquette, and that everyone would be delighted. After Furong left, Yu sent orderlies with a banquet of bird’s-nest dishes. Halfway through the meal, a messenger arrived to say that Third Granny was keeping both women overnight. These signs of mutual goodwill suggested that once they met the old lady, every difficulty would be readily resolved. They needed only a prompt reply from A’tu confirming that Yu Wucheng was not under Lai Hanying’s coercion, and success would be almost assured. Rising early the next morning, Hu and Qiu washed and dressed, each putting on a brand-new robe and riding jacket. Zhou acted as their attendant, carrying the visiting-card box. One of Qiu’s servants followed the sedan chairs with a bundle of clothes and a strip of red felt. They went straight into the city to the Yu house in Tieping Lane. Having learned the outline of their intentions from the Seventh Lady, the household was ready. The main gates stood wide open, and Shaowu waited outside. When the chairs arrived, he ordered the bearers to carry them in as far as the eaves before the main hall. After they had exchanged bows and greetings, Hu said, “Please ask the old lady to come out, so that we may pay our respects.”

“We really are unworthy of such an honour.” Bowing with his hands lowered, Shaowu replied, “My grandmother asks you both to change into ordinary clothes and take tea in the rear hall.” “The proper courtesies cannot be omitted,” Qiu said. “On a first visit, we must make our formal obeisance in the main hall.” After repeated polite refusals, Shaowu said, “Obedience is the best way to show respect,” and withdrew behind the marble screen. Zhou and Qiu’s servant moved an armchair into the place of honour, spread the red felt before it, and waited quietly. Soon footsteps could be heard, and skirts and robes came into view. The Seventh Lady herself was supporting Third Granny as she walked shakily out. Hu and Qiu rose together and stood side by side below the place of honour. Hu looked closely at her face and could not conceal his surprise. Knowing her reputation for heroic deeds, he had imagined a tall, sturdy woman of the sort found in Shandong. Instead she was tiny. Nothing seemed to show beneath her pleated red skirt; he supposed her feet must be bound into three-inch golden lotuses. How could so frail a woman have commanded the fearful respect of countless bold men? One truly could not judge by appearances. Her face, however, revealed something extraordinary. It was wrinkled like orange peel, and her mouth had sunk inward as though all her teeth were gone. Yet her eyes remained quick and lively. Looking towards her guests, she turned to the Seventh Lady. “Which is your Little Uncle?” “The taller gentleman.” Hu stepped forward. “I am Hu Xueyan. I have come expressly to pay Third Granny my respects.” “Oh, you overwhelm me! Mr Hu, you must not speak like that.”

“Third Granny,” the Seventh Lady said, “Little Uncle belongs to the same generation as Uncle Yu. Please sit, so that he and Mr Qiu may make their bows.” “And Mr Qiu too! I am still less deserving of the honour.” After much mutual deference, Third Granny would only stand beside the chair while the two gentlemen kowtowed. Her grandson returned their bows by kowtowing in turn. “My honoured uncles, please change into ordinary dress and come through to the rear.” Supported once more by the Seventh Lady, Third Granny went in ahead of them. Hu and Qiu changed out of their robes and riding jackets, and Shaowu escorted them to the second hall. The reception was splendidly arranged: eight dishes of fresh and dried fruit and covered tea bowls on silver stands.
