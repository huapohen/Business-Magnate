HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

INSTALLMENT 056
A Swift Rise to Prominence, Volume III — Chapter Twenty-Eight, Part Three

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

Liu had guessed exactly right: this was precisely what Hu Xueyan wanted. Hu now understood how much real influence Yu Wucheng possessed, what abilities he had, and how he thought and behaved. He felt that a discussion with Yang Fengmao would be more useful than one with Yu. There was, of course, another indispensable participant: Zhou Yiming. The three sat close together in the waterside pavilion to work out a plan. Hu began by explaining his position. His purpose had changed. Getting that boatload of foreign rifles to Hangzhou was now a secondary concern; his chief aim was to help Yu Wucheng regain his standing and thus prove worthy of Third Granny’s regard. In the brotherhood, such a gesture counted for everything. Yang’s feelings toward Hu had changed again and again, from indifference to considerable admiration; now they had become profound gratitude. “Uncle Hu,” he said frankly, “my master may not yet understand what is in your heart, but I understand it completely. Give us your instructions, and whatever we can do, we shall do with all our strength. Since this is what you have in mind, may I ask how you think my master can recover his position?” “On two fronts: with the authorities and within the brotherhood,” Hu answered at once. “As far as the authorities are concerned, successfully arranging this surrender would naturally be best. Failing that, there must be action to put the men down by force. That, too, would be a great service.” Yang understood. His heart began to pound, but he was not yet ready to commit himself. He watched Hu intently, waiting for him to continue. “Within the brotherhood, we must make your master’s name ring out again!” “Exactly.” On this point Yang agreed wholeheartedly. “That is what I have always wanted. But we need an opportunity. If only he could accomplish one or two things that would really impress people!”

“The opportunity is right before us. But let us put that aside for a moment. An idea has just occurred to me; see what you think of it. There is a saying: ‘Repair the plank road in plain sight, but secretly cross at Chencang.’ Lame Changgen’s whole attention is fixed on your master and me. With both eyes on Tongli, he is neglecting everything else. I want to take advantage of that opening to get the arms shipment out of Shanghai at once. Fortunately, we have people in Songjiang to look after it. Nothing should go wrong.” “Yes, yes!” Yang nodded repeatedly. “That is a risk worth taking.” “But there is another part to it. I should like Shaowu to escort the shipment. Of course,” Hu added quickly, “if anything does go wrong, he will certainly not be held responsible. My idea is that once the arms reach Hangzhou, this service will allow us to put his name near the top when recommendations for rewards are submitted to the throne. It should do him some good, and it will give us something to show Third Granny.” “Excellent. If you have chosen him, Uncle Hu, what more is there to say? I shall go back to Suzhou and tell him myself.” “You need not go. I shall arrange it.”

They next discussed how to deal with Changgen. Hu and Yang agreed that everything depended on evidence. Once they had proof, any course would be open to them. They could call in government troops to surround and suppress his men with full justification; or, on a smaller scale, they could convene an incense-hall tribunal under their own brotherhood’s rules. Confronted with the evidence, he could have no defense and would have to submit to their punishment, even the sentence of “three knife thrusts and six holes.” “At present all we have is this report. There are no letters or written documents, and no one will come forward to testify. What can we do to him? Naturally, I could find a way to have him brought before a magistrate and interrogated under torture. The resentment that would earn me is a minor matter. What matters is the damage to your leader’s reputation: people would say he had used official power to harm another man. I am sure he would never accept that name.” “Certainly not, certainly not,” Yang said repeatedly. “Once a man acquires that reputation, he can scarcely hold his ground in the brotherhood.” “So unless we abandon the matter and keep clear of his trap, there is only one course left: ‘How can you catch the tiger’s cub without entering the tiger’s den?’” “I have thought of that too, but it seemed too dangerous.” “Provided the rescue is properly coordinated, there is no danger. Here is what I propose...”

Hu’s plan was to accompany Yu Wucheng to this Banquet at Hongmen, prepared for the negotiations to break down and for both of them to be kidnapped. When their captors’ boat reached a checkpoint, men would board unexpectedly on the pretext of an inspection and rescue them. Changgen’s treachery would then be established. Whether to settle the affair through the authorities or within the brotherhood could be decided afterward. Yang listened closely, working through every stage, and found Hu’s plan more thorough than his own. Their ideas for the first part were the same; the difference lay in the escape. Yang had intended to station men in ambush and stage a rescue like the opera Seizing Adou by the River. Hu would use official authority as cover, “besieging Zhao to save Yan.” One meant a rescue by force, the other by strategy; the latter was plainly superior. “Uncle Hu, I have complete confidence in your ability to meet whatever happens. My only fear is that my master’s violent temper may provoke Changgen into a rage. Apart from that, with the rescue properly arranged, we cannot fail.” “Success depends on who knows what,” Hu replied. “Changgen thinks he is hidden while we stand in the open. In fact, it is he who is exposed and we who are hidden. If word gets out, we shall be the ones exposed again.” “Yes,” Yang said solemnly. “I suggest that only you, Mr Zhou, and I know of this. Once the arrangements are complete, we can tell my master.” “Exactly.” They gave nothing away. After the meal, Liu Bucai again kept Yu company at the gaming table while the three resumed their discussion. When every detail had been settled, they went about their separate tasks.

The first task, naturally, was to inform Yu Wucheng. He objected to parts of the plan. Having his own son escort the rifles, for instance, might allow people to say afterward that he had been the first to betray Changgen. That would sound very bad. But he had always relied on others to accomplish things. Yang was his most capable disciple, while Hu had shown himself superior at every turn to anyone on Yu’s side. Their newly established family connection made it still harder to object. He therefore agreed with an expansive air. “I leave it all to you. Whatever you say, I shall do!” “There is one point on which I need your decision, Brother Yu. Once everything has been exposed, shall we settle it officially or privately? If it is to go through the authorities, I shall make the arrangements. If it is to be settled privately, that is a matter for your brotherhood, and I cannot interfere.” Yu considered. “Privately, I think. Bringing in the authorities would not be a good thing.” “Then we shall do as you wish. I shall be with you throughout, in any case, ready to follow your instructions whenever anything arises.”

The words “with you throughout” sank deep into Yu’s mind. They gave him the sense that here was a man who would share his troubles and good fortune alike, and his feelings toward Hu changed accordingly. He was generous by nature, with something of a spoiled young gentleman’s reckless habit of acting on impulse. “Fengmao,” he said at once, “tell your fellow disciples and all the younger men that from now on Uncle Hu’s word carries exactly the same weight as yours or mine.” “Yes,” Yang answered with willing sincerity. “We would not dare treat Uncle Hu with anything less than respect.” “You are too kind, far too kind!” Hu felt both pleased and abashed. “With such affection from you and your disciple, how can I ever repay you?” “My dear Hu, you have it the wrong way round...” “Master!” Yang interrupted. “This is not the time for that. Uncle Hu still has urgent business to attend to. We can talk over supper tonight.”

Hu knew that in the world of the brotherhood, the more seasoned a man was, the more readily he took offense or grew suspicious. Every point of etiquette had to be made clear. Zhou Yiming had appeared without warning, which already suggested that Hu was setting up a separate camp and acting on his own. To keep Qiu Fengyan hidden as well, another man working in the shadows, would be altogether improper. He therefore took the opportunity to explain. “There is something I must make clear to you, Brother Yu. Zhou and I have another friend, Qiu Fengyan, the officer appointed to escort that shipment of rifles. Both of them were worried about me and have now hurried to Tongli to help. More hands make the work easier. Since we are deploying our people, we ought by rights to be together; but too many of us in one place might attract attention. I should like your opinion: should we all lodge together or remain apart?” Yu had no means of deciding which arrangement was better, but he could understand what he was being told. Since Hu was worried about attracting attention, his own preference was obvious. “Apart would be better, apart would be better!” Yu replied without hesitation. “Mr Qiu belongs to the class of officials eligible for subprefectural and county magistracies. Like Third Master Liu, he is excellent company. It was through him that Third Granny came to take Mrs Hu as a sworn daughter.” “Oh!” said Yu. “Then I must show him some courtesy. I shall send an invitation tomorrow and ask him to dinner.” “That is unnecessary. Once the business is successfully concluded, we can celebrate properly. If you would like to meet him, I can bring him here tonight.” “Excellent! I only hope he will forgive the informality.”

With that settled, Hu went with Zhou to see Qiu. At the inn they found him holding a volume of Huang Zhongze’s Complete Works of Liangdang Studio, reciting poetry with a tipsy, misty gaze. At the sight of Hu he laughed. “My dear Hu, I truly admire you! Come, come, let me offer you a cup first.” “Wait, wait. I shall drink with you over supper later. For the moment, ‘the military situation is urgent.’ Put down your cup.” To deprive Qiu of his wine was certainly to spoil his pleasure. But his admiration for Hu was genuine, and he accepted whatever Hu said. He joined him at another table to discuss the business in detail. Hu explained the plan for the secret shipment and had Qiu write three letters on the spot. One was to the Songjiang Chief, explaining the circumstances and requesting protection along the waterways. Another, written in Qiu’s own name to Wang Youling, set out Yu Shaowu’s appointment to escort the rifles, providing a record on which a future recommendation for reward could be based. The third introduced Zhou Yiming to He Guiqing, saying that Zhou had confidential matters of importance to report in person.

When the letters were finished, Hu invited Qiu to the Zhu house for supper and a meeting with Yu Wucheng. Qiu, the Wine Muddlehead, suddenly became exceedingly cautious. Secrecy should come first, he argued, and there was no need for him to meet Yu. Hu insisted, however, that since the meeting had already been promised, a last-minute change might arouse Yu’s suspicions. Qiu therefore had to go. The two exchanged expressions of admiration; wine made friendship easy, and Yu found him very congenial. When they spoke of Shaowu’s escort assignment, Yu, though he disapproved of it, now felt obliged to commend his son earnestly to Qiu’s care. Supper lasted far into the night. Qiu and Zhou then took their leave together, returned to the inn for a short nap, and boarded boats hired in advance. One went north to Suzhou, to see He Guiqing and notify Yu Shaowu that he should join the others in Shanghai. The other went east, first to Songjiang to see the Old Master, then back to Shanghai to ship the rifles.

Arranging the rescue at the checkpoints would take some days, so they first employed the delaying tactic they had agreed upon. Yu told Changgen that Hu insisted on entertaining him first as a mark of respect. A visitor should always call on the local man, but the local man should first extend hospitality. Yu therefore offered a compromise: he would give a small dinner at which the two sides could meet and talk. Once the terms had been settled, they could accept Changgen’s invitation. This was perfectly reasonable. Changgen suspected no hidden purpose and felt that postponing his own plan for a few days would do no harm. He readily agreed. At noon on the day after Qiu’s departure, Yu held a grand banquet at the Zhu house. Changgen arrived limping, with no fewer than twenty followers. Whether this was a display of importance or a precaution because he felt unsafe, no one could tell. The hosts had to scramble to provide extra tables, while Hu hurriedly told Liu to obtain more gifts. He had prepared eight sets, each consisting of cloth for an outfit and a red packet containing two taels; now twelve more were needed. This was no mere display of wealth. He meant to win the men over. At some future moment when his life hung by a thread, even this slight bond of goodwill might make an enormous difference. After the customary exchange of courtesies over seating, Hu, as the visitor from afar, took the place of honor next to Changgen. The conversation consisted, naturally, of polished pleasantries. Afterward Liu took the bank at pai gow to entertain the “honored guests,” while Yu led Hu and Changgen to the waterside pavilion for serious business. Yang was the only other person present.

“Changgen,” Yu began, “my mother has been fond of our friend Hu’s concubine since she was a child, and has taken her as a sworn daughter. It is a case of ‘the flood washing into the Dragon King’s own temple’—members of one family failing to recognize one another. A remarkable coincidence, when you think of it. Hu is not an initiate, but he is a truer friend than many within our ranks. His friendship with the Songjiang Chief and You Laowu needs no explanation. And you must have heard of Yu Si of Huzhou; they are in business together. So I must ask you to show consideration over that matter.” “Master Yu, there is no need to put it so strongly.” Changgen appeared entirely sincere. “We meet people here and there, only to discover that they are our own. Out of respect for Third Granny and yourself alone, I could have nothing more to say. Besides, I very much want to make our friend Hu’s acquaintance.” “I am grateful, very grateful!” Hu clasped his hands. “Since you are being so accommodating, I must make some gesture in return...” “Nonsense!” Changgen waved this aside. “There is no need to discuss that matter again!” The question of the rifles had at last been addressed. They turned to the proposed surrender.

Changgen was an accomplished actor. He had no intention of surrendering, yet he haggled over every condition and argued back and forth as though he were perfectly serious. Above all, he insisted that once his men were incorporated into the government army, they must remain stationed in the triangle formed by Jiading, Kunshan, and Qingpu. Hu patiently argued with him until they reached an impasse. Then Yu could contain himself no longer. “Changgen,” he said in a low voice, “when you do anything, you should ‘think of yourself for the first half of the night, and of others for the second.’ Let me ask you this: once you have accepted the amnesty, if your superiors order you to attack the county town of Shanghai, will you go?” “Well... Master Yu, you know my position.” “Exactly!” Yu seized on his reply. “And it is because other people understand your position and do not want to put you in a difficulty that they intend to move you elsewhere. Otherwise, you may still feel a bond with the Small Swords Society, but they may not feel any obligation to you. Suppose they suddenly try to swallow you up. What will you do then? To be frank, you would not even be free to give way. Why? First, once you become government troops, the Small Swords will no longer regard you as friends; they may even try to wipe you out. Second, if you retreat, you shake the army’s morale. Military orders are immovable as a mountain; they make no allowance even for fathers and sons. Surely you have seen the opera Executing the Son at the Camp Gate?”

Unable to answer this, Changgen brooded for some time, then assumed an earnest expression. “Master Yu, my dear Hu, I really do have difficulties. The men’s pay is barely enough to keep them alive; it will not support their families. At home they have other ways of making a little money. If they are transferred and cannot look after their dependents, none of them will have any peace of mind. What you say, Master Yu, could not be clearer, and I shall leave the decision to the authorities. But ‘even the emperor does not send hungry soldiers into the field.’ Whatever happens, I must ask them to grant six months’ bonus pay to provide for the men’s families. If their homes are unsettled, their minds will be unsettled, and they will not fight with all their strength. Am I not right, my dear Hu?” “Yes, yes. You understand the situation perfectly.” Hu spoke with great sincerity. “I shall certainly press your case. Six months may be difficult, but I promise to obtain three. More would naturally be better.” “Enough, enough! We have got this far, Changgen. It would be unreasonable to keep arguing!” “Very well.” Changgen appeared a little reluctant, as though Yu were invoking his seniority and he dared not disobey. “I shall do as you say.” “Excellent! At last we have reached an agreement.” Hu turned to Yu. “Brother Yu, I should like to return to Suzhou tomorrow. Official business moves slowly; settling this may take five or six days. But we need exact figures for the men and horses, so the authorities can make their plans.” He wanted Changgen’s muster roll. Yu understood, but found the request difficult to frame. If Changgen made an excuse and refused, a man of Yu’s standing would suffer an intolerable loss of face.

Unexpectedly, Changgen was most accommodating. Before Yu could speak, he said, “Quite right, quite right!” Then he called, “Guisheng!” One of his followers, a remarkably powerful-looking man, strode proudly in to receive his orders. A pistol hung at his waist, decorated with a scarlet silk tassel. “Bring me my document wallet.” When it arrived, Changgen took out a sheet of paper and handed it to Hu. It listed 2,700 men and 350 horses, followed by the quantities of their weapons: long spears, broadswords, white-waxwood staves, and more than forty foreign rifles. Hu had never personally handled a negotiated surrender, but conversations with Wang Youling, Ji Heling, and Qiu had given him some understanding of how to distinguish appearance from reality in such affairs. Very few bandit leaders reported their strength honestly. They either inflated it or concealed part of it, and the direction of the discrepancy revealed their intentions. A man sincerely wishing to surrender wanted to be taken seriously, so he exaggerated his men and horses to increase his bargaining value. A man driven by desperation to surrender temporarily merely to survive would conceal some of his forces, preserving strength for a later change of sides. What Hu now wanted to discover was Changgen’s actual strength.

“You have dealt so frankly with me that my work as intermediary could not be easier. I am deeply grateful. There is just one thing I should like to ask: when I return to Suzhou, may I submit this list of yours to the authorities?” Submission meant that the list would become an official record. “Once a word enters a government office, nine oxen cannot pull it back”: when Changgen surrendered, his men and horses would have to be inspected and counted against it. Hu was testing his attitude. Anyone seriously contemplating surrender would consider this carefully; anyone who was not would attach no importance to it. The test produced its answer. “Submit it by all means!” Changgen replied. “When the time comes, they can count according to that list. I will give you a written guarantee: not a man short, not a horse missing.” The more emphatic he was, the less credible his words became. Misfortune could arrive without warning. Among 2,700 men, someone might suddenly fall ill and die. How could he guarantee that not one would be missing? Hu’s thinking ran too deep for either Changgen or Yu to suspect his purpose. They merely felt that the discussion had reached a suitable stopping point, and agreed that on the day Hu returned from Suzhou they would drink to their success in Miaozhen’s chambers.

After business, some entertainment was in order. The gaming in the main hall had expanded from one table to two: pai gow at one and the dice-shaking game at the other. There were also two mahjong tables in the side rooms. Yu accompanied Changgen over to take the bank, but Hu objected. The banker had won money, he said, and ought to continue so that the other players had a chance to recover their losses. Living in Hu’s company day and night had deepened Liu’s understanding of him. He caught the meaning at once and changed his objective: he would no longer try to win. Winning at gambling is difficult; losing is easy. But even when losing, Liu employed some skill. By unobtrusive observation he knew exactly who had lost heavily and who had won a little. Using the possibilities for arranging hands in the full version of pai gow, he eased up or held firm as appropriate, redistributing the gains and losses. Before long, seven or eight players out of ten had recovered their stakes and were showing a profit. Liu reckoned his own loss at three thousand taels, then rose with a smile and “abdicated his throne.” His loss left Changgen’s followers delighted; all declared him a gambler of the first order. Changgen took the bank next. Custom required a leader playing against his followers to let them win something, and he soon lost a thousand taels. Hu wanted to lose some money to him, but could not see how to manage it.

“Well?” Changgen asked, unaware of Hu’s intention. “Aren’t you going to try your hand?” “I am a novice at this,” Hu replied with a smile. “Let me watch a little longer.” Whether from a sudden burst of enthusiasm or some other motive, Changgen abruptly raised his voice. “My dear Hu, how about a wager between the two of us?” “Certainly!” Hu answered just as readily. “I shall be happy to oblige. You mean head to head?” In such a game there was no distinction between banker and ordinary player. Changgen knew how to make a handsome gesture in public. “Naturally! Neither of us will have an advantage. What shall we stake? You decide.” He meant how much money, but Hu deliberately answered a different question. “A sincere heart!” Everyone looked from Hu to Changgen. Changgen paused, blinking as though deeply puzzled, then broke into a broad smile. “Yes, yes! A sincere heart! My dear friend, I shall not lose to you.” He meant that his own heart was sincere too, though the claim might be true or false. It was a figurative declaration prompted by the occasion and could not be taken literally. Hu brought the conversation back. “I was joking. You, I, Brother Yu—which of us is anything but sincere with our friends? Why wager on that? Come, let us play for money!” He turned to Liu. “Third Master, lend me ten thousand taels.”

Liu counted out ten thousand taels in banknotes and handed them over. Hu laid them at the Heaven Gate position on the table. Changgen called for Guisheng to bring his document wallet again, then placed it in the middle, indicating that he would settle after the result was known. At a gaming table this was an arrogant gesture, implying a right to impose on someone of lesser standing. Yu found it offensive. “I shall have a wager too!” he said. A seasoned man needed no more than a hint. Changgen immediately reached for the wallet, saying with an apologetic smile, “If Master Yu is playing, I should not dare accept the wager. Later, you must take the bank for a few rounds and let us play against you.” It was an acknowledgment of Yu’s rebuke, and Yu let it pass with a smile. Changgen ventured no further liberties. He properly laid a stack of banknotes beside him, then asked, “The full game or the small one?” “The small one is quicker!” Changgen shuffled the ebony pai gow tiles, picked out two hands, and took up the dice. “Odd comes in, even goes out.” He threw a five. Since that meant the outer hand came in, he drew it toward himself and turned it over. His eyes widened in dismay: a dead ten!

Hu did not want to win those ten thousand taels. He was no expert gambler, but he understood gamblers’ feelings. Sometimes the money was a small matter; it was the humiliation they could not bear. Changgen’s present circumstances were especially easy to guess. He was so hard pressed that he was ready to take desperate risks. Ten thousand taels was no small sum. A soldier’s pay was between one and a half and two taels; even three months’ bonus pay for his 2,700 men, if they joined the government army, would come to less than ten thousand taels. To lose it all in a single turn of the hand—Hu felt uncomfortable merely imagining it. If Changgen could afford the loss, that would be one thing. But he plainly could not. Losing would only drive him toward more desperate schemes, as though he were being forced to “take to Liangshan” as an outlaw. These thoughts flashed through Hu’s mind, and his hands moved just as quickly. While everyone else was still staring and sighing over Changgen’s bad luck, he seized his own two tiles. Everything favored Hu. Changgen had already conceded defeat and pushed his ten thousand taels in notes across, looking decidedly embarrassed. The others all watched Hu’s tiles, and the impatient ones called, “Turn one over first!” With thumb above and middle finger below, Hu slowly felt a tile. Even the least sensitive fingers could have identified it: an Earth tile. He must not expose it, for the moment he did, his victory over Changgen would be certain. Ignoring the spectators’ encouragement and concern, he continued his performance. At the second tile he started, frowned, then shook his head. He pushed both tiles back into the unarranged heap and casually retrieved his own banknotes.

“What have you got?” Changgen asked, picking up one of Hu’s tiles and feeling it. “Ding-seven, a dead ten,” Hu answered languidly. “An amicable draw!” How could it be ding-seven? Changgen did not believe him. His middle finger carefully traced what was unmistakably a “two-six” tile; with that tile, the hand could not be a dead ten. He felt the other and understood. The total was indeed ten, but the combination was an Earth Gang. “An amicable result is a rare pleasure!” he said. “Harmony is best. We have had our wager. Good friends should play against each other once, never twice. Thank you for your hospitality, Master Yu!” “But it is still early. Won’t you stay a little longer?” “No more gambling,” Changgen replied. “We shall have plenty of time together. When our friend Hu returns from Suzhou, we can meet again.”

After he left, Yu asked Hu quietly, “What hand did you really have? I cannot believe you failed to beat a dead ten.” “An Earth Gang. His circumstances did not seem good, and I could not bring myself to take his money.” “You certainly know how to part with money! Throw a copper coin into water and you at least hear a splash. Your ten thousand taels have simply dried up without a sound!” His words suggested regret, but he was deliberately teasing. Hu could not immediately tell what he meant and could only smile. “My dear Hu, no wonder even my mother admires you!” Yu now explained his thinking. “You have extended your friendship. We shall see whether the cripple is a man or a beast. If he is a man, he will not do anything foul. If he is a beast, we shall treat him as a venomous snake and strike straight at its vital spot. His death would be no loss.” “Exactly my intention,” Hu replied. “If we have to act harshly now, he will have only himself to blame. Not only shall we have easy consciences; even if someone comes forward on his behalf, there are reasons to be heard on every side of the table, and we can put ours before everyone.” “Quite right! You know every nicety of brotherhood conduct. I cannot imagine where you learned it all.” Hu smiled. “Enough of that. I shall leave early tomorrow and should be back in three to five days. Everything here is in your hands, Brother Yu.”

On the fifth day, within the time he had set himself, Hu returned to Tongli with Zhou Yiming. They began deploying their people at once. Zhou and Yang were to hold the checkpoints at the two ends of the canal, Eldest Brother Zhu would provide support, and Liu would play the part of a convivial guest, accompanying Hu and Yu to their Banquet at Hongmen. Once everything was arranged, Changgen’s invitation arrived. The dinner was set for two days later. The reason for the delay, rather than entertaining Hu immediately on his return, was that Miaozhen’s cook needed two days to prepare a lavish feast of delicacies from land and water. Business, of course, still had to be discussed separately. On the day the invitation was delivered, Changgen came in person to ask for news. He brought a blue cloth bundle. What it contained was a mystery; since the guest said nothing, the hosts could hardly ask. After a few pleasantries, he inquired about the results of Hu’s journey. “Four months’ bonus pay...” Hu set out the three principal terms: four months’ bonus pay; the possibility of recommending Changgen for a fourth-rank military appointment; and a posting still to be determined, but certainly outside his present area. He watched Changgen closely, curious to see what evasions he would offer.

“If we are going to come over, we cannot keep complaining about the terms. With you there, my dear friend, our men will never be allowed to suffer. I shall do as you say.” Changgen untied the blue cloth himself. Inside was a stack of old registers, their covers bearing the words “One Heart, One Purpose.” “These are the muster rolls. I have only this one set. There was no time to have a fair copy made, so I must ask you to look at the originals.” Hu and Yu exchanged astonished glances, unable to understand his intention. The books were greasy and grimy, unpleasant even to hold. Inside, the entries were covered in alterations and corrections. Some were marked “deserted,” some “dead,” and others “transferred to such-and-such a unit.” These were genuine working registers.

“Excellent, excellent!” Hu could only treat this as evidence of real sincerity. “Let me borrow these originals for two days. I shall divide them among several people and have them copied quickly.” “There is no need to trouble yourself. Other people would not understand some of the changes. I shall send my own men to copy them. But,” Changgen added, turning to Eldest Brother Zhu, “I intend to send three men. They will have to impose on your hospitality for two days.” This seemed a further demonstration of good faith, treating the Zhu house as a place where he could transact confidential business. Before the host could answer, Yu agreed for him. “A small matter, a small matter! Send them over by all means.” “Thank you! Then it is settled. I have something else to attend to today, so I shall not keep you. Please come early in the afternoon the day after tomorrow. There are still many things on which I want your advice.”

Once Changgen had gone, Hu felt both deeply anxious and greatly excited. Drawing Yu aside, he asked quietly, “What do you think, Brother Yu? The fellow does not seem to be playing tricks.” “Yes, I cannot quite understand it either. He has even brought the original registers. It almost looks as though he means it! I think we should ask Zhou to investigate again.” “Quite right!” Zhou was accordingly sent to discover Changgen’s real intentions. If he genuinely wished to surrender, his attitude had changed completely. What could account for such a sudden reversal? They needed a convincing explanation before they could believe it. Before Zhou’s report arrived, however, news came from Suzhou. He Guiqing sent Hu a letter by special messenger. The Jiangsu Military Affairs Office had reported that armed bands of “bandits” were continually moving between Qingpu and Jiading, behaving furtively and claiming that they were gathering from various places to await an official inspection. He feared that they were using this pretext to stir up trouble and urged Hu to find out at once whether there really was such a gathering for surrender. If there was not, Qing troops would be sent to surround and suppress them. At the end of the letter he added a special injunction: “This matter is of great consequence. I earnestly request an immediate reply.”
