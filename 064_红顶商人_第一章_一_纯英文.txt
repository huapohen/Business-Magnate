HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 1 — Part 1 of 6

CHAPTER ONE

“Your Excellency,” said the bodyguard, saluting Zeng Guofan, who was enjoying his customary game of go after dinner, “a dispatch officer from Zhejiang requests an audience. Will Your Excellency receive him?” Zeng was in the middle of a ko fight. The survival of his entire southeastern quarter of the board depended on it; he could not afford to abandon the struggle. Yet in the end he put down his stones and rose. “Of course I must see him. Tell him to come in.”

The dispatch officer wore travelling clothes that were all but in rags. It was late in the ninth month, long past the time for changing to a winter hat, yet he still wore a summer one. Its bright blue finial showed that he was a military officer of the third rank. “You Tianyong, assistant brigade commander of the Zhejiang governor’s own troops, pays his respects to Your Excellency.” He hurried forward a couple of paces, knelt and kowtowed. A great hole in the back of his coat exposed a patch of yellow-brown skin. “Get up, get up!” His face looked as though it had never been washed, and Zeng could scarcely bear the sight. “Take Commander You somewhere to rest,” he told the bodyguard. “Give him a meal, then ask him back to speak with me.” “If I may report to Your Excellency,” You interrupted, “Governor Wang ordered me to reach Anqing within a fixed time and deliver these dispatches. I beg Your Excellency to read them first.” “Very well, give them to me. Get up and speak.” “Thank you, Your Excellency.”

You stood and retreated a couple of paces. Turning a little sideways, he opened his coat and drew out an oilpaper packet he had carried against his skin. It was thick and heavy, evidently containing more than a letter of a few sheets. The oilpaper had split, but the large envelope within, a foot or so in length, remained as clean and sound as new. The moment Zeng took it, he knew it held cloth or silk, not paper. He turned it over to read the address: “For the personal and exclusive attention of Governor-General Zeng at Headquarters, Anqing.” Below stood: “Respectfully sealed in his own hand by Wang Youling.” He broke it open. As he had expected, it contained a folded square of snow-white Hangzhou silk. With an idle flick of his hand he shook it out, then started. The writing was black tinged with red, and several purplish-red spots were plainly bloodstains. Wang Youling had written in blood just four characters, each as large as a great bowl: “We await your mighty reinforcements.” Beneath them ran a line in smaller characters: “Wang Youling, Governor of Zhejiang, respectfully leads the millions of officials and people of this province in a hundred prostrations, and begs you with tears.” Zeng had cultivated himself all his life towards the ideal of an unshaken mind. Now, however, his expression changed despite himself.

The civil advisers and military officers at headquarters were all visibly moved by this startling, extraordinary appeal. They watched Zeng, waiting to see what he would do. Slowly he rolled up the silk. “You have had a hard and arduous journey,” he told You. “Go and rest first.” “Thank you, Your Excellency,” You replied solemnly. “Nothing could comfort me more than to have reached you. All my hardships are forgotten now. I beg only that Your Excellency send troops at once.” “I have my own plans.” Since You would not leave to rest, Zeng began asking about Zhejiang. “When did you set out?” “I left Hangzhou on the twentieth of the ninth month. Yuhang had already fallen. I imagine Hangzhou is under siege by now.” “Hangzhou has very strong walls and moats. If I remember the Comprehensive Gazetteer of the Empire correctly, it has ten gates.” Zeng recited, “‘Awaiting the Tide’, one hears the ‘Clear Waves’ sound; ‘Surging Gold’ and ‘Qiantang’ make ‘Great Peace’ secure. In the reign of Emperor Renzong of the Song, the recluse Xu Zhonghui wished that his descendants might never leave Qiantang. He said it would always be spared the ravages of war. I should think Hangzhou could hold out.” You had heard the verse: it strung together the names of Hangzhou’s ten gates, such as Houchao, Awaiting the Tide, and Qingbo, Clear Waves. But this talk of some man from the Song dynasty meant nothing to him. He understood only that, by saying Hangzhou could hold out, Zeng seemed to have no intention of sending troops. Desperately anxious, he had to speak.

“Hangzhou’s walls are strong, certainly. But the city cannot hold out for long.” “Oh?” Zeng spread his fingers and combed them through his beard. “Why is that? Tell me.” “Hangzhou has insufficient stores of grain—” Prosperous though Hangzhou was said to be, its households did not keep large stocks of rice. The rice market of western Zhejiang was at Chang’an, a hundred li northeast of the city. Each year the landlords retained only enough of the grain collected as rent to feed their own families, sending the rest to Chang’an to await a favourable price. Thus the city had not even ten days’ provisions. That spring and summer, in the lean months before the harvest, rice prices had soared. Hangzhou had been captured and stripped bare in the second month of the previous year, and the inhabitants who remained were struggling to survive. Where could they find money to buy and store grain? They had meant to lay in proper reserves when the new crop came in, but no one had foreseen the army’s headlong collapse. The grain lying in abundance across the fields had all fallen to the Taiping forces. Zeng sighed deeply. “If only Zhang Yuliang and Li Dingtai in eastern Zhejiang had been willing to fight with all their strength and hold them off for a while!” Then he asked, “Ample provisions are the first requirement in defending a city. Has Governor Wang done nothing?”

“Governor Wang has been doing everything he can. Last year he issued a proclamation inviting merchants to buy supplies, promising exemption from likin duties wherever they passed. But the roads were unsafe, and the rice merchants dared not come. When I left, I heard that he intended to ask Intendant Hu to go to Shanghai to purchase food and arms. I do not know whether they have arrived.” “Which Intendant Hu?” Zeng asked. “Hu Yuanbo?” “No, Hu Xueyan.” “Ah, yes, him! I hear he is a very capable man.” “He is indeed. All the great local gentlemen in Hangzhou have either fled or gone into hiding. It is entirely through Intendant Hu’s efforts to borrow grain and raise contributions that the imperial troops have been kept going.” Zeng nodded and considered Hangzhou’s situation for a moment. “What about the south bank of the Qiantang? Zhejiang’s military funds now come from Ningbo and Shaoxing. That route is still open, I take it?” “Yes. We depend entirely on it. But—” “Go on. Why should you hesitate?”

“If I may report to Your Excellency, once one crosses the Qiantang, Xiaoshan, Shaoxing and Ningbo are all under Commissioner Wang. He and Governor Wang do not get on. Things—” You shook his head slightly, unable to continue. Commissioner Wang was Wang Lüqian, the imperially appointed commissioner for militia organisation. Zeng knew him well and had received a letter from him complaining that Shaoxing and Ningbo prefectures raised a hundred thousand taels a month for the provincial capital, yet Wang Youling had not sent a single soldier across the river. From what You was now saying, the truth seemed otherwise. But whoever was right or wrong, discord among commanders and hostility between soldiers and civilians could bode no good. The situation in Zhejiang was profoundly discouraging.

“Go and rest.” A man in Zeng’s position had no need to tell You what measures he proposed, much less explain his reasons. He merely gave his orders. “Get a good night’s sleep. Collect my reply tomorrow, then return to Hangzhou at once and report. I shall have someone prepare the dispatches, a horse and your travelling expenses.” “Yes, Your Excellency.” You rose and saluted. “Thank you, Your Excellency.”

The steamer plying between Shanghai and Anqing was the Siming, owned by the British firm of Swire. Her comprador, Xiao Jiaji, came from a wealthy Shanghai family and had been born with a taste for seeking out the strange and unexplored. Curiosity had first led him to study English under Gu Yingchun. Through his teacher’s wife, the Seventh Lady, he had come to know “Uncle” You Wu—the name he used in imitation of her child—and so acquired connections with the Grain-Boat Guild. Yet, like Hu Xueyan, he understood everything that went on inside its “threshold” while remaining an uninitiated outsider. Zeng Guofan had sent Li Hongzhang with troops to relieve Shanghai, and the Siming had consequently made several trips to Anqing in succession. By the time the business was over, it was late autumn before Xiao could find time to visit Gu.

Gu Yingchun was doing very well. His partnership with Hu in silk and tea had brought him a tidy fortune. When the worsening situation in Jiangsu and Zhejiang cut off supplies, he had turned to land. Wealthy families seeking refuge poured into the foreign settlements at Shanghai, driving land prices higher and higher and swelling his profits. Being on the spot also made it easy to choose a site, engage workmen and buy materials, so he had built an exquisite residence on the newly opened Second Road. His family of three—the Seventh Lady had borne him a son—employed more than ten servants. Teacher and pupil had always been close. Naturally, they began with easy conversation about Xiao’s travels. They had exchanged only a few remarks when they heard the Seventh Lady’s voice; then she appeared before them. Her broad, round face was heavily made up, the white exceptionally white and the red exceptionally red. Her dazzling jewellery made her almost too brilliant to look at. “Are you going out, Madam?” Xiao rose to greet her. “Yes. Two relatives have come from far away, and I am taking them to see something of Shanghai. We shall browse in the foreign shops and take a little air—”

“In this cold weather?” Gu interrupted, laughing. “You must be mad!” He delighted in picking holes in his wife’s expressions. Long accustomed to it, she ignored him and continued. “At noon I shall treat them to a foreign meal, and in the afternoon we shall see the Western circus. Nothing is settled for the evening. Shall we dine together?” “No need to arrange anything. Come home for dinner. The crabs are good just now; I shall get a basket.” “Yes!” she exclaimed with delight. “We have not had a proper feast of crabs this year.” Then she sighed. “What a calamity! With all this war and turmoil, even the supply of crabs has been cut off. There is no pleasure in being alive these days.” “There, there! You should be content,” Gu said. “You live in the foreign settlement, with no worries about food or clothing, yet you say life has no pleasure. What of the people trapped among the Longhairs?” “It is precisely because people are trapped among them, with no news coming through and no knowing whether they are alive or dead, that one cannot stop worrying. That is why I say there is no pleasure in life.” Her face clouded. “We cannot attend to everything,” Gu said consolingly. “Go out and look around; take your minds off it. Come back this evening for crabs.” She said no more, but went out with her head lowered.

Gu, too, grew despondent. “Things are very bad.” He shook his head. “I fear Hangzhou may be finished within the next few days.” “And Mr Hu?” Xiao asked. “I wonder how he is managing there.” “No letter has come.” Two tears suddenly rolled down Gu’s face. “Such a good friend, and we can only watch him being trapped inside! Who knows whether we shall ever see him again? These last two nights, whenever your teacher’s wife and I have spoken of him, neither of us has slept till morning.” “Heaven protects good men,” Xiao said to comfort him. “Nothing about Mr Hu—his features, his temperament or his conduct—suggests a man destined for disaster. Besides, with his insight and ingenuity, how could he sit helpless in a beleaguered city, unable to think of anything?” These words helped a great deal. Gu thought for some time, then nodded. “I cannot see the signs of a short life in him either, however I look.”

After a meal at Gu’s house, teacher and pupil went out in the same carriage. Gu dropped Xiao at the shipping company, then went to his property firm and sent its senior outside clerk to buy crabs. His instructions were explicit: they must be good, whatever the price. With that authority, the task was easy. The clerk was an able man. He waited at the inland-waterway wharf until a boat from Jiaxing arrived with a dozen or more baskets of crabs. Quick of eye and hand, he laid hold of a good basket and refused to release it before discussing the price. “Half a tael each, large or small. There are thirty-two in this basket. I shall be especially generous and ask only fifteen taels.” “Fifteen taels—and you call that generous?” “Take them or leave them. You ought to understand: crabs are not dear in Jiaxing, but we have risked our lives bringing them all the way to Shanghai. Surely that is worth half a tael each?” He reached to reclaim his goods. The clerk would not let go, but neither would he pay the asking price. He took out twelve taels in silver and pressed them into the owner’s hand. The man refused. After much coaxing and noisy bargaining, they settled at fourteen taels.

The Seventh Lady had just returned when the crabs reached the house. She took them out to look at them: golden-haired, purple-backed, extraordinarily large and strong. Set upon a round Fujian lacquer table as smooth as a mirror, they braced themselves on their eight legs and scuttled imperiously in every direction. The sight made the clerk swallow. Kind-hearted by nature and skilled at putting people at their ease, she said at once, “You have bought too many. Take a few back to the firm and share them with your colleagues.” She lifted a string from the basket—five with pointed abdominal flaps and five with round ones, ten in all—and handed it to him. He refused, but she insisted that everyone should have a taste, and he finally took them away. She then went into the kitchen herself to direct the cook in steaming the crabs with perilla leaves. Afterwards she opened a chest and brought out a set of silver implements: tiny pincers and hammers, exquisitely made.

They were eating with great animation when shifting shadows appeared outside, accompanied by voices and footsteps. The Seventh Lady feared neither heaven nor earth, but such a commotion terrified her. Her limbs suddenly weakened and her face went white. When she was six, her family’s house had caught fire; this was exactly how it had begun. Nearly thirty years had passed, yet the impression had never faded, and the old terror remained. “Do not do that!” she cried, at once angry and alarmed. “What are you all rushing about for?” Before she had finished, she saw the male servants helping someone inside. Her alarm grew, though she was somewhat reassured to see at a glance that Gu was unhurt. He hurried ahead to lift the door curtain himself, calling repeatedly, “Hold him steady! Hold him steady!” Then he found a moment to call into the room, plainly addressing her, “Get someone to bring a reclining cane chair, quickly!” Recovering a little, she asked, “Who is it?”

Xiao Jiaji appeared from somewhere. “Mr Hu!” he answered. “Which Mr Hu?” “Who else? Little Uncle!” At that her heart contracted. She hurried to the door just as the servants brought Hu there. In the lamplight she could scarcely recognise him. “Little Uncle?” “Seventh Sister!” His face was covered in beard, and he looked dreadfully worn. He managed a faint smile that exposed a startling row of white teeth. “It is I.” “It really is Little Uncle!” Tears streamed from her eyes. “How have you come to this?” “This is no time for questions!” Gu urged impatiently. “Where is that cane chair?”

She turned at once and told the maids to bring it and lay a padded quilt upon it. The male servants crowded around to help Hu recline, and only then did she notice the injury to his leg. “Fetch a doctor! Bring ginger broth!” Gu gave his orders in rapid succession. “Put some congee on!” Taken by surprise, the Seventh Lady had lost all presence of mind. Xiao was calmer. “Sir, let Mr Hu settle himself first.” By the time Hu was resting in the chair, a maid had brought a bowl of ginger broth with red dates. He drank it between gasps, his hands shaking and his legs twitching. At the sight, the Seventh Lady’s vision blurred with tears.

“He is utterly exhausted!” Gu told his wife. “He must not eat much at a time yet. Bring out that old wild ginseng.” Hu had not eaten a full meal for two months. He had escaped all the way in a little boat, but because he carried official dispatches he dared not show himself. Hiding by day and travelling by night, he had passed through one Longhair stronghold after another, with scarcely any chance to find food. Even when he found some, he could not eat his fill: his stomach and intestines were so weakened that a sudden full meal would have been impossible to digest. It was said that whenever the soup kitchens opened in winter, a few starving men died on the first day because they ate too greedily. The Seventh Lady understood this too. She hurried to fetch the old wild Jilin ginseng she had treasured for so long, originally obtained from the imperial palace, and gave it to Hu to chew and swallow, to sustain his failing strength. “Little Uncle,” she said, looking at his injured leg, “let me see the wound.”

She reached to lift his foot, but he hastily drew it away. Near Jiaxing, while the Longhairs were questioning him, a single unfortunate answer had earned him a sword cut. Without doctor or medicine, he had found some incense ash in a deserted country temple and roughly covered the wound, then bound it with strips torn from his underjacket. Now it was festering, filthy with blood and discharge, and he would not let her soil her hands. “Seventh Sister, do not touch it.” The few words left him breathless. After a pause he managed two more: “I’m hungry!” “I know, I know! The congee is cooking.” An idea occurred to her. “I shall get Little Uncle something to eat first.” She went into the kitchen herself, ladled out a bowl of ready-made chicken broth and skimmed off the fat. She tore off a piece of breast meat, chopped it to a paste and stirred it into the broth. Then she took a rice-flour milk cake, crushed it and soaked it in the soup until the whole became a bowl of gruel, which she carried to Hu herself. The smell alone made him swallow repeatedly. Once the bowl was in his hands, he longed to gulp it down at once. But he thought that an open display of his ravenous hunger would wound the couple’s feelings. Forcing himself to exercise restraint, he pretended to be composed and ate delicately, spoonful by spoonful.
