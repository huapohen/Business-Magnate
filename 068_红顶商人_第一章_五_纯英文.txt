HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 1 — Part 5 of 6

“In that case, we must first decide what we mean to do. As for an escort, if we can persuade the British and French admirals to send troops, we shall have a force strong enough to make the voyage safe, and it will cost us nothing. But there are two difficulties. First, they may still require an official request from the governor of Jiangsu. Second, it cannot be arranged in three or five days.” “A delay is out of the question!” Hu Xueyan answered at once. “Every day seems like a year to me. I wish I could leave tomorrow.” “Then the only quick solution is to hire some of Ward’s men. That will probably cost a considerable sum.” “How much?” “It depends on how many we hire. At least thirty taels a man, with a thousand in compensation for each man killed. For fifty men, that means fifteen hundred at the very least. And if—” If they were all killed, another fifty thousand would be payable in compensation. Only as the words reached his lips did Gu Yingchun realize how ominous they sounded. If that happened, Hu himself would surely not survive. He swallowed the rest of the sentence.

Hu took no offence. “Fifteen hundred it is, then. The officer in command will need an extra payment, of course. I do not mind. But”—he pointed to Xiao Jiaji—“his concern is sound. Men accustomed to fighting ashore may find, once they are aboard a boat, that all their strength and courage are of no use.” “We shall have to ask them. A generous reward may bring brave men forward, but money cannot buy back a life. Unless they feel reasonably certain, they will not agree rashly. We are outsiders; there is no need to imagine that we know better.” Gu’s last remark had something of a master’s admonition to his pupil about it, and Xiao, who had been preparing to offer an opinion, could hardly speak now.

“I know Yang Fang, as it happens. We are not intimate, but he has been kind enough to think well of me. I shall call on him tonight.” “Good! We shall divide the work. Let us settle it now: I shall see to the rice and the sea-going junks; you arrange for the foreign officers.” Brother You turned to Gu. “One other thing: you must get some ready funds together.” “That presents no difficulty.” Hu took a ring from his hand and gave it to Gu. “You know the firms with which I do business. Find out how much money I have with them and draw on it as you need.” The ring was of pure gold. If it did not weigh a whole tael, it weighed at least eight mace. It was enormous, and equally ostentatious. In fact, however, it was a seal. The four characters on its face, “Seal of Hu Xueyan,” cut in relief in seal script, could summon eighty or a hundred thousand taels almost at a word. Gu’s own resources were sufficient without it. “There is no need. You never take that ring off. Keep it yourself.” “No,” Hu replied. “I have another reason. If all goes well on this return to Hangzhou, well and good. But if we never meet again, I entrust everything to you. Tomorrow I shall give you a list of what others owe me and what I owe them.” This provision for his own death distressed everyone. Gu was even more unwilling to accept the seal ring. Taking Hu’s hand, he tried to force it back onto his finger. They were still struggling over it when the Seventh Lady returned and naturally asked what was going on. Knowing how deeply she felt things, and that the truth would grieve her, they exchanged a glance and put her off with some casual explanation.

“The food is ordered: seafood banquets at eight taels a table. We have booked forty tables,” the Seventh Lady announced. “The proprietor says he will lose money, but he wants to make his name through this engagement. Since he attaches so much importance to it, we shall look foolish if too few people come and the occasion falls flat. Brother Five, I am a little worried.” “What is there to worry about? We are inviting people to a gathering and a feast. Do you suppose we cannot find enough guests? I shall speak to Li Delong presently.” “And Chief Yu?” “You need worry even less about him. This plan of Little Uncle’s is just what he could wish for. Plenty of his people are sure to come.” Brother You added, “If you are still uneasy, I shall have Li Delong spread the word that we have taken over the Taihe Restaurant to entertain the whole Sea-Junk Guild. Anyone who stays away will be showing contempt for us.” “Good. I shall send word for another ten tables to be held in readiness.” With that, she went out again.

“Sister Seven is a delightful woman,” Hu said with a smile. “So fond of a lively gathering—she is surely born to good fortune.” “Never mind that. There is another matter. Yingchun, what do you think of this?” Brother You continued, “Little Uncle needs men to help him. To be frank, neither you nor I would be much use on the journey. I shall send Li Delong, and you can send Xiao Jiaji to accompany him all the way to Hangzhou.” “Well…” Gu looked hesitant. “No, no, there is no need,” Hu hastened to say, sensitive as ever to other people’s difficulties. Gu was genuinely in a quandary. His relationship with Xiao differed from those in the Grain-Boat Guild, where disciples were received in a formal initiation, a master’s command carried absolute weight, and men thought nothing of risking death. Xiao was merely his apprentice in foreign languages and business. In a matter of life and death, he could not compel him; he must ask the young man himself. Yet his friendship with Hu was so deep that, wherever he could help, he was bound to do all he could. He could not bring himself to refuse. Moreover, although Hu said there was no need, his face betrayed disappointment, which made Gu feel worse. After considering the matter, he could only speak frankly. “Little Uncle, if I had a brother of my own, I would certainly send him with you. Jiaji may be my apprentice, but he belongs to the Xiao family. Let me ask him.” Then it struck him that this too was ill judged: if Xiao proved afraid and refused, would it not make his apprentice seem unworthy? He added an excuse to cover a possible refusal. “His mother is ill in bed. If her condition permits, I am sure he will go.” He had scarcely finished when Xiao’s voice came from outside the door. Arriving just in time to hear, he volunteered at once. “I shall go! Of course I shall go!” This solved Gu’s difficulty and did him credit as well. Hu, however, still had to make a polite refusal. He knew that the sick mother was an excuse Gu had provided out of consideration for his apprentice, leaving him a way to retreat. But even men who understood one another still had to observe the proper forms; and this particular formality could not be left to Gu. Hu must say the words himself.

“Jiaji, I know you are loyal to your friends. But loyalty and filial duty come first. Since your mother is unwell, you ought to stay and look after her.” “It is nothing serious!” Xiao, quick to take the hint, answered promptly. “She has an old stomach complaint. She will be better in two or three days.” “Then it is settled.” Gu rose. “Since you are going, you had better be involved in the arrangements from the beginning. Come with me to see Mr Yang at Daji.” Daji was the name of Yang Fang’s firm, which dealt exclusively in goods for the foreign trade. When master and apprentice arrived, Yang was giving a large dinner for visiting merchants. They could quite properly have accepted his invitation to join the company and exchanged the usual courtesies, but Gu wished to make clear the urgency of his business. He declined politely but firmly, adding that he had an awkward favour to ask. Naturally, it would have to be discussed in private. “Certainly!” Yang readily agreed. “Come this way.” They sat close together in a corner of the reception room. Gu came straight to the point. Yang drew in his breath, looking as though the request presented considerable difficulty. “Brother Yang, forgive me for saying something more that I ought perhaps to leave unsaid. Eastern and western Zhejiang share their fortunes and their troubles. For the sake of the people of your own province, you must somehow find a way.” “Of course I shall try. Of course I shall.” Yang repeated the assurance several times. “The difficulty is that Ward has recently quarrelled with Intendant Wu. Foreigners can be very stubborn. Get on the right side of him, and anything is possible; cross him, and it is hard to make him listen. The only thing to do now is send someone to arrange a meeting with him tonight. Once I have finished attending to my guests, we can go together. Meanwhile, stay and have a little food and wine.”

At this, Gu could hardly decline again. He joined the dinner and exchanged courtesies while turning over in his mind the problem of persuading Ward. Suddenly Xiao said, “Master, I think I ought to go back for a while and return later. I must question Mr Hu carefully.” “About what?” “Foreigners are very thorough in their work, and this involves fighting. They are sure to want a precise account of the conditions along the route. Otherwise they will never agree.” “Exactly!” Yang said approvingly. “This young fellow has excellent judgement. Go quickly, then. Will two hours be enough for your questions?” “Quite enough.” “Good. I shall arrange to meet Ward at nine. Whatever happens, be back here by half past eight.”

Xiao returned before the appointed time. Besides obtaining answers to all the questions he had thought necessary, he brought a message from Hu. “Master, Mr Hu asked me to tell you: ‘A challenge will move a commander more readily than a request.’” This had the true flavour of a strategist’s advice: directing events from the command tent, he had sent out a secret prescription for victory at a distance. As the carriage jolted along, Gu pondered Hu’s brief maxim over and over. Ward was encamped near Jing’an Temple in western Shanghai. Yang was a familiar visitor. The sentry at the gate held up a lantern, took a look, and waved them through. The carriage drove straight to Ward’s office.

After the introductions, the four men sat around a small round table. Yang began by saying that Gu was one of the representatives of the Zhejiang authorities and had a favour to ask. Gu then took over, first clarifying his position. The authorities’ official representative, he explained, was Hu Xueyan, a highly successful merchant who held office under the Qing government; he himself had been appointed to act on Hu’s behalf. At this, Ward raised his first question. “Why has Mr Hu sent a representative?” “He has been wounded, quite seriously. He hopes to return within three to five days, and to do so he must obey the doctor’s orders and remain absolutely still. He is recovering at my house.” “I see.” Ward appeared to understand. “Please go on.” Gu explained his purpose. Along with his request, he offered a generous compliment: Ward, he was sure, would support this charitable undertaking on humanitarian grounds, and his brave men would certainly carry out the mission successfully.

Before Gu was halfway through, Ward was already shaking his head. As soon as he finished, Ward answered coldly, “I am sorry. You have my sympathy, but I can do nothing to help you.” “That is a great disappointment. May I ask why you cannot help?” “Certainly. First, Zhejiang lies outside the area in which I am supposed to deploy troops. And the mission is dangerous. I cannot be sure of success.” “Your first reason hardly seems valid. As I have already explained, this is a charitable mission—” “No!” Ward interrupted. “I have my responsibilities.” “Are they not to support the established authorities—to assist the Chinese government?” “Yes,” Ward said reluctantly. “But Shanghai must come first.” “Detaching fifty men would scarcely impair your strength.” “Whether it would or not is for me to judge.”

“Colonel,” Yang put in soothingly, “everyone has placed great hopes in you. You should not refuse so categorically.” “No!” Ward continued to shake his head. “The mission is too dangerous. It is a worthless risk.” “It is not dangerous.” Gu pointed to Xiao. “He can explain all the circumstances.” “No. I do not need his explanation.”

This flat refusal to hear them, with its unmistakable suggestion of contempt, angered Gu. Remembering Hu’s advice, he immediately saw what to do. With a cold laugh and a face like frost, he turned to Yang. “You cannot believe what people say. We are told that these foreign officers uphold justice, hasten to help those in distress, and are full of loyalty and courage. Who would have thought it was nothing of the kind? They are merely a pack of cowardly, money-grubbing mercenaries!” At the last words, Ward’s expression changed violently. He sprang up and glared down at Gu. “Whom are you calling cowardly, money-grubbing mercenaries?” “You ought to know.” “I certainly do!” Ward roared. “You must apologize. We are not mercenaries.” “Then you are regular troops?” “Of course.” “Regular troops must take orders from someone. Tell me, are you not subject to the Chinese authorities? Is it Xue or Wu? Just give me the name, and I shall find a way.” This touched Ward’s most vulnerable point. If he admitted that someone could command him, they could simply have that person issue an order. Would that not diminish his own standing? Gu continued, “To be fair, perhaps I went too far in accusing you of greed. But I do not admit that I was wrong to call you a coward.” “That is your greatest mistake. Do you realize what an insult it is to call a soldier a coward?”

Gu gave no ground, meeting him word for word. “If it is an insult, your own conduct has invited it.” “What!” Ward seized him by the shoulder and shook him hard. “Tell me! How have I shown cowardice?” Seeing violence threaten his master, Xiao immediately stepped between them. Yang also tried to intervene, but Ward was strong and furious, and would not let go. Gu remained perfectly composed. “Cowards are always brave enough in private quarrels,” he said coldly. At that, Ward released him. Turning to Yang, he declared, “I must maintain my authority. This man has insulted not merely an individual but the entire force. It is a serious matter. Unless he has a satisfactory explanation, he will be responsible for all the consequences.” Yang had no idea what Gu was trying to do and could not help reproaching him. “This is hardly the way to proceed. We came to ask a favour. Why provoke an open quarrel? It will be difficult to put matters right now.” He had spoken in Chinese, and Gu answered in the same language. “Do not worry. This is precisely the position I wanted to force him into. Of course I have a satisfactory explanation.” Yang knew nothing of the further purpose behind Hu’s clever prescription of challenging a commander rather than petitioning him. He could only urge Gu repeatedly, and with great earnestness, to keep calm and avoid a dispute.

“Set your mind at rest. Unless he refuses to listen to reason, I shall convince him.” Having said this in Chinese, Gu turned to Ward and continued in English. “Colonel, hundreds of thousands of people in Hangzhou are on the verge of starvation. They need food as you and I need air at this moment. With your help, we could risk this passage and carry grain into the city, saving hundreds of thousands of lives. Is that a ‘worthless risk’?” The question silenced Ward. He rolled a cigarette, lit it at the lamp, and sent his answer out through a thick cloud of smoke. “There is no possibility of success.” “Let us leave the possibility aside for a moment. Answer my question first. If the risk brought success, would it be worth taking?” Cornered, Ward could only admit, “If it succeeded, of course it would.”

“Excellent!” Gu seized on the admission. “I regard you as a man of worth, and naturally you would wish to do something worthwhile. You will recall that I told you the mission was not dangerous. Xiao could explain all the circumstances. Yet you refused even to consider it. At the mere mention of the Taiping forces, you lost your nerve—” “Who says so!” Ward protested. “You are slandering me.” “I should like you to demonstrate your courage by your actions—and show your worth.” “Very well!” Stung into agreement, Ward blurted out, “Let me hear the circumstances first.” He rose and went over to a map. Delighted by the change and filled with admiration, Yang hurriedly picked up the lamp from the table and motioned Xiao to explain. Gu joined them. In the lamplight, they all studied the map pinned to the wall, drawn on a sheet of thick foreign paper. It was far more complicated than a Chinese map, and the many little red and blue triangular flags made it harder still to understand. Xiao, however, often looked at navigation charts aboard steamships. After studying it briefly, he understood it.

“We shall encounter no enemy at sea. The possible danger begins here.” Xiao pointed to Biezimen. “In fact, there is only one stretch that presents much danger. The sea is wide, and the Taiping forces have no gunboats, so they cannot threaten our vessels. Only here, where hills on the north and south constrict the channel, is there a narrow passage. This is what produces the famous Zhejiang tidal bore. Once we have passed through it, the river widens again, and the danger is over.” “Then how wide is the river at that passage?”
