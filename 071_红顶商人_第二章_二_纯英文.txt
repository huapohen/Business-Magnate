HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 2 — Part 2 of 4

“You speak English, and you know the foreign firms in Shanghai. You are clever too. You will certainly make a convincing show,” Hu said. “Remember, the Longhairs are provincial fellows as well. Use the foreigners to overawe them.” Once his instructions were given, however, he wrote no letter. This too showed his care: if a letter were found in a search, the whole undertaking would fail and Xiao would lose his life. Yet Xiao needed some token to prove his credentials when he met Wang Youling. The gold ring on Hu’s hand would have been unmistakable, but he feared it would catch a Longhair’s eye and be stolen. After long consideration, he could think of nothing but an oral message. “Before I left, Governor Wang talked with me for a long time. He entrusted all his affairs after his death to me. His youngest son, whom he loves most dearly, is called Tiaoyun and is only five this year. He wanted to place the boy under my name. I said we should discuss it after I returned from Shanghai. No third person knows of this conversation. Tell him, and he will know that I sent you.” This was the best possible proof. Xiao asked exactly how the two characters of Tiaoyun’s name were written and committed them to memory. But he could not yet leave: first they had to find a small boat.

There were boats about. Ferries carrying refugees passed from time to time, but with the foreign soldiers patrolling the junks, loaded guns in hand, none dared approach. Here they needed Li Delong. Borrowing Kong’s telescope, he picked out an empty boat in the distance and swam towards it. He caught hold of its side and raised his head to face the boatman. Before saying a word, he drew a dripping horseshoe-shaped silver ingot from his clothing and handed it up. Truly, a handsome reward will always find a brave man: he hired the boat without difficulty. Dusk was falling and visibility was poor, making it easier to slip ashore. Hu pointed out the direction himself. Just as they were about to cast off, another thought struck him. “Wait, boatman! What is your surname?” The man pointed at the water. “Jiang—River.” “Old Jiang, thank you for your trouble,” Hu said. “Take my friend ashore, then come back and bring me word. I shall give you another ten taels. I will not deceive you. If I do, may I fall straight into the Qiantang and die a dreadful death.” Such a terrible oath seemed to move the boatman. “What is your honourable surname, sir?” he asked.

“Wang.” “Rest assured, Mr Wang. Once I have landed this young gentleman, I shall certainly come back and report.” “I am relying on you!” Hu bowed from the junk. “I shall have the silver ready and wait for you here. Even if it is the middle of the night, come back without fail. Have you a lantern on your boat?” “A lantern, yes.” Jiang was quick-witted too and understood his purpose. “But if I hang it outside at night, the wind off the river will blow it out at once.” “Quite right. Come, then! I shall scratch your back with six fingers—an extra favour for you.” Hu gave him a storm lantern burning Mobil kerosene. He was to hang it at the bow as a signal when returning with news, lest the foreign soldiers, unaware of his purpose, fire on him by mistake.

Once Xiao had gone, Li Delong could not help asking why Hu had treated the boatman in that manner, even swearing by the gods as though afraid of being disbelieved. Was he worried about Xiao? “Once I have let him go, there is no point worrying,” Hu replied. “It is the boatman I am guarding against. He might land our man and then go to the Longhairs to inform on him for a reward. I tied his feet with ten taels so that he would come straight back. Naturally I had to swear an oath, or he would not believe me.” “Mr Hu, I truly admire you. You leave nothing to chance. But why did you tell him your surname was Wang?” “That has another cause. The Qiantang ferrymen all hate me. My real name would have caused trouble. Let me tell you why. Twenty years ago—” Twenty years before, Hu had still been an apprentice in a bank. On one occasion he was sent to Xiaoshan County on the south bank of the Qiantang to collect a debt. He failed to collect it, and in a little eating house he lost almost all his scant travelling money at dice, keeping only the ten copper coins needed for the ferry. “When the boat reached midstream, they collected the fares,” he said. “The man came to me, and I put my hand in my pocket—but could bring nothing out.” “Why?” Li asked. “Misfortunes never come singly. A hole had worn in the pocket, and all ten coins had slipped out without my noticing. The Qiantang ferrymen were notoriously fierce. I had heard of people actually being pushed into the river because they could not pay. Naturally I was desperately embarrassed. I could only tell the truth and promise to fetch the money from my bank as soon as we landed. I might as well have talked my mouth off. They insisted on stripping off my clothes.” “The villains!” Li exclaimed indignantly. “But surely the whole boat did not just stand by and watch?”

Of course not. Someone lent him ten cash, sparing him the humiliation of being stripped. Yet the experience stung him so deeply that, once ashore, he swore that if he ever prospered and had the means, he would buy two boats, engage some boatmen and establish a charitable ferry to carry passengers between the banks without charging a single coin. “To tell the truth, I could have fulfilled that wish long before I did. Who would have thought one could be prevented from doing good? Delong, what do you suppose the reason was?” “Easy enough. When one man does good, another loses his livelihood.” “Exactly. The Qiantang ferrymen joined forces against me and would not let me establish the free crossing. Fortunately Governor Wang helped me in the end.” At the time Wang had been transferred to the post of prefect of Hangzhou. His personal friendship made helping Hu a duty; equally, as the local official, he was bound to benefit the people and encourage charitable acts. He issued a proclamation forbidding those who earned their living by ferrying passengers to obstruct the scheme, and also applied for an official reward for Hu. Countless people benefited from the free ferry. Even though Hu had not set out to fish for praise, his reputation spread everywhere, and he became known as “Hu the Benefactor”. But most Qiantang boatmen gnashed their teeth at the mention of that name. That was why he had concealed his true surname from Jiang. Now that his spirits had lifted, Hu had recovered his usual gift for telling a story. He made this little incident so entertaining that Li listened with delight—and with growing admiration.

“Mr Hu, there must be such a thing as moral recompense. After the good you have done on this river, you surely cannot come to harm upon it. We shall all share in your good fortune.” This was exactly what Hu wished to hear. His face lit up. “Thank you, thank you! May your gracious words come true.” Nor was he the only one encouraged. Everyone aboard took heart from Li’s remarks, believing that with a good man among them, calamity would turn to blessing. At once the oppressive atmosphere of the past two days—their sense of being trapped, their anxiety and despondency—lifted. Dinner was a cheerful affair. Even Li, who was no drinker, agreed to take a cup.

“One really cannot disbelieve in spirits and gods,” Kong observed comfortably, cup in hand. “But true goodness is doing good without letting anyone know. There is nothing remarkable in charity performed merely to gain a reputation for virtue.” “I disagree. If everyone would do good for the sake of a good name, this world would be a fine place. Some people are simply charity swindlers,” Hu said. “As the saying goes, ‘Since the Three Dynasties, the fear should be that men care too little for a good name.’” “Charity swindlers?” Li laughed. “That is a new name to me.” “It means cheats who use charity as a cover. They are the hardest sort of swindler to guard against, though in time people always see through them. In anything a man does, he cannot counterfeit his heart for ever. Some imagine themselves so clever that everyone will fall into their trap. In the end their true nature shows, and they ruin themselves. A man’s worth depends on whether he keeps his word. Take Governor Wang. To speak frankly, his reputation as an official in Zhejiang was not among the very best. But now he says he will not leave, and he stays. He means to share the fortunes of Hangzhou’s people, to live or die with them. In that alone he is worth far more than Governor-General He.” At this, everyone’s thoughts turned naturally to Xiao. When would he enter the city? What would Wang do on receiving the news? How should the boats respond? “He is certain to take action. But—” Hu suddenly held his cup without drinking. His face grew bleak. After a long silence he sighed. “I really cannot see how we are to get this rice ashore. Even if they fight a passage through, how can they keep the supply route open?”

“Mr Hu, I wonder whether this might work,” Li said. “Does Hangzhou not have water gates? Could we get a few little boats, divide the rice among them and smuggle it into the city?” “I fear not—” Hu had scarcely begun when a shot rang out. Immediately someone shouted, “Do not fire! Do not fire! The messenger is coming back!” Hu and Li hurried out of the cabin. Sure enough, a point of light was slowly approaching. As it drew nearer they could see a storm lantern hanging at the bow. When the boat came alongside, Li called, “Boatman Jiang!” “It is I!” Jiang answered, throwing up a rope. Li caught it, made the boat fast, helped him aboard and invited him into the cabin. Hu had already placed a gleaming silver ingot on the table. “The young gentleman has gone ashore,” Jiang said. “I have come to report.” “Thank you for your trouble.” Hu pushed the silver towards him. “A little something for your New Year’s entertainment.”

“Thank you, thank you.” Jiang took the silver, then hesitated before speaking. “Mr Wang, there is something I think I had better tell you. As soon as the young gentleman landed, the Longhairs took him.” Capture itself was nothing to fear; what mattered was how he had been taken. Hu asked calmly, “Were they very rough with him?” “No, not particularly. He is a brave young gentleman. He did not try to run when he saw them, so they treated him rather politely.” Half relieved already, Hu asked casually, “Is there any news from inside the city?” “I do not know.” Jiang shook his head, his face suddenly full of distress. “Inside and outside are two different worlds.” “Then what about outside?” “Outside? You mean the Longhairs, Mr Wang?” “Yes. What news is there on their side?” “I do not know much. A few days ago they were saying they would return to Suzhou. Some of them were selling their plunder from roadside stalls for next to nothing, as though anxious to turn it into cash. But these last two days I have heard no more about leaving.” Hu understood. The Longhairs too were finding their provisions hard to maintain. For the moment they were locked in a stalemate with the defenders. If the city could be supplied and hold out for another month or two, the enemy might retreat without a fight. Yet there was another side to it: since the Longhairs were short of food, the dozen or more grain-laden boats lying on the river must tempt them greatly. If they massed small craft and attacked regardless of their lives, the danger would be severe. That night Hu was once more too anxious to sleep. His every thought was a hope that Xiao would get into the city and Wang would assemble enough men to fight a passage through and hold the supply route. A single day would suffice: they could bring the junks alongside the bank, unload the grain and carry it into the city.

Xiao did indeed find his way inside. From the moment of his capture, the Longhairs had regarded him differently. Every civilian they seized ordinarily trembled with terror. Only this “new fellow”—their usual term for a newly captured civilian—was different. The others had their hands bound, and one man’s queue was tied to the next man’s to prevent them escaping. Xiao, however, was treated with considerable courtesy, just as Jiang had reported. They escorted him to the officer’s “residence”, where even the questioning was polite. “You look as though you come from elsewhere. What is your name, and what is your business?” asked a minor commander in a yellow robe and cap, speaking with a Hubei accent. “My name is Xiao. I have come from Shanghai,” Xiao answered calmly. “To tell you the truth, I hope to conclude a large piece of business. If it succeeds, Hangzhou will be unable to hold out any longer.” The breadth of his claim instantly impressed the commander, who changed his form of address. “Mr Xiao, what great business is this? Why should Hangzhou become unable to hold out once it succeeds?”

“I really cannot discuss it with you,” Xiao said. “Please send me to see the Loyal King.” “I do not know where His Highness is stationed, and I cannot gain an audience with him myself. I can only refer you to my superiors. But, Mr Xiao—” The man hesitated. “You will not get me into trouble, will you?” “How could I?” “If what you say is untrue, will not all the blame fall on me?” Xiao laughed. Seeing that the man was simple and easily deceived, he deliberately assumed a look of contempt. “What an amusing thing to say! Why should you suppose I am not telling the truth? I was living very comfortably in Shanghai. Why else would I travel all this way? Let me be frank: the British have commissioned me to come here. I must see the Loyal King and lay an important matter before him.” Suddenly he asked, “May I know your name?” “Lu Deyi.” “When I see the Loyal King, I shall put in a good word for you. You are certain to receive a handsome reward.” Li Xiucheng managed his army differently from the other Taiping commanders. He had always set great store by attracting able men. At Xiao’s words, Lu was more anxious than ever to show him proper attention. “Mr Xiao,” he answered sincerely, “thank you for your kindness. But it is late today. Stay the night while I send a report to my superiors. They will send someone to fetch you. Would that suit you?”

It would not do to press too hard. Xiao thought that a night’s stay would allow him to question this obliging man and learn more about the situation, giving him a firmer basis for action. He therefore replied with apparent satisfaction, “Very well. I shall stay the night.” Lu treated him as an honoured guest and entertained him with wine. Xiao knew the Yangtze ports well, and Lu came from Hanyang. Recent events in Hubei supplied a topic, and they soon fell into congenial conversation. Eventually they came to conditions inside Hangzhou. Lu showed himself a man of genuine kindness. His face fell. “Truly a calamity!”

He sighed. “The thought of it spoils one’s appetite. I only hope the city falls soon, so that its people may have a chance to live. If this siege goes on, I fear they will all perish.” “Yes,” Xiao said, seizing his opportunity. “Naturally, the great business I have come to arrange will help your side, but it will really benefit Hangzhou’s people too. Yet there is something I do not understand. When the Loyal King took Suzhou, his humanity and generosity won everyone’s gratitude. Why, then, does he not give the people here a way to survive?” “It is hard to dismount once one has mounted a tiger,” Lu replied. “I hear the Loyal King had an arrow shot into the city with a letter urging surrender. It was most earnestly expressed, but no answer came.” “Oh? What did the letter say?” “That soldiers and civilians, Manchus and Han alike, might surrender if they wished, and those who did not wish to surrender would be allowed to leave. The Loyal King has already sent a memorial to the Heavenly Capital, asking the Heavenly King to pardon the Manchu troops and let them return north. The journey there and back takes more than twenty days, and the imperial rescript has not yet arrived. Once it comes, someone will be sent to negotiate peace with Ruichang. Things may look very different then. I have been to many places,” Lu added, “but the Manchu soldiers in Hangzhou seem to be the fiercest of all.” This recalled Hu’s words to Xiao: with food in store, Hangzhou could hold out for six months or a year. He felt the weight of his responsibility more keenly than ever. Lying that night in Lu’s “residence”, he imagined every possible turn of events again and again. How could he gain Li Xiucheng’s trust and free himself from surveillance? Once he encountered the imperial troops, how could he convince them that he was no spy and have them take him into the city to see Wang? He tossed and turned until he heard the fourth watch being called, and only then drifted into an uneasy sleep. He had no idea how long he had slept before he woke with a start. Voices clamoured and footsteps hurried outside, as though something momentous had happened. All sleep vanished. He sat bolt upright and listened intently, catching a cry: “The demon wind is up! The demon wind is up!” He seemed to have heard the expression somewhere. Clenching his teeth, he searched his memory until at last he remembered: in an idle moment aboard the junk, Hu had told them that the Longhairs called the Qing troops “demons”. “The demon wind is up” meant that imperial troops were attacking.

Alarmed and delighted at once, Xiao hurried out of bed and dressed himself securely. Still he dared not act rashly. Opening the door a crack, he peered out. Longhairs were pouring outside with weapons of every kind—red-tasselled spears, ash-wood staffs, great chopping swords and foreign firearms. Gunfire had begun already, mingled with shouts that seemed now near, now far, now east, now west as the wind rose and fell. The attacking imperial force appeared considerable.
