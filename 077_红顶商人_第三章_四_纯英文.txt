HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 3 — Part 4 of 4

“Excellent!” he said. “The signs of refinement and distinction. Your blessings will increase as you grow older.” A’qiao glanced at Hu, smiled faintly and ignored his predictions. “There are only a few dishes. I fear we shall not entertain you properly, Dr Zhang.” With that she rose and left. He felt an inevitable sense of loss. Men and women did not dine together, especially when the man was a stranger. By appearing, she had fulfilled the courtesies of a hostess. Indeed, by ordinary standards he had already enjoyed more than he could expect; there would be no further opportunity to see her. “But does she not have another favour to ask?” The thought reassured him. Whatever the request might be, he resolved that there could be no discussion unless she made it in person.

As expected, only Xiao kept him company at dinner. The dishes were delicately prepared. When the hotpot was brought in at the end, A’qiao spoke a few polite words from behind the curtain, then vanished again. They talked for some time after the meal, until Dr Zhang could bear it no longer. “Did you not say that A’qiao wanted me to do something for her?” “Yes. Let me go and ask.” The doctor watched the screen, straining his ears. After a long while someone emerged, but it was only Xiao. Behind the screen, however, a slender shadow could be seen. “A’qiao says you will certainly refuse, so she had better not mention it.” “Why should she not?” he blurted out. “What makes her think I shall refuse?” “Then I shall tell you!” came the answer from behind the screen.

A’qiao appeared with the words. She had changed her clothes. Earlier she had worn a black satin fur jacket edged with grey squirrel and a pleated skirt of palest blue silk. Now she was in informal dress: a silk-padded jacket of bamboo-green ning silk. Too fond of looking pretty to wear bulky padded trousers, she would not bind the cuffs at the ankles as northern women did either. Instead, her loose-cuffed trousers were of soft black satin, trimmed with very broad ornamental bands. For warmth, the jacket had been cut to fit closely at the waist, making her figure all the more graceful and alluring. Dr Zhang was half-tipsy, yet at heart he remained a cautious, proper man. Her beauty was so dazzling that he dared not look closely. Turning his face slightly aside, he asked, “Whatever you wish, A’qiao, please tell me. Is it a particularly careful prescription for a tonic paste?” “That too, naturally,” she replied. “But there is no hurry. My younger sister entrusted me with this duty. She was uneasy about leaving our dear relation alone in Ningbo. I cannot look after him constantly, and even when I do, I cannot be as attentive and considerate as my sister. I have been thinking how fortunate it was that, in such a grave illness, he found you. Your skill has brought him back to health as surely as spring revives the earth. He must be out of danger now. But if he travels to Shanghai by boat without you to care for him, I really cannot feel easy. So there is nothing for it but—” She drew an embroidered handkerchief from beneath her arm and smiled with her lips pressed together, as though too embarrassed to finish. Dr Zhang was intoxicated by the clear, oriole-like notes of her voice. Absorbed in the sound, he had paid too little attention to its meaning. Her sudden silence puzzled him. “Why have you stopped? Please go on. I am listening carefully.”

Her meaning was already perfectly clear. If he had listened carefully and still failed to understand, his mind must have been elsewhere. Seeing how thoroughly he had lost his senses, Xiao gave A’qiao a glance: she need not wheel her horse and draw her bow any longer, but should come straight to the point. “Very well, I shall. But, Dr Zhang—” Her large eyes flashed with a lively, almost girlish innocence. “Once I have said it, you must not turn me down!” This was a serious demand. The doctor collected himself and went over her words in his mind. Only then did he understand, and he found agreement rather difficult. “There, you see?” A’qiao deliberately spoke softly to Xiao. “I said I had better not ask. Now I have only invited a refusal—” “Not at all,” the doctor broke in uneasily. “I understand what you mean. The question I am considering is not whether I ought to accompany him.” “Then what is it?” “Whether the patient can travel. A sea voyage in weather like this—if his illness returned, it would be no laughing matter.”

His objection was reasonable, but it was hard to know whether it was sincere or merely an excuse. A’qiao was formidable herself and deliberately pressed him, though indirectly. Looking at Xiao, she asked, “Did Dr Zhang not say that, with him to look after the patient on the journey, there would be nothing to worry about?” “Certainly. With Dr Zhang there, what need we fear?” Their exchange made him seem unwilling to help, which distressed him greatly. Still he dared not act rashly. Rising, he said, “Let me examine him again.” In the next room, Hu had heard every word. Partly because the prospect of having his wish fulfilled delighted him, and partly because he meant to conceal his remaining symptoms, the examination naturally showed a marked improvement. Only now could Dr Zhang consider his own circumstances. Amid war and disorder, with the year drawing to its close, it was hardly the time for a long journey. But he had spoken too generously to retreat or qualify his offer. At the same time, romantic fancies filled his mind. He could not bear to relinquish the chance of sharing a sea voyage with A’qiao. At last he made up his mind and agreed.

Hu was naturally grateful beyond measure. But it was A’qiao’s gratitude that Dr Zhang wanted. Hu understood the delicate distinction perfectly. He wrapped a hundred taels in red paper and asked her to present them with her own hands. “A physician should have a heart ready to sacrifice his own flesh,” the doctor said, waving both hands in refusal. “To speak of money would obscure the sincerity of my intentions.” Fine words—but A’qiao understood those “intentions” only too well, and was the more determined to give him the money because she could not and would not repay them in the manner he desired. Naturally, she kept that thought deep within herself. “I understand your kind intentions, Dr Zhang. This is a small token from my ‘brother-in-law’. He says that if you refuse it, his mind will be uneasy and his recovery will be slower. In that case, he would not dare ask you to undertake the journey.” The doctor savoured every word. “I understand your kind intentions” was branded on his heart as if with a hot iron, never to be forgotten. “In that case, I can only be shameless enough to accept. But—” He went no further. To show A’qiao that he had agreed entirely for her sake, he decided to repay Hu’s gift with one of his own: a couple of valuable restorative medicines. But there was no need to announce it beforehand. To do so would spoil the gesture, so he checked himself.

“Then may I ask, Dr Zhang,” Xiao interposed, “when should we prepare to leave?” “The sooner the better. I must return before the New Year.” “You will certainly be back in time.” Xiao considered the arrangements and made a proposal. “Let me have all tomorrow to prepare. Shall we leave the day after?” “The day after tomorrow must be a good day.” A’qiao knew few characters, but could manage an almanac. Confidently pointing to the entry for the first day of the twelfth month, she read, “‘Auspicious for setting out.’”
