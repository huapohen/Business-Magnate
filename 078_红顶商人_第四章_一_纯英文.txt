HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 4 — Part 1 of 2

CHAPTER FOUR

They spent a full day making the arrangements. Early on the third morning, everyone went aboard; after they had settled themselves, the gong sounded and the anchor was raised. Dr Zhang brought a bundle wrapped in blue cloth to Hu’s cabin. “Your gift was far too generous, Mr Hu. I feel ashamed to accept so much. I have brought two medicines for you to keep.” “Thank you, thank you! You are too kind.” Hu assumed that they were ordinary medicinal ingredients. Only when the bundle was opened and revealed two brocade-covered cases did he realize that they were precious tonics. In the long case lay a complete ginseng root, every fine rootlet intact, secured with red and green silk threads. A red label on gold paper bore eight characters: “Finest Wild Mountain Ginseng from Jilin.” “This root was an item of tribute. It came out of Minister Zhang’s household—genuine imperial-palace stock.” As he spoke, the doctor opened the square case. Inside were two sections of deer antler, each a little over a cun long and covered with fine white hairs. Nothing about their appearance told Hu whether they were good or bad.

“Everyone knows that velvet antler comes from a deer’s horns, but not every deer’s horn is velvet antler. Old antler is useless; inside it is all fibers and channels. What one wants is newly grown antler, filled with vital essence and blood, like this.” Dr Zhang went on, “There is an art to harvesting it too. If the man lacks skill, one stroke may take off the deer’s head—” He had seen the process himself. At the turn of spring into summer, when everything flourished, deer were driven into an empty enclosure and chased without respite. Timid creatures by nature, they fled with all their strength, driving their blood and vital energy upward into the newly growing antlers. A gate was then opened, just wide enough for one deer to escape at a time. Beyond it was a curved passage formed by rails. At one end a man waited with a sharp axe, concentrating intently. As the deer was about to emerge, he judged his moment and struck, severing precisely the newly grown section of antler. Only velvet antler gathered in this way was of the finest quality. Hu listened with great interest. “They say, ‘No one may see how medicines are prepared, but Heaven knows the maker’s heart.’ Yet good or bad quality will become known in time. One person tells ten, and ten tell a hundred; that is how a reputation spreads. Dr Zhang, I hear that you have a pharmacy too. It must be a large concern.” “I could hardly call it that. It is a family business that I merely keep going. My medical practice keeps me so busy that I have little time for it.”

At this, Hu could hardly pursue the subject. Liu Bucai had become so absorbed in gambling that he took little interest in Hu’s plan to open a pharmacy. Hu had thought of asking Dr Zhang’s advice, but a man who could scarcely tend his own affairs would hardly leave his own field to cultivate another’s. There was no point in discussing it. Still, the doctor was an honored guest specially invited to accompany them, and they must do their best to entertain him. What diversion could they find? Conversation with the hands would be better than mere talk: the most suitable amusement, and almost the only one available, was a game of mahjong. Ningbo mahjong was as famous as the Cantonese variety. It was said that the transformation of madiao cards into mahjong had resulted from Ningbo players’ continual experiments and improvements since the Ming dynasty. Dr Zhang was a devotee himself and readily agreed to Hu’s proposal.

Hu, of course, could not play. They needed one more person to complete the table, so the boat’s master would have to join them. Xiao Jiaji was an expert, but what of A’qiao? He had to ask. “Sister A’qiao, have you played with Ningbo people before?” “Of course.” “Have you ever played aboard a boat like this?” “This is only the second time I have traveled in one. But mahjong is mahjong. What difference does it make whether one plays afloat or ashore?” “For this kind of mahjong, you need a good memory—” “Naturally.” She thought the warning unnecessary. “If you cannot remember what the players before and after you are discarding or taking, how can you play at all?” Hearing this, Xiao could hardly say more. They unfolded a small square table and divided the counters. The boat’s master then lowered a cord fastened to the cabin roof and hung a bamboo basket on the hook at its end. It hung exactly over the center of the table, level with their heads and within easy reach. A’qiao had no idea what it was for.

She was competitive by nature and hated to admit ignorance of anything, so she would not ask. It must have some use; she would watch and see. They determined the dealer and took their seats. A’qiao sat next in turn after Dr Zhang, while the boat’s master, opposite her, dealt first. Having drawn his fourteen tiles, he inspected them from left to right, immediately laid them face down, and displayed a North Wind as his discard. Xiao, who preceded him in the order of play, called “Pung!” Dr Zhang explained to A’qiao, “This is where the Ningbo players calculate so finely. On his first discard the dealer puts out North instead of South. If the player before him claims it for a pung, the dealer gets another draw at once. It may be a South Wind, giving him the pair that would otherwise have gone to the next player. A draw like that makes all the difference.” His repeated use of the word for drawing tiles, with its other sense of touching or feeling, grated on A’qiao. She ignored him. Xiao displayed an East Wind; when no one wanted it, he raised his hand and tossed it into the hanging basket.

So that was the basket’s purpose! A’qiao began to feel alarmed. “The Ningbo way of playing is different,” she blurted out. “Yes—” Dr Zhang immediately explained. Heavy seas made the vessel pitch and roll, so Ningbo players prided themselves on remembering their tiles at a glance and playing with them face down. Space aboard was limited too; there was no room for a large square table. Sometimes four players merely sat in a circle with a board across their knees. Naturally, there was no spare space for discarded tiles, so these went into a basket. “But this table is not so very small,” he added, looking at the boat’s master and Xiao. “Why not play as we do ashore?” The master had no objection. Xiao, however, smiled—and that smile offended A’qiao. She thought he was looking down on her and resented it bitterly.

“Never mind, never mind,” she said. “Let us play by the rules.” In effect, she had refused the doctor’s kindness, but he took no offense. She, however, was discovering the truth of the saying about suffering any misery to save face. Trying to remember the other three players’ discards was a torment. Halfway through the hand, all three seemed ready to win, while her own tiles were still a muddle. The longer they played, the harder it became to remember which tiles had already appeared and which had not. “This is no way to go on,” she told herself. “I may never win a single hand. Losing money is one thing, but I cannot afford to lose face.” She decided to change her tactics and concentrate entirely on her own hand. Provided she did not become liable for another player’s limit hand after three exposed sets, she would risk discarding any tile, however unfamiliar. The doctor worried on her behalf, continually reminding her how many of a tile had appeared and which were exhausted. With this help, she settled comfortably into the game and devoted herself to building her own hands. In less than two rounds she won with a pure one-suit hand, a Three Dragons hand, and a mixed one-suit hand. Her luck was running high.

“Dr Zhang, the player after you is on a tremendous streak,” the boat’s master said. “You must keep a closer watch on her.” The more he urged caution, the more freely the doctor played. Not only did he refuse to withhold tiles she needed; he even broke up promising combinations to feed her, adding reminders such as, “Sister A’qiao, this is the fourth Three of Characters. If you do not take it now, there will be no other.” Xiao was playing sharply too, withholding the tiles the boat’s master wanted and scarcely allowing him a single claim. It was almost three against one. The master lost heavily, but dared not offend his customers. After four rounds he pleaded a stomachache and broke up the game. A’qiao alone had won a great deal, yet she had not found the game particularly enjoyable. Embarrassed to take the boatman’s money, she pushed away the counters. “Let it go! Let it go!” Then she stood up and left.

Her generosity naturally earned the boat’s master’s gratitude and respect, and he attended to them with exceptional care. The voyage to Shanghai went smoothly. Hu took his medicines regularly and developed no new symptoms, giving Dr Zhang little trouble. Nevertheless, he continued to show the doctor great consideration. As soon as they reached the Shanghai wharf, he sent Xiao ahead to announce the distinguished visitor and ask Gu to prepare a welcome. Gu was out, but fortunately the Seventh Lady could decide everything herself. She had already heard the earlier part of the Ningbo story from Li Delong. Though anxious about Hu’s illness, she had been somewhat reassured to know that A’qiao was caring for him. She had expected him to remain too ill for a long journey until after the New Year. His unexpectedly early return therefore filled her with both surprise and delight.

She hurriedly made the arrangements, hired three enclosed winter sedan chairs, and rushed to the wharf with male and female servants. Going aboard, she saw A’qiao first and then Hu. His frightening thinness saddened her, and she shed a couple of tears. “You must not laugh at me, Dr Zhang,” she said, rather embarrassed herself. “Little Uncle has suffered one misfortune after another lately. The story of his troubles would fill a basin with tears. But at least Heaven has sent him good people to help him, turning danger to safety and misfortune to good. That is how he came to meet someone as skillful and kind-hearted as you.” The doctor had heard of this lady, whose frank, outspoken manner commanded both respect and a measure of fear. If he hoped to win A’qiao, her help would be indispensable, so he was especially courteous. “You are too kind, too kind! You yourself are the most warm-hearted person under Heaven.” The Seventh Lady loved nothing better than to be praised for her kindness. She found that he had none of a famous physician’s airs: he was pleasant and agreeable in conversation. She took to him at once. Originally she had meant to put him up at an inn; now she changed her mind. “Dr Zhang, since you have come all this way, stay and enjoy yourself for a few days. You must be our guest at home—though I fear our little house will not do you justice.” A’qiao tugged at her, plainly disapproving. But the invitation had been given and could not be withdrawn. She could only see how the doctor answered and then decide what to do.

“You are too kind! I must get home before the year is out. I fear staying with you would put you to a great deal of inconvenience.” He meant this as a polite demurral, but the Seventh Lady took advantage of it to leave the question open. “Let us get you home first and discuss it there. We should go ashore now.” Hu naturally had to be settled first. A chair was carried aboard, and he was wrapped in a quilt like a swaddled infant, lifted inside, and screened securely behind its curtains. Getting him ashore required particular care. The boat’s master directed the operation himself; the whole crew helped, laying four gangplanks before carrying the chair to the wharf. The second chair was for Dr Zhang. The remaining one should have been A’qiao’s, but she insisted on squeezing into it with the Seventh Lady: she had something on her mind that could not wait. The Seventh Lady had barely heard the opening when she began to laugh. A’qiao was annoyed. “I am speaking seriously, and you laugh at me!” “I am not laughing at you. I am laughing at that doctor—a toad dreaming of swan’s flesh. Never mind. Tell me, and I shall find a way out for you.” “That is more like it!” Her annoyance vanished. She explained everything in detail, finishing just as the chair reached the house.

The first task at home was to settle Hu; the second was to entertain their guest. For that the master of the house must return. Moreover, the Seventh Lady had devised a way of rescuing A’qiao from her predicament, and Gu would have to carry it out. Xiao was eager to go home and see his mother, so she asked him to find his teacher first and explain the situation. He tried two places without success. Finally, at the firm, he learned that Gu had gone to the Zhejiang Maritime Grain Transport Bureau. Zhejiang’s tribute-grain shipments had long been suspended, and the bureau now served merely as the province’s dispatch station in Shanghai, forwarding official communications. Gu had presumably gone there for news of Hangzhou. Xiao was leaving a message and preparing to go when his teacher returned. His face was so gloomy that, if he had been seeking news, it was plainly bad.

The sight of his pupil, however, brought him pleasure. Like his wife, he had expected Xiao to remain away until after the New Year. His first question was therefore, “Where is our patient?” “He has come back too—with the doctor. It was very kind of the man to make the journey so near the year’s end. Madam asks you to come home at once. She wants you to show him a good time for a couple of days.” “That is easily done. But how is Little Uncle?” “Out of danger. A few days’ rest and care should get him on his feet.” Gu sighed deeply. “I fear that once he gets up, he will fall ill again.” Xiao understood at once. “Has Hangzhou fallen?” “On the twenty-eighth of last month.” Gu’s voice seemed drained of strength. “I have just heard at the bureau.” “And Governor Wang?” “They say he died for his duty. We do not yet know the details. Perhaps he escaped; one cannot be sure.” “No, he would not have.” Xiao remembered their recent meeting: the first time he had seen Wang had become their final parting. Hot tears streamed down his cheeks.

“Ah!” Gu stamped his foot and sighed. “If even you feel like this, what will it do to him? I truly do not know how to tell him.” “You must not tell him now. His illness would return at once. You must not merely keep silent, but find some way of preventing him from hearing it.” “I know. Go home and see your family. There is no need to come back tonight. We shall meet tomorrow morning.” Teacher and pupil took the same carriage. Gu dropped Xiao at home before returning to his own house. Naturally, he first greeted Hu with affectionate inquiries, then sat down for a cordial conversation with Dr Zhang. By then it was almost dinnertime.

The Seventh Lady found an opportunity to draw her husband aside and discuss the evening’s entertainment. At home, even with Li Delong, there would be only three men at dinner, making a rather dull party. Better to take the doctor to a restaurant. “Best of all, give him a dinner with courtesans,” she said. “Of course we shall do that. But how do you know he likes such dinners?” “It should not stop at dinner. Better still, find him a good woman whom he can take home. There is a reason for it; I shall explain later.” “I do not know what you are plotting, but I shall do as you say.” Gu continued, “There is something important I must tell you. Be extremely careful not to let Little Uncle hear it, or the consequences will be dreadful—” “You are driving me frantic! What is it? Tell me!” Yet no one knew her better than her husband. If he blurted out the news of Hangzhou, she would make such an outcry that everyone would hear. He warned her once more. “Do not cry out. Hangzhou has fallen. We do not know whether Governor Wang is alive, but almost certainly he died for his duty.” She did not cry out. For a long time she could say nothing. Then, like Xiao, she began to weep. Closing her eyes, she said, “How bitterly I regret it!”

“Regret it?” Gu was bewildered. “Regret what?” “We are his adopted kin, after all. It may have been presumption on our part, and we dared not take it too seriously, but the relationship existed. We watched him trapped in Hangzhou, waiting for death, and did nothing for him. Perhaps even beneath the earth he is reproaching us.” “This was fate. Even a man as capable as Little Uncle could do nothing. What could you and I have done? We can only find out exactly what happened. If he really died for his duty, we shall arrange a Water-and-Land service to deliver his spirit.” She said nothing, but knitted her brows in deep thought. Gu always watched her particularly closely at such moments: this was how she looked when faced with a difficult matter on which she meant to reach a decision. “Take our guest out first. Come back early if you can. And make further inquiries about Governor Wang.” He assented to each instruction, then asked, “Where will Dr Zhang stay?” “Here in our house,” she answered without hesitation. “We shall have considerable need of his help over the next few days.”

Gu did not know what she had in mind, but he understood that he must court the physician’s favor with particular care. He therefore arranged a full dinner at the best Western restaurant and summoned two courtesans to join them. The doctor was much taken with a popular woman, the Fourth Miss of Yanchun House. Gu accordingly arranged a two-table banquet at her establishment, sent invitations flying out to his friends, and installed Dr Zhang in the seat of honor. The guests were all veterans of the pleasure quarters; each summoned at least two courtesans. Surrounded by jewels and beauty, the party became extraordinarily lively. The doctor, new to the foreign settlement, was quite bewildered—and thoroughly enjoying himself. Naturally, their table talk included current events. Gu was anxious for news of Hangzhou and questioned the speakers closely.

According to their accounts, the army’s resolve inside Hangzhou had begun to collapse after the twentieth of the eleventh month. The chief reason was the exhaustion of food. On the twenty-fourth, a quantity of wood-ear fungus had been found in a dried-seafood merchant’s shop, allowing each man one liang by weight. On the twenty-fifth, some salted olives, known locally as “salted green fruit,” had been discovered, yielding half a liang apiece. Troops from other provinces then began searching households, rich and poor, for food. The governor’s own central troops were all Zhejiang men who had spent many years in Hangzhou and knew many people there. Retaining a little shame, they pulled their hat brims low and demanded provisions in assumed Fujian or Henan accents. Nor was looting food their only breach of military discipline. Order collapsed. Wang Youling personally led a small detachment of his own guards, seized more than a dozen offenders, and had them executed in the street. It did nothing, however, to restore either discipline or morale.
