HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 5 — Part 1 of 3

CHAPTER FIVE

People in the foreign settlement, particularly those who spent their time in pleasure and revelry, were accustomed to late nights and late mornings. With business to attend to, Gu Yingchun rose comparatively early, but even he did not get out of bed until nine. This morning, at eight, a maid knocked at the bedroom door. Someone had come from the firm with a message. “What message?” Gu asked through the window. “A Third Master Liu has arrived from Hangzhou. He is at the firm.” “Which Third Master Liu?” Still sleepy, Gu could not think who it was. The Seventh Lady, in the inner room, remembered at once. Lifting the bed curtains, she said, “Is it not Liu Bucai—Third Master Liu?” “Him? Surely not. He is one of our own people. He would come straight here. Why go to the firm?” “Madam is right,” the clerk answered from outside the window. “We asked him to come to the house, but he said his clothes were too ragged. He was embarrassed to call.” So it really was Liu Bucai. The unexpected news left Gu bewildered and momentarily speechless. The Seventh Lady thought faster. They still knew nothing of the Hu family’s circumstances; some misfortune might have occurred. If Hu learned that Liu was here, he would insist on seeing him immediately. Face to face, nothing could be concealed from him, and that might be disastrous. She therefore answered for her husband, telling the clerk to return to the firm and say that Gu would be there at once. She also warned the servants not to let Hu know that Liu had arrived in Shanghai.

“To think that he has come,” Gu said. “Will you go with me?” “Of course!” Husband and wife hurried to the firm together in a carriage. When they met Liu, all three were silent for a moment. Gu and his wife studied him from head to foot. His clothes were ragged, but his spirits and complexion were quite good. He did not look like a man near starvation. “Third Uncle Liu!” The Seventh Lady was the first to speak. “Are you well?” “Well enough, well enough!” Liu seemed suddenly to awaken. Blinking, he said, “Alive for a second time, and together with you again—of course I am well.” Husband and wife both breathed more easily. “And the Hu family?” she asked. “Are they all well?” “Refugee life has been hard. The old and young took turns falling ill, but now they have all recovered.” “Ah!” She let out a long breath and pressed her palms together in thanksgiving. “Thank Heaven!” Then she added, “But now I do not understand. Countless people have starved to death in Hangzhou—” She swallowed the rest. Her unspoken question was plain: if so many had starved, how had every member of the Hu household survived? Liu understood, but no brief answer could explain it. “It was truly the Bodhisattva’s protection. I could not tell it all in three days and nights.”

He changed the subject abruptly. “I hear Xueyan brought grain to Hangzhou but could not get it into the city and returned to Shanghai. Where is he?” “He has been very ill and is not fully recovered, though he is out of danger now.” The Seventh Lady spoke apologetically. “Forgive us, Third Uncle Liu, but you must not see him yet. Let us find out exactly what happened first. Did Governor Wang really die for his duty?” “A good death! A good death!” Liu, ordinarily so easy-going that nothing could make him take matters seriously, spoke with loud conviction. “A death that counted. To be honest, his reputation as an official was not particularly good. Now there can be nothing but good said of him. Even the rebels admire him.” According to Liu, on the day Hangzhou fell, Li Xiucheng, the Loyal King, rode alone straight to the governor’s yamen. Expecting Wang to take his own life, he had hoped to prevent it. But he was too late. Dressed in his court robes, Wang had already hanged himself from an osmanthus tree to the right of the main hall. Respecting his loyalty, Li had the body taken down and laid to the left of the pavilion by the eastern outer gate. He found a fine coffin for the burial preparations. Naturally, Wang’s household, old and young alike, was placed under protection. “The rebels have some humanity after all,” the Seventh Lady said. “Was there not talk of sending the coffin to Shanghai?” “I heard nothing of that.”

“What of the Manchu quarter?” Gu asked. “General Ruichang must have died for his duty too?” “The Manchu quarter held out for another three days—” During those days, Li suspended his attacks and sent men to offer terms of surrender. The terms were generous. The bannermen would be allowed to leave freely, taking their portable valuables, and would receive travelling money as well. Ruichang was also shown an edict from the Heavenly King granting the Hangzhou bannermen a special pardon. The intention was to allay their fears, but it had the opposite effect. Perhaps the terms were so generous that they were difficult to believe. Besides, defeated officers returning to the banner establishment would certainly be punished. Death would be hard to escape; they would lose the compensation their families might otherwise receive and might even be struck from the banner rolls, leaving their descendants disgraced and without a livelihood. Ruichang and his officers therefore agreed never to surrender. Once the three days were over, Li ordered an assault. Every member of the garrison took up arms and resisted fiercely. The Manchu quarter had a circuit of nine li, five gates, and large hongyi cannon mounted on its walls. More than three thousand rebels were killed by their fire. It was not until the afternoon of the first day of the twelfth month that the quarter fell. Ruichang drowned himself in a lotus pond. The deputy banner commanders Jiechun and Guanfu also took their own lives. Countless men, women, and children died in fires they themselves had set or by throwing themselves into West Lake.

At this point in his account, Liu himself grew frightened and pale. Gu quickly ordered hot tea so that he could recover his breath, then asked about his own experiences. “I happened to be in Hangzhou when the situation became critical. Xueyan discussed it with me. Huzhou was under siege too, so I could not return there for the time being. He asked me to escort his family to the three Tianzhu temples for refuge. We parted then, and I never saw him again. Later I decided that the temples were unsafe as well. We kept retreating farther into the countryside. Truly, the Bodhisattva protected us. We reached Liuxia.” Liuxia was a place west of Hangzhou. Its name meant “keep it.” According to tradition, when Emperor Gaozong of the Song moved his capital to Hangzhou and surveyed possible sites for a palace, this place pleased him, and he ordered it kept for consideration. Hence the name. With its hills, winding paths, streams, and many secluded places, it was well suited to sheltering refugees. “But there were so many refugees that a market sprang up. With all those people, there was no seclusion left. Seeing the danger, I told Xueyan’s wife that we must go farther and deeper into the hills: the more remote and impoverished the place, the better. She had good judgement and agreed. I found a spot deep in the mountains where people scarcely ever went. Best of all, there was a ravine with running water. Once we had water, there was nothing to fear. I hired men to build a thatched shelter, only three chi high, with boards laid underneath. Then I had seven or eight dan of rice, a jar of salted vegetables, and a dozen or so hams carried up. You may find it hard to believe: while untold numbers were starving in Hangzhou, we never went a day without solid rice.” “No wonder you do not look as though you have gone hungry,” said the Seventh Lady. “Did the rebels never find you?”

“They nearly did. One day I went gambling—” “Wait a moment,” she interrupted. “Were there places to gamble even among the refugees?” “Not only gambling. There were singing entertainers too. The market was thriving.” It was the refugees who had created that market. At first someone had put up a thatched hut to sell everyday necessities. It had no business name and was simply called “the little shop.” Then it became a tea shop, where people gathered to hear the news. Refugee life was full of anxiety and boredom; people needed distraction, and the tea shop became a gambling house. At first Liu had walked seven or eight li to this new market each day merely to avoid losing contact with the world and to learn what was happening. Later he went chiefly to satisfy his appetite for gambling. Pai gow, the treasure game, dice—he played them all. If he could hold the bank, he did; otherwise he played against it. He became one of the gambling house’s mainstays.

One afternoon he was banking at the small version of pai gow and enjoying extraordinary luck. Often he turned up a zero-point hand, only to find that the heavily backed opposing hand was also zero, so that he still came out ahead. At the height of the game, someone suddenly shouted, “The rebels are coming!” Liu was reluctant to believe it, having once been deceived by the same cry. On that occasion, the players had fled in panic and nothing had happened. When they returned, however, the gaming table had been stripped bare. They learned afterwards that someone had raised the false alarm to seize the money. Suspecting another attempt to profit from panic, Liu stayed while the others ran, calmly collecting his stakes. “Third Master Liu!” the proprietor warned him. “The rebels really are here.” Only then did Liu take fright. He hurriedly thrust several dozen taels into his waistband, slung five or six strings of copper cash over his shoulder, and bolted through the door. But it was already too late: two rebels pursued him relentlessly. Urgent though the danger was, he kept his head. His clothes were better than other people’s, and he had copper cash over his shoulder; they would never let him go. If he simply kept running, would he not lead the enemy to the family’s hiding place? Once he saw that, he knew what to do. He pulled a string of cash towards him, undid its slipknot, and sent a thousand coins clattering across the ground. After a few more strides he did the same again. When all five or six strings had been scattered, the weight was gone and he could run more lightly. Even so, he dared not take the direct route for fear of revealing their shelter. He made a long circuit and did not get home until evening.

“After that, Xueyan’s mother and wife forbade me to go gambling again. In any case, the market broke up. That first party was only a small detachment of rebels which had stumbled upon the place. There were too few of them to do much. The next day, or perhaps the day after, a large force came. They raped, looted, seized people, and finally set the place on fire. Who knows how many refugees suffered?” Liu’s expression was complicated. Fear and sorrow still lingered, yet he also looked relieved, even pleased with himself. “Luckily I saw what was coming. That was one wager I judged correctly.” Even discussing matters of life and death, he could not shed a gambler’s language. The Seventh Lady felt both admiration and amusement, but concern predominated. “You never met the rebels again after that?” “No. Several times we heard them, though. Only someone who has lived through it knows how dreadful that constant fear can be.”

Even now, the days of fear were not entirely over. Once the rebels entered Hangzhou, Li’s discipline kept the usual burning, killing, rape, and robbery from becoming too severe. Yet the people of Hangzhou were unwilling to submit. Men hanged themselves, women threw themselves into wells, and whole families took their own lives in countless numbers. This was not entirely an expression of loyalty to the dynasty. Many had lost all desire to live, for different reasons. Some feared humiliation and suffering. Some had seen horrors beyond anything they could emotionally endure and sought release. Others had neither clothes nor food and could find no means of prolonging their lives; believing death inevitable, they chose it sooner rather than later. Still others, having survived terrible calamities only to find everything lost in the end, killed themselves in bitter despair. Among a hundred thousand households, perhaps not one had escaped the catastrophe as the Hu family had. Yet now calamity threatened them again. They could no longer remain in their mountain hut, chiefly because their food was exhausted and the whole family had to come down. The city was uninhabitable: the stench of bodies made it impossible to approach. Had it not been the depths of winter, epidemic disease would already have spread. Fortunately, Hu’s mother had always been devout. Since her son’s sudden rise in the world, she had been more convinced than ever of the Bodhisattva’s protection. Monks and nuns who came soliciting donations for temples, large or small, never left her empty-handed. The three Tianzhu temples were an important centre of worship, and she knew them all. Finding a place to stay was therefore easy enough. The difficulty was still food. All Hangzhou depended on twenty thousand shi of rice that Li had brought from Jiaxing. Without the troops’ needs, it might have lasted some time. But the army received its rations first, and only afterwards was grain sold to civilians at controlled prices. The benefit to ordinary people was limited.

“Now the whole household, old and young, has only one meal of gruel a day. I can manage, but something must be done for the old people and the children.” “We shall find a way,” Gu said. “But there is something I do not understand, Third Uncle Liu. You have always had a good appetite. How can you keep going on one meal of gruel a day and still say you can manage?” Liu smiled a little sheepishly. “I find ways to get a meal at the rebels’ residences.” The Seventh Lady laughed too. “You really would snatch food from a tiger’s mouth! How do you manage it?” “That I cannot tell you. Let us leave idle talk aside. There is something serious I must discuss with you. A bastard is making trouble for Xueyan. If he is ignored, something will happen.” The “bastard” was Yuan Zhongqing, acting magistrate of Qiantang County. He had originally been a petty militia commander under Yuan Jiasan. After making a windfall from the war, he worked through one of Yuan’s advisers to have his name added to a list of men recommended for honours. He obtained sixth-rank status and the right to wear a blue feather. Later he violated military orders, and Yuan Jiasan intended to execute him. Terrified, he deserted overnight and fled to his native Jiangxi.

The governor of Jiangxi at that time was Zhang Fu, a fellow graduate of He Guiqing’s examination year and a favourite pupil of Muzhang’a. Yuan falsely represented himself as a sixth-rank county assistant magistrate entitled to the blue feather. Through further connections, he secured a position under Zhang. Before long, the rebels attacked Nanchang, the provincial capital. The people of Nanchang threw themselves into its defence, winning Zhang’s warm regard. That goodwill extended to their fellow native, and the “bastard” benefited: he was appointed to assist with military supplies at the manufacturing bureau. It was an extremely lucrative post, and Yuan found opportunities for profit on every side. Soon afterwards, the victory at Ningguo occasioned a special recommendation for honours. Zhang took particular care of Yuan, telling his advisers to give him an excellent assessment and recommend him for promotion to county magistrate. Ordinarily this would have been wonderful news. Anyone else would have been delighted. Yuan, however, looked miserable and could neither sit nor rest in peace. His colleagues naturally wondered why. “Yuan, promotion is just ahead of you! The authorities have taken special care. This is no routine recommendation with a token appraisal; yours runs to sixteen characters. The capital is sure to approve it. You are about to rule a county. Why look as though you have lost both parents?” “Promotion? It will take all the merit my ancestors accumulated to keep me from a criminal charge.” Then he shook his head. “No, the charge is certain. Even my ancestors’ merit will not help.” His colleague was astonished. “Why?” At first Yuan dared not explain. But the man was so earnest and helpful, solemnly promising to find a way out, that Yuan finally revealed his secret. “To be frank, my sixth rank and blue feather are perfectly genuine. The county assistant magistracy is a fake. What will happen when this recommendation goes up?”

“What? You are not really an assistant magistrate?” A false appointment could not stand scrutiny. Once Jiangxi’s recommendation reached the Ministry of Personnel, the records would naturally be checked. Yuan was being recommended for county magistrate because he supposedly held an assistant magistracy. The first question would be how he had obtained that appointment. Finding no record, the ministry would send an enquiry. Could Yuan produce an official certificate or a receipt for a purchased appointment? Such impostures were not unknown. Almost all the ministry’s clerks were old hands at extortion, knowing every variety of fraud and forever looking for an opening. Once they had discovered his weakness, they would demand whatever they pleased. Even after extracting enough money, they might still charge him with falsely claiming official status and have him sent into military exile. His colleague proved as good as his word. After consulting someone knowledgeable, he found a solution: Yuan could purchase an assistant magistracy now. Since the war began, the government had greatly expanded the sale of appointments to raise military funds. Each province had received large quantities of blank receipts from the Ministry of Personnel. These were called receipts for the actual amount paid. There were many schemes and discounts for purchasing appointments; the receipt stated the silver actually contributed and could later be exchanged for a formal ministry certificate. Paying the silver and obtaining a receipt was easy enough. The difficulty was the date. If the receipt showed that the appointment had been purchased after the recommendation, the deception would be exposed at once. “There is only one way. We must call in a favour.” “And favours cost money. I know,” Yuan replied. “My post yields a little, but I have spent it on cultivating friends. After food and expenses, there is not much left. Everything I have is here.” He opened a small chest kept by his pillow. It contained quite a few banknotes, but all the little sums together came to only a hundred taels or so. Backdating a receipt was a serious risk and would cost at least three hundred. His friend knew that Yuan was putting on a performance. But having taken the matter this far, he could hardly abandon a good deed halfway through merely because Yuan was unreliable. He added fifty taels of his own and, after much pleading with the people handling the business, got it arranged.
