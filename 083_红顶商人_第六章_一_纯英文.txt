HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 6 — Part 1 of 5

CHAPTER SIX

More than a month later, Liu Bucai returned to Shanghai. He had proved remarkably resourceful: his mission to bring out Hu Xueyan’s family had actually succeeded. Yet with the whole household about to arrive, Hu found himself burdened with a new worry. A’qiao was living with him in a rented “little house”, with no settled status. He did not know what to do about her and could only appeal to the Seventh Lady. “Seventh Sister, you must find a way for me. There is no one else I can consult.” “Naturally! I cannot refuse to concern myself with your affairs, Little Uncle. But first you must decide what you want.” His own attitude towards A’qiao was precisely the difficulty. He could only smile bitterly. “Seventh Sister, is not the whole Romance of the Western Chamber already in your head?” She did indeed know their situation thoroughly. In a word, they appeared devoted lovers, yet a deep gulf lay between them. A’qiao’s heart belonged to Hu, but she would rather remain his mistress outside the household than become a concubine within it. She dreaded the number of people in his family. Besides attending upon his mother, she would have to show deference to his principal wife and even watch Furong’s expressions for signs of favour or displeasure. There was another consideration. She had come from the governor-general of Liangjiang’s official residence. Though not his principal wife, she had virtually acted as mistress of his household. To become any other man’s concubine now seemed a descent. This was also the chief source of Hu’s misgivings. He himself had arranged her union with He Guiqing from beginning to end. To take her over now felt somehow improper, whether he considered his own feelings or other people’s criticism. Renting a “little house” and living as temporary lovers could at least be explained: she had travelled a thousand li to join him in adversity, and gratitude had awakened a love he could not resist. He could offer that excuse to himself and to others. Bringing her into his family would leave him without an answer.

There was another formidable obstacle. Mrs Hu had stated her position with absolute firmness. A successful man might have several wives and concubines; that was nothing unusual. But no one could take away the standing of the principal wife. If Hu chose another woman, he might marry her and bring her home, but he must not establish a separate household outside. At the same time, she had promised to treat every woman he brought home as a sister. Her conduct towards Furong had already proved that she kept her word. This made her position all the stronger; even Hu’s mother had to respect it. But understanding Hu’s predicament and deciding his course were two different things. The Seventh Lady could do the first, not the second. “If you want my help, Little Uncle, you must first make up your own mind. Will you keep her or part from her? Once your purpose is settled, we can find a way. But I must tell you plainly beforehand: whatever happens, I cannot help you conceal it.” Hu was bitterly disappointed. Concealing the affair from his wife was exactly what he had hoped she would help him do. He could find nothing to say.

His silence exposed his thoughts completely. The Seventh Lady advised him, “Little Uncle, harmony at home brings success in everything. Your wife is good and capable, a tremendous help to you. But let me add this: she is formidable too. You must never give her cause to hate you. If you want to bring A’qiao home, I shall wear out my legs and talk till my mouth is dry trying to persuade her. Of course, I cannot promise success. If you decide instead that you and A’qiao must go your separate ways, leave that to me. I can arrange a friendly parting without spoiling the affection between you.” “Then tell me how we could part on such terms.” “I cannot yet. I must give it some thought. But of that I am confident.” Hu thought for a long time without reaching a decision. “We shall talk tomorrow,” he sighed. “Think it over carefully tonight, Little Uncle, and make up your mind. If you mean to bring her home, I must begin preparing the way. I shall ask Third Uncle Liu”—she gestured outside—“to do some work with your mother and your wife beforehand.” Hu started. What sort of work? A moment’s reflection made it clear. Though his wife had said she would not forbid him to take concubines, that did not mean she felt no jealousy. Her sisterly affection for Furong arose partly from her determination to set a virtuous example, partly from Furong’s gentle willingness to accept a subordinate place. Their dispositions and circumstances had combined to produce an unusually happy arrangement. He could not expect every wife and concubine to behave so well. The Seventh Lady meant Liu to sound them out and smooth the way. If Hu truly wanted A’qiao at home, and A’qiao herself would agree to enter the household formally, such preparation was necessary. Yet this also reminded him how formidable A’qiao was—quite unlike Furong. Even if everything went smoothly at first, if she changed her mind and his wife kept her promise, the happy state would not last. Two strong women confronting one another would neither yield. Open quarrels and secret struggles would raise mountainous waves of jealousy. Then he would truly know how hard it was to be a husband caught between two wives.

These reflections brought worry and vexation together, further spoiling his appetite. But he never liked to dampen another person’s pleasure. Liu was in excellent spirits, so Hu roused himself to keep him company, and they talked until midnight. When he returned to the “little house”, A’qiao had tea and refreshments ready as usual. Heavy curtains in the bedroom kept out the sharp chill of early spring. She wore only a lined jacket of soft satin, cut so close that her waist seemed slender enough to clasp in one hand. Taking her hand, Hu found that she really was too lightly dressed. “For the sake of looking pretty, you freeze till you jump! Mind you do not make yourself ill.” She smiled without answering. She poured him hot tea, then cradled a small teapot of Qianlong five-colour porcelain, filled with hot tea, to warm her hands like a hand stove. Her bright eyes watched him, waiting for him to speak. Every evening he would tell her what had happened outside—where he had eaten, what amusing people he had met, what news he had heard. Tonight, contrary to his habit, he sat without a word. “You are tired, are you not?” she said. “Go to bed early.” “Mm. Tired.” His mouth answered, but his eyes remained fixed on the hanging kerosene light beneath the ceiling, called a “safety lamp”. She remembered only one other occasion when he had been so absent-minded, so blind to what lay before him: the night he learnt that Wang Youling had died for his country.

“What is it?” she asked unconsciously in the softest, most coaxing Suzhou dialect. “What is troubling you?” “My mother is coming.” Hu had said little to her about bringing out his family. She knew only that they were all trapped in Jiaxing and that reunion was impossible for the moment; she had thought no further. His sudden announcement threw her into confusion. “That is happy news!” she said, forcing a smile. “Happy, certainly. But it brings worries too. A’qiao, what do you say?” “About what?” She knew perfectly well. After a little thought he said ambiguously, “We cannot go on like this for ever.” Her colour changed and she lowered her head. He could see her lashes moving, but not the expression in her eyes. Now his own thoughts grew confused. He rose, threw himself on the bed and stared vacantly at the canopy. She said nothing, but neither did she weep beneath the lamp. Setting down her teapot, she lifted an earthenware pot from the kerosene stove and poured out some richly simmered chicken broth. From a tin she took seven or eight salted biscuits, arranged them on a porcelain plate and placed both dishes on the dressing table. Then she removed Hu’s boots and slipped embroidered slippers onto his feet. Only after attending to him in proper order did she speak. “Get up and eat.”

Sitting at the dressing table for his bedtime supper had always been the most agreeable moment of Hu’s day. As he ate, he watched A’qiao remove her make-up and listened to her soft Wu speech, now a remark, now a pause, full of entertaining trifles that required no thought. He would not have exchanged that pleasure for a throne. Tonight his mood was different. Yet on second thoughts he would not surrender the pleasure, and sprang from the bed like a tiger, giving her a fright. “You!” She cast him a reproachful glance. “You really are behaving strangely tonight.” “Take pleasure while it lasts.” Suddenly he felt ravenously hungry. “What else is there to eat?” “At this hour, only dry refreshments. The wonton seller and the man with Huzhou rice dumplings and tea eggs have already passed.” She asked, “Did you not eat enough at the Gus’?” “I scarcely ate at all.” “Why? Did the dishes not suit you?” “The Seventh Lady made Luzon shark’s fin, and there was raw fish too. Unfortunately I had no luck with such delicacies. I could not eat.” “Why ever not?” “Ah!” He shook his head. “Let us not speak of it. Bring me some more salted biscuits.” He would say no more, and she asked no more. She did as he requested, then sat to remove her make-up. Gathering her hair in one hand, she combed it repeatedly with a boxwood comb. The room was very quiet. Only the crunch of Hu’s biscuits could be heard.

“When is your mother arriving?” A’qiao suddenly asked. “Soon. In ten days or a fortnight.” “Where will she stay?” “I do not know yet.” “They are nearly here, and you still do not know where they will live? Is that not ridiculous?” “I have been busy these last few days and have had no time to arrange it. I shall see to it after Third Master Liu leaves tomorrow. With money, need one fear being unable to find a house? Only—” “What?” She turned towards him. “Why have you stopped?” “I do not know how large a house it ought to be.” “Stranger still! Surely you can work out how much space you need for the number of people?” “It is the number of people I cannot work out.” Hu glanced at her and deliberately turned away, though he was watching her expression in the mirror. She remained silent and composed, inscrutable. Then she rose and prepared the bed, still without a word. “Let us sleep.” Hu patted his waist. His stomach was full, but his heart felt empty, suspended between heaven and earth with nothing to hold it.

“What is really troubling you? Say it plainly. All this trailing thread and tangled vine makes one itch with impatience.” How could a woman as clever, alert and worldly as she fail to understand? Her deliberate pretence of ignorance was no good sign. Hu did not usually care to play intricate games with women, but with this woman, at this moment, he made an exception. Meeting reserve with reserve, he smiled. “A hidden thought is more enjoyable if you guess it slowly. Why expose it all at once?” Unable to get through to him, A’qiao fell into offended silence. She finished arranging the quilts and helped him undress and get into bed. Then she moved a foreign lamp to a small stand inside the great rosewood bed and turned down the wick, letting the dim yellow light hide her displeasure. In the stillness, Hu’s thoughts became clear. He saw only two courses open to himself and A’qiao: continue as they were, or part. “Do not torment yourself with fancies,” he said without quite intending to speak aloud. “Let me think of a proper solution.”

“What are you talking about, out of the blue like that?” “What else but you? My wife will not allow you to live outside, and you will not live with the family. I must find a way.” A’qiao said nothing. She was reasonable enough to understand his difficulty. But if she yielded out of consideration and agreed to join the household, only to find they could not get on, she would have to leave in disgrace. They would not merely quarrel; she would acquire a bad name: she parted from every man she joined. Better to harden her heart now and leave him to struggle with the problem. She could decide what to do when she saw the result. Yet she must show some tenderness. From beneath the quilt she reached across and clasped his left arm tightly, expressing both her gratitude and her dependence upon him.

Hu had no one else to consult. Once more he went to the Seventh Lady. “Seventh Sister, there is no need to mention living together. I think it would be best to continue as we are. Since you will not help me conceal it, could you help me gain her consent?” “You mean you want me to plead with your wife and persuade her to let you go on living outside together?” “Yes.” “Difficult!” She shook her head vigorously. “A country has its laws, and a family its rules. Your wife runs the household now, and the rule she has made is reasonable. Even your mother could hardly override it, much less those of us who are her juniors.” “Never mind seniority. She listens to you. Plead for me this once: an exception that will never be repeated.” “Little Uncle, how can you talk of not repeating it? You must never say that. It would only make her angrier: ‘Oh, two are not enough! Now he is thinking about a third!’” “You are right. Say whatever you think best, so long as you succeed.” “I fear I shall not.” After a long silence she added, “For your sake, Little Uncle, I shall have to risk this rebuff. But you must not blame me if I fail.” “There is no need to tell me that, Seventh Sister. I leave everything to you. Whatever else may be forgotten, I owe her so much for nursing me through that illness in Ningbo.” He was reminding her to lay special stress on A’qiao’s service: it justified an exception, an unusual indulgence. Yet his reminder too was unnecessary. The Seventh Lady had already considered it. Even if she expected a rebuff, she needed some ground on which to appeal, however slight the chance of success. That ground was A’qiao’s hazardous journey to Ningbo and her tireless nursing, hardly undressing as she tended him and prepared his medicine. She had also decided where to begin: by persuading Liu Bucai.

“Last winter, when Little Uncle took rice to Hangzhou but could not get into the city, he went on to Ningbo and fell gravely ill with fever. When the news reached Shanghai, I was beside myself. Third Uncle Liu, consider the circumstances: Ningbo was in the Longhairs’ hands, and he was a stranger to both the people and the place. How could he survive such an illness? Everything depended on having someone considerate to care for him. There could be no neglect. I discussed it with Old Gu and said I must go myself. He said my going would only make matters worse. Why? First, I am too impatient to nurse a sick man. Second, though our friendship makes us as close as family, and I myself would not mind, Little Uncle might feel constrained. He would be embarrassed to ask me to bring tea or water or attend to the less pleasant necessities. A patient must not lack anything. Without someone close and devoted, someone who understood his needs as her own, he could not recover.” “That is true,” Liu said. “So A’qiao volunteered?” “No. I begged her to go. She and Little Uncle had once had something between them, but that was long over. Everything had to begin afresh, and I was the one who lit the fire. Third Uncle Liu, consider it for me: do I not bear some responsibility for the present situation?” “I understand. If you had not begged her to go, there would be no difficulty now. Yet you begged her solely to save Xueyan’s life—and in fact it is as good as true that she saved it. Is that what you mean?” “Exactly!” she said delightedly. “Third Uncle Liu, you have a worldly man’s quick understanding. One hint is enough!”

“Seventh Sister,” Liu said gravely, “put those two arguments to Xueyan’s wife and she will have nothing to say against them. She is a thoroughly reasonable woman. But she will not be happy at heart. Is it worth forcing this concession from her? Think it over.” “Thank you, Third Uncle Liu. But for Little Uncle’s sake, I have no choice.” “That is not the way to put it. Our friendship is close enough that we need not fear displeasing one another. In doing this, Seventh Sister, you must ask whether it will benefit the whole Hu family. You cannot regard the matter as settled merely because Xueyan gets his way for the moment.” His view went deeper. Reflecting upon it, she suddenly understood. Yet there was a difference between them. Liu was an intimate of the Hu household and lived with them. Whatever private troubles that family had, he naturally knew far more of them than she did. She felt that they must discuss the matter afresh. “Third Uncle Liu, I must listen to that. I ought to do what is best for the whole family. Besides, once we are all in Shanghai, it will mostly be the women who visit one another. If Mrs Hu harbours a grievance against me and we are only outwardly cordial, where will the pleasure be? There is just one thing I still do not understand. If we arrange this, will it really disturb the harmony of the whole household?”
