HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 6 — Part 2 of 5

“That is hard to say. From what I know, Xueyan’s wife has her own way of running the household. Break her rule once, and her words will carry less weight thereafter.” “Little Uncle has said it would be this once only, never again. If anything of the sort happens in future, I shall stand up for Mrs Hu before she even needs to speak.” Liu suddenly chuckled, then sighed without answering. His manner suggested that she did not understand what she was doing, which rather annoyed her. “Third Uncle Liu, have I said something wrong?” “Your words are right, and your heart is warm. But that is precisely how you will bring trouble upon yourself. As the saying goes, even an honest magistrate cannot settle a family quarrel. Seventh Sister, even if you were Judge Bao himself and judged the matter perfectly, you would still have trouble.” “How? I do not understand.” “Seventh Sister, you are clever in everything else and confused only here. In a lawsuit, either the plaintiff wins or the defendant does. You mend one thing and damage another. Why put yourself through it?” She understood at once. If she sided with Mrs Hu in future, she must offend Hu himself. Was that not mending one thing at the cost of another? “Enough, enough! Really, Third Uncle Liu, if you have a sound reason, why not state it plainly instead of going round in such circles? Fortunately I have changed and have the patience to question you. Otherwise you would have led me astray!” Her complaint was quite unreasonable, but amusingly so. Liu could only laugh.

“I do not want to be a female Judge Bao. I would rather remain a female Zhang Fei.” There was something behind this, which Liu recognised at once. He had to answer. “What do you intend, Seventh Sister? A female Zhang Fei is all very well, but a rash Zhang Fei would be another matter.” “Zhang Fei could be subtle beneath his roughness too. Naturally I know where to stop. Rest assured: there will be no trouble.” Liu considered. “Then do you still want me to prepare Xueyan’s wife?” “Yes. But the argument will no longer be the same.” That puzzled him. Surely this was a question of yes or no: either forget the matter entirely or raise it. How could there be another way of raising it? “The first half can still be used. Mrs Hu should understand how A’qiao and Little Uncle became attached again. If she knows the whole story, she will not begin with a prejudice against A’qiao.” The Seventh Lady paused. “The latter half must be changed as follows.” Her plan was first to reassure Mrs Hu, which would also reassure Hu himself. Once the family reached Shanghai, his separate mistress could not be concealed. He would also ask the Seventh Lady what she had achieved. She therefore needed his wife’s cooperation to keep the situation calm for the time being.

“I need time to work patiently upon A’qiao. My fear is that, before I have made any progress, husband and wife will already be quarrelling. Once people openly fall out, a deadlock often follows. Mrs Hu had best pretend to know nothing. If Little Uncle stays away from his own camp at night, she need not investigate.” “I understand, and Xueyan’s wife can certainly do that. But he often springs surprises. What if she pretends ignorance and he comes straight out with the truth, face to face?” “I doubt that will happen. If it does, she should neither agree nor object, but put him off as best she can.” “I suppose that is the only course.” Liu nodded. “In a word, overcome firmness with softness.” “Which means tact. Tell Mrs Hu that I undertake to settle the matter within three months. What do I mean by settle? Her rule will remain intact. If A’qiao will not enter the Hu household, she will certainly have joined some other man’s.” “So you mean to transplant the blossom to another branch.” Liu was intrigued. “Have you already found a man for her?” “No, no. I must look slowly.” Suddenly she laughed. “Actually, Third Uncle Liu, you would be quite a match.” “You are joking! How could I graduate in the same class as Xueyan?”

It was already the chou watch, after midnight, when Hu returned home, but he was still in excellent spirits. Sitting by the dressing table as A’qiao removed her make-up, he asked about the women’s outing that evening. “We went for a foreign dinner first. There was nothing very good to eat. Their fried quail was not as good as the eight-piece fried chicken in a Beijing restaurant. And with only the two of us, we looked like—” She shook her head and smiled bitterly, unwilling to finish. Like what? Hu closed his eyes and imagined himself as one of the waiters serving them. Two ladies without male companions, boldly showing themselves in public and handling knives and forks over a foreign dinner—what sort of women would they seem? Judging by their ages and their dress, they would look like two remarkably fine proprietresses of expensive courtesan houses. That must have been A’qiao’s thought, and the reason she refused to finish. Understanding this, he understood her feelings as well. Even if she did not aspire to an aristocratic mansion, she had certainly come to despise the pleasure quarters. Her desire for a respectable life was sincere. This made it harder still to give her up. He blurted out, “What did Seventh Sister say to you?”

“What do you mean, what did she say?” A’qiao’s hands stopped in the act of undoing her hair. “What should she have to say to me? You knew beforehand, did you not? Then tell me: why did she suddenly leave Master You at home today and invite me out to the theatre and a foreign dinner?” This volley of questions threw Hu into confusion. He could not parry it. But he possessed one gift: a smile could conceal any embarrassment, and his embarrassment never lasted long. He soon recovered his composure. “I do not understand what you mean. I was asking whether she told you why she suddenly felt like taking you out. Apparently you do not know. Then I know still less.” “Even a man as clever as you does not know?” She gave a faint, cold smile. “Then there is nothing more to say.” “We are husband and wife chatting together. What harm is there in talking?” She suddenly raised her head, her clear, bright eyes fixed upon him. “Husband and wife? Am I so fortunate?” His careless remark seemed to have given her a weapon, but it did not defeat him. “Here we are husband and wife,” he answered calmly. “Then,” she nodded as though speaking to herself, “there is all the more reason not to listen to the Seventh Lady.” “What did she say?” “She advised me to go back.” “Go back” could mean either returning to her own family or entering Hu’s household as a concubine. Her family home was in Mudu, near Suzhou, which was now in the Longhairs’ hands. There could be no reason to advise her to go there.

Once her meaning was clear, he ought to ask what she intended. But he already knew the answer, so there was no point. After a long silence he burst out, “Tomorrow I really must look for a house.” His manner was rather inscrutable. A’qiao remembered how, when they had discussed finding a house a few days earlier, he had hinted that she should live with his principal wife. Did he still mean that? She must find out. “If you cannot find one in time,” she ventured, “why not let them stay here first?” “There is not room.” Did he mean there was no room in any case, or no room if she remained? She still could not tell and had to try again. “They can squeeze in for the moment. Refugees cannot expect every comfort.” “And you?” “I?” She spoke resolutely. “I shall move elsewhere.” “Why trouble? Better to leave things as they are.” Hu thought for a moment and decided plain speaking would be best. “I feel as you do. Continuing just like this would be best. I have already asked the Seventh Lady to plead with my wife when she arrives, to say a few kind words for us and persuade her to make a special exception.” “That is strange!” A’qiao sounded angry. “She urged me to go back instead—the exact opposite of what you asked. Why?”

Hu deeply regretted his carelessness. Knowing what the Seventh Lady had advised, he should not have told the truth and made her seem disloyal to the task entrusted to her. At the same time, A’qiao’s question alerted him to something. Yes—what did the Seventh Lady mean by acting so? It was puzzling. Whatever the reason, he must defend her now. “Perhaps she was only trying to discover your attitude. What did she say?” “She said, ‘A woman must have a proper settled place. Better to join the Hu family formally and kowtow upon entering the household. Otherwise you should make a firm decision.’” “What did she mean by a firm decision?” “Ask her.” The weariness of A’qiao’s tone showed that this “decision” meant a course she could not possibly accept. Putting her remarks together, Hu understood the Seventh Lady’s intention, but not why she had formed it. “She often takes unexpected measures. Do not be angry yet. Calm yourself and wait to see what happens.”

“How long must I wait?” A’qiao suddenly cried, her voice high and shrill. “Do you know what she said about you? That you are slippery! That you have no constancy and fall in love with every woman you see! Dogs have eaten the conscience of a man like that. She urged me to part from you soon, or I should suffer for it later. Well, I think she was right in every word. Hmph! You could deceive a person to death and never pay for it.” The barrage, striking both directly and by insinuation, left Hu bewildered. He could scarcely believe his ears. Naturally he was angry too—not with A’qiao, but with the Seventh Lady. She had not merely failed to act loyally for him: she had betrayed a friend. With such friendship between them, how could she resort to so underhand a trick? It was more than he could swallow. He had never been so angry. His face turned livid and his lips pale. Just as he was about to erupt, a thought warned him. The Seventh Lady was called a hero among women. She was neither without judgement nor ignorant of loyalty between friends. There must be a reason for what she had said—a very deep reason, too deep even for him to fathom. At that thought his expression softened at once. “What else did she say?” he asked. “What else?” A’qiao’s anger was not merely lingering; it grew as she thought. “Unless you had given her some hint that you meant to abandon me, would she have said such things? You heartless—” Suzhou women were fond of the curse “may you be cut into a thousand pieces”, but some tenderness remained in A’qiao, and she swallowed the words. Hu offered no defence. Until he understood the Seventh Lady’s purpose, he feared that defending himself might spoil it. Yet he could not say nothing at all. He answered vaguely, “Why must you listen to her?”

“Then whom should I listen to? You?” A’qiao pressed him harder. “Speak! Give me one plain, firm assurance.” What assurance did she mean? He was genuinely puzzled. “Tell me what you want me to say.” “There! I knew your heart had changed.” She stamped her foot and spoke bitterly. “Do you really not know what to say? You simply refuse to say it. Enough, enough! At last I know you.” Her angry cries in the quiet night roused the maids and older servants. Shadows gathered outside the window; they seemed to want to enter and mediate but dared not. Hu felt thoroughly uncomfortable. He rose and urged, “It is late. Let us sleep.” Then he quietly made his way into the adjoining room, where there was another bed used occasionally for afternoon naps. It offered a convenient refuge from the lioness’s roar. He turned up a lamp and sat alone, lost in thought. Seeing no further disturbance, the servants withdrew. A’qiao, offended, ignored him and went to bed by herself. Hu did not dare provoke her again and made do with a night apart. The next morning, when he came out of the adjoining room, she was already sitting at the dressing table. Silent and pale, she looked thinner than ever. On closer inspection he thought he saw tear marks. Perhaps she had wept enough in the night to soak her pillow.

“Why do this to yourself?” he said. “You are ruining your health.” “I have thought it over,” she said dully. “This cannot come to a proper end. And I cannot match you. Enough, enough.” She waved her hand and turned her head aside, as though relinquishing everything. Naturally he felt distressed, but could think of nothing suitable to comfort her. “I am giving Chief Yu lunch today,” he said, meaning that he must go out early. “Go, then.” There was coldness in her voice. He hesitated, longing to say another word or two of reassurance. Yet he had no adequate assurance to give and could only abandon the attempt. It was barely ten when he reached Gu’s house. The Seventh Lady was already up and vigorously directing the servants in preparing to receive their guests. The rosewood chairs in the main hall all wore bright red satin covers embroidered in gold. Fresh flowers stood in the vases, and eight tall silver dishes, polished to a brilliant shine, held fresh and dried fruit. The weather was fine, sunlight filled the courtyard and there was no wind. All the doors in the screen and the windows stood open, making the house especially spacious and cheerful.

“You are early, Little Uncle! Have you eaten?” Suddenly she noticed his appearance. “You look very unwell. Are you ill?” “No. I scarcely slept last night.” “Why?” She added, “Even one sleepless night should not leave you looking like this. There must be a reason.” “There is. Where is Old Gu?” “At the firm. He will be back at half past eleven.” “It is still early for the guests. Are you busy, Seventh Sister? If not, I should like a few words.” Her eyes blinked several times. Calmly she answered, “I am free. Let us talk in Yingchun’s study.” Once there, Hu could not begin. He held his tea and stared into space. She already had some idea of his trouble. If she was right, the effect had come rather quickly. She needed time to consider what to say. His silence therefore suited her, and she remained silent too.

At last he spoke. “Seventh Sister, A’qiao had a terrible quarrel with me last night. What exactly did you say to her?” Instead of answering, she asked, “How did she quarrel with you?” “She said I had hinted to you that I meant to abandon her. There is no truth in that, is there?” She smiled. “What else?” With difficulty he answered, “She said you called me slippery, said dogs had eaten my conscience, and said I fell in love with every woman I saw.” She smiled again, this time with a trace of embarrassment. “Little Uncle,” she asked almost teasingly, “were you angry?” “A little at first. Then I thought again and ceased to be angry. You are not a woman without judgement. There must be a reason for speaking that way.”

Relief and gratitude spread over her face. “Little Uncle, it was only because I knew you understood my intentions that I dared act so rashly. In fact, it was not rash. I discussed it with someone beforehand and thought it through carefully. I concluded that this was the best course. But I could not tell you first. Had I done so, it would not have worked.” Setting that aside, she asked again about A’qiao. “What did she say? Why did she quarrel with you? Because she believed me?” “She believes I hinted that I meant to leave her, and that you said those things for that reason. I should have thought the same in her place. With our friendship as it is, why else would you run me down so completely before her?” “Quite right. Entirely right.” She asked with great concern, “And you, Little Uncle—did you defend yourself?” “No. From the way things stood, there seemed little use.” His unwitting cooperation, like drum and drumstick answering one another, fixed her resolve beyond alteration. She had accepted Liu Bucai’s advice and considered the awkward situation between Hu and A’qiao from the standpoint of the whole family’s harmony. A clean cut through the tangle seemed the best solution. Yet she could not control matters single-handed, nor could she explain her intention to Hu in advance. She could only act as opportunity offered, saying as much as each stage allowed.
