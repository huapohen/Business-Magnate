HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 6 — Part 5 of 5

With such terms and such friendship behind them, Fatty Zhang ought, by all ordinary reasoning, to have accepted without hesitation. Yet he still wavered. For one thing, the blow he had suffered in banking had truly chilled his heart. For another, friendship required feeling to be repaid with feeling: precisely because Hu Xueyan treated him so generously, he must consider whether he could open up new business and put the bank in order. If he could not be sure of doing so, it would be better to decline now and preserve their friendship than to disappoint his friend later. Of course, he could neither bring himself to refuse nor bear to let the opportunity go. After thinking it over and over, he said, “Let me look into it first.” “Look into it? There is no need!” Hu replied. “Fukang is naturally less lively than it was when Governor Wang was alive. Compared with the other Shanghai banks, I can honestly say that we fall short of the best but stand well above the worst. There is absolutely no deficit. Our borrowers are reliable, and the few large depositors are all substantial men. They will not all descend on us to withdraw their money at once. The trouble is that I cannot give it my whole attention. The chief clerk I engaged is a straightforward fellow, but his health is poor; the place has become lifeless and never gets ahead. With you there, things would certainly be different. Let me provide an idea or two and you carry them through. Working together, we shall polish Fukang’s signboard until it shines like gold again.” Put that way, the venture was worth undertaking. Still, Zhang asked, “In exactly what capacity am I to join Fukang? You know the rules of banking.” By those rules, authority rested entirely with the chief clerk; shareholders were not to interfere. Hu had already thought this through. “In relation to me, you are a shareholder,” he answered without hesitation. “In relation to Fukang, you are the chief clerk. You will be working for yourself, not serving another man.” The explanation was complete, and Zhang was satisfied. “Then it is settled,” he said firmly. “You supply the ideas; I carry them out. You represent us outside; I take charge within.” “Excellent! That is exactly what I mean—”

“Wait!” A sudden thought struck Zhang, and he asked urgently, “What about the man already there?” “You need not worry about him. His health is poor, and his son has made his way in the world. He is a comprador with an American trading firm and has long urged his father to retire and enjoy life. The old fellow is grateful for my treatment of him. He has offered his resignation several times, but when I refused to let him go he felt ashamed to insist. He could wish for nothing better than to have you take over.” Zhang was relieved. “I was afraid of breaking another man’s rice bowl.” Once more he grew reflective. “My own employer treated me badly. We must not make him say behind our backs that his employer treated him badly too.” “Do you think I am that sort of man?” Hu asked. “Old Zhang, once a gentleman gives his word, four horses cannot overtake it. From this moment, we are partners. Now let us talk business. How do you think we should proceed?” That stumped Zhang. He had begun as an apprentice and risen step by step to chief clerk. Before advising on internal management he would have to examine the actual position, and before discussing expansion he would have to study the market. Asked to produce an idea out of thin air, he was helpless. At last he said, “The price of silver fluctuates sharply nowadays. With prompt information, we could make a handsome profit simply buying and selling.” “Certainly. You can deal with that yourself; there is no need to consult me. What we must discuss is how to let out a long line and fly our kite far ahead: three years from now, once affairs have settled down, how can we make a sudden leap into prosperity?” “That,” Zhang said with a laugh, “is beyond my abilities.”

Business talk always roused Hu’s enthusiasm. Now, pleasantly warmed by wine, he was more eager than ever. “We seldom have time to ourselves. Let us make some proper plans today. Old Zhang, you must know the practices of the Shanxi remittance houses?” “Different trades are mountains apart. Banks and remittance houses may look like the same business, but their methods differ.” Before Hu, Zhang dared give nothing but an honest answer. “Besides, their influence does not extend south of the Yangtze. I have had no opportunity to see their inner workings.” “For us bankers, the Shanxi houses are the only formidable rivals. Know yourself and your enemy, and you can win a hundred battles. That is why I always pay close attention to them. Let me tell you something about them first.” As Hu understood it, the Shanxi houses had originally dealt chiefly in remittances, with the capital as their centre. In recent years, war and difficult roads had made it inconvenient to transport government funds to Beijing. Almost without a formal arrangement, the houses had taken over some of the duties of the central and provincial treasuries. They paid no interest on government balances, while their remittance charges were substantial: naturally, the profits were great. Moreover, wealthy merchants and high officials in the provinces considered the capital the safest place under heaven. They therefore sent much of their ready cash there, which in practice meant depositing it with the houses. Such deposits were intended to preserve capital, not earn income, so the interest paid was very low.

“Once you have deposits, you must find an outlet for them. Leave funds to rot, and large ingots will never give birth to little ones,” Hu said. “In recent years, the Shanxi houses have commonly lent to officials in the capital. They call it making ‘capital loans’. I hear that against a note for ten thousand taels they actually advance seven thousand—” “What?” Zhang interrupted loudly. “What sort of loan is that? Worse than the usurers who collect their instalments every day!” “You think it worse, but the borrowers agree willingly enough. The wool comes off the sheep’s back in the end. It is the common people who suffer.” “How so?” “Think about it. When an official borrows, what can he repay with? Naturally, the people repay for him. Suppose someone is appointed provincial treasurer of your Zhejiang. He must make payments in the capital and pay for his journey. Once he arrives, he needs a residence, chairs and horses, servants—what does not cost money? So he first takes a capital loan. After assuming office, he finds some public money to divert and pays it off, then gradually makes up the sum. Unless he devises ways to extract it from the people, he will be left with a deficit in his accounts.” “But is that not risky? Suppose he refuses to acknowledge the debt once he takes office?” “He will not. First, there is a guarantor, who must also be an official in the capital. Second, there is a signed note. If he repudiates the debt, a petition can be lodged with the Censorate. One impeachment from a censor, and the debtor loses his post. Third, others will help the houses and prevent defaults. Why? The poor Hanlin scholars live on loans. They hope to be sent out as examiners or education commissioners, collect ceremonial gifts from their pupils and repay their debts. Having repaid, they can borrow again and keep going. If anyone defaults, the houses may act together, declare officials untrustworthy and stop lending. The poor scholars would panic. They will help recover the money.” Hu paused, then went on. “There is only one real risk: something may happen to a newly appointed official on his journey. He may die or lose his appointment. Even then, it depends. A sound guarantor will shoulder the whole obligation.” “No wonder!” Zhang exclaimed. “These past few years the Qi, Tai and Ping groups of remittance houses have been opening branches all over the provinces. So this is the advantage!” Eager to try it himself, he asked, “Why should we not learn from them?” “Exactly!” Hu drained his cup. “That is just what I have in mind.”

Hu intended to follow their example with two kinds of loan. The first would go to officials. With travel between north and south so difficult and circumstances so altered, transfers and promotions in recent years had often departed from normal procedure. The requirement that officials be sent to the appropriate ministry and presented for an imperial audience had not been abolished, but exceptions were commonly made. In particular, among civil and military officials recommended for promotion on the strength of wartime service, there were even provincial treasurers and judicial commissioners—men responsible for the finances or justice of an entire province—who had never been to Beijing. A man appointed from the capital could, of course, take a capital loan. But an official promoted within the lower Yangtze region—a Jiangsu magistrate made prefect in Hubei, for instance—could not move without money for the journey and for establishing his household. Hu proposed to help such men with loans on the same model. “There is risk, of course. But we must reckon the whole account, using the surplus here to cover the shortfall there. Since the likin taxes began, goodness knows how many men have grown rich. Someone given a tax post gains an extra day’s profit for every day earlier he arrives. However high the interest, he will borrow, and we shall not suffer a bad debt. Our method is to earn more from these accounts to make up for losses on others. We can say it plainly from the start: we shall ‘take from the rich to help the poor’.” “Take from the rich to help the poor!” Zhang repeated the phrase once or twice and nodded. “I understand the principle. What is the second kind?”

“The second is for families of the inland gentry who have fled to Shanghai. At home, most lived on rents. In the morning they carried a birdcage to the teahouse; in the afternoon they slept at the bathhouse; in the evening they sat down to a drinking party and went home tipsy. Of the three hundred and sixty days of the year, at least three hundred passed like that. To flatter such men, you call them young gentlemen of leisure. Less politely, they are idle vagabonds. Had their ancestors not earned merit and left them great estates, they would certainly have become generals of the outstretched hand. When they first fled here, they had some cash and valuables, so they did not immediately fall on hard times. But spend without earning, and even a mountain is exhausted—especially in this foreign settlement, with its feasting and pleasure. After these few years, a good many once-famous young gentlemen are close to begging.” This was no exaggeration. Zhang had encountered several himself; the most wretched even depended on their wives and daughters to earn a living entertaining men. He therefore sympathised deeply with Hu’s account, but disapproved of lending to such people. “It is easy to relieve an emergency and hard to rescue poverty. We should be certain to lose the money.” “No,” Hu said. “We must look further ahead. The Longhairs’ allotted days are almost over. Once Jiangsu and Zhejiang are recovered, the refugees will go home. The rebels cannot carry off their great tracts of farmland. After a year or two of hardship, they will be gentlemen of leisure again. Why should the loans be lost?” “Ah!” Zhang drew a deep breath. “That had not occurred to me. On that basis, I have a method to suggest.” “Let us hear it.” “Require their title deeds as security. If they have no deeds with them, have them sign a note stating that, if they fail to repay, specified land shall be valued and surrendered in settlement.” “Quite right! That makes it safer still.”

“One thing more!” Their long conversation had taught Zhang a great deal and sharpened his wits. “Since we are relieving poverty, we must look ahead. These men brought up as young masters spend ten thousand if they have ten thousand, regardless of the consequences. We should not advance the full amount on a property at the first application. Leave something in reserve for an additional advance when they cannot make ends meet.” Now they were thinking along exactly the same lines. Their discussion grew wider and deeper. Yet they spoke only of lending. Where were the funds to come from? The question had been in Zhang’s mind all along, but he had held it back, expecting so meticulous a man as Hu to raise it without prompting. Hu never did, however, and Zhang was obliged to ask. “Old Hu, both these kinds of loan are long-term. Those secured on land, especially, cannot have a definite prospect of repayment until the country is recovered. That may take more than two or three years. It will require a great deal of capital. Have you considered that?” “Of course. How can we do business with loans going out and no deposits coming in? I have a way of attracting deposits, but I fear you may not approve.” “Why should you think so? We are in business. If deposits come our way, surely we shall not turn them down?” Hu did not answer at once. He smiled and sipped his wine with a mysterious air that once more left Zhang unable to fathom him. Zhang’s feelings were mixed: admiration, but also wariness. Hu’s resources were so unpredictable that one could never safely let down one’s guard with him.

At last Hu spoke, asking a wholly unexpected question. “Old Zhang, suppose I were a Longhair and wanted to deposit money with you under an assumed name. Would you dare accept it?” “Well—why should I not?” “What if there were conditions?” “What conditions?” “He asks no interest, and it is not a demand deposit. He will withdraw it after three years or five. There is only one condition: whatever happens, you must repay the full amount.” “Of course we repay the full amount. What else could we do?” “Old Zhang, you have not understood what I mean, nor what is at stake. You know what happens when a household’s property is confiscated. Any private assets entrusted elsewhere are ordinarily pursued as well. What I mean is that, even if the authorities recover this deposit from you, you must still repay the depositor. Would you dare accept that risk?” Only now did Zhang see. “I would not!” he said, shaking his head vigorously. “If the authorities came looking for the money, we would have to disclose it. Otherwise we should be guilty of concealing rebel property—a terrible charge. Once they had taken it away, how could we pay the man when his deposit matured?”

“I knew you would not dare,” Hu said. “I would. Why? Because I am certain they will not pursue it.” “Oh? What makes you so sure? Let me hear your reasoning.” “What reasoning is needed? We have been fighting the Longhairs for years and have taken plenty of their leaders alive. When have you seen the authorities trace their assets?” He lowered his voice. “Look at the imperial troops. Once they catch a Longhair, they strip him of everything and never report it. If anyone traced the property, they would have to begin with the troops who plundered it. Would that not be making trouble for themselves? As I said, the Longhairs’ days are nearly over. Many are laying secret plans. There is still one catastrophe for them to survive; once through it, they can live in comfort for the rest of their days.” “What catastrophe?” “The fall of the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom. Once it collapses, the Longhairs will be like rats crossing the street, with everyone shouting to strike them down. That first confusion will be the most dangerous time. Once order is restored, the court will naturally issue an edict: punish the ringleaders, spare those forced to follow. Still less will it pursue their private property. That is why I say that, if they survive that catastrophe, they can live free of want for the rest of their lives.” Now Zhang understood everything. The Longhairs who had gorged themselves on plunder needed two kinds of protection against the coming disaster: protection for their lives and for their wealth. When it came, they might escape alive but lose their property. In other words, saving their lives was easier than saving their wealth. Hence the need to arrange matters early.

Once he saw the scheme, Zhang could not help exclaiming, “Excellent! Excellent!” Yet he shook his head instead of nodding. “Old Hu,” he said, with something of the man who feared the sky might fall, “such ingenuity may provoke Heaven’s jealousy!” “There is nothing so extraordinary about it. I mean no one harm. Why should Heaven be jealous?” “But is it against the law?” Feeling he had spoken too bluntly, Zhang hurried to explain. “Old Hu, it is because the scheme is so good. As the saying goes, good things meet many obstacles. I am afraid there may be something that makes it unworkable. I cannot feel quite at ease.” “You are right to ask. We cannot break the law. But the court’s laws are definite things: we do what they say, and that is obeying the law. Where they say nothing, we may follow our own wishes. Concealing a criminal’s property is certainly illegal. But if it is a question of law, we have an answer: when a man comes to make a deposit, he does not have ‘I am a Longhair’ written on his forehead. If he deposits under an assumed name, who knows his identity?” “In fact, we do know. Our consciences cannot accept that.”

“Old Zhang, Old Zhang!” Hu took a sip, at once moved and pleased. “I was not mistaken in you. You are good-hearted by nature. That is excellent. But as for conscience, a merchant’s conscience concerns his customers. Fair dealings, cheating neither young nor old—that is our conscience. Officials must have a conscience towards the court. It is really the same principle as a merchant’s duty to his customers. ‘Master the arts of letters and arms, then sell them to the imperial house.’ The court is the customer of civil and military officials; it feeds and clothes them. They cannot disregard their consciences towards it. We need not consider conscience in that way.” “What should we consider, then?” “The law. To obey the court’s laws is to satisfy our conscience towards the court.” Zhang nodded and drank in thought. After a long while, he spoke with pleasure. “Old Hu, I think I understand at last. For years I have wondered: among the four callings—scholar, farmer, craftsman and merchant—why does one hear of dishonest merchants, but not dishonest scholars, farmers or craftsmen? Evidently, a merchant’s conscience needs a different explanation. I could never work out what it was. Now I understand. Obey the court’s laws and treat customers fairly: that is conscience, and that keeps one from being a dishonest merchant.” “Exactly! To speak plainly, if merchants obeyed the court’s laws and officials were conscientious towards it, the world would certainly be at peace. More than that: if officials were conscientious, merchants would not dare disobey the law. When officials have no conscience towards the court, to demand that merchants supply it is mere pedantry.” “Yes, yes. Let me think that through.” Zhang spoke as he reflected. “Suppose a Longhair leader is captured and his household’s property confiscated. An official stifles his conscience and pockets the property. That is acting without conscience. But if we act conscientiously? A Longhair offers a deposit under an assumed name, and we refuse because the money ought to be confiscated. What is the result? We simply benefit a corrupt official, who pockets it all the same. Exactly!” He slapped the table and raised his voice. “It is no use for merchants alone to be conscientious towards the court. Obeying the law is enough!” “Old Zhang!” Hu too raised his cup with pleasure. “Now you have truly understood.”

They thoroughly enjoyed their drinking, and in the end Zhang insisted on paying. Before they parted, Hu gave him a particular reminder: he wanted the bank and A’xiang’s affairs settled during the next few days, before his family reached Shanghai. Once they were reunited after their ordeals, he meant to spend proper time with his aged mother. Even the most pressing business would have to be put aside. Zhang agreed repeatedly. On arriving home, he first consulted his wife about winding up their little grocery. She had some judgement of her own. His account made her both happy and wistful. Going into the bedroom, she opened a chest and took out a packet of soft cotton paper. She unwrapped it to show him a cheap vine bracelet mounted in silver. Her husband was puzzled. What could it have to do with their discussion? But Mrs Zhang wished to tell an old story. “This bracelet is Xueyan’s. It was this that showed me he would prosper. It happened before he met Governor Wang. He had lost his job at the bank and was having a very hard time. One day he told me that a good friend had come from Jinhua to seek work in Hangzhou and fallen ill at an inn. He owed a fortnight’s room and board and still needed a doctor. Without five taels he could not get through. Could I help? Though Xueyan was down in the world, he did not have the air of a beaten man. His cotton gown was faded and patched, but washed and starched as neatly as ever. That showed he had a capable, virtuous wife to support him. For that reason, I lent him five taels without a moment’s hesitation.” “Really!” Zhang was fascinated. “There was such a story, and you never told me! Did he repay you?”

“Do not interrupt. Listen!” Mrs Zhang continued. “Xueyan told me, ‘I am in poor circumstances now. I do not know when I can repay these five taels, but I certainly shall.’ To be honest, since I was willing to lend, I naturally did not expect repayment at once. So I said, ‘Never mind. Repay me when you have it.’ Then he pulled this vine bracelet off his arm and put it in my hand. ‘It is not worth even one tael,’ he said. ‘It cannot count as security. But it belonged to my mother, and I value it greatly. This will remind me not to forget to repay what I owe.’ I refused it, but he absolutely would not take it back, so he left it here.” “That does not sound like Xueyan. He always keeps his word.” “You think that because the bracelet is still here, he never repaid the five taels? Not at all! He paid them back long ago.” “When?” “Soon after his luck turned, when Governor Wang was appointed to Zhejiang.” “Then why do you still have the bracelet?”

“That is what you cannot help admiring about him. He brought a red envelope containing a banknote for five taels, with four kinds of modest gifts besides. When I offered the bracelet, he refused it. Five taels now, he said, were nothing like five taels then. The kindness he owed me had not been repaid. He wanted me to keep the bracelet and come to him if I ever had a difficulty. Only after he had helped me would he accept it back. I thought his mother must be wearing gold and jade by then and would hardly miss a vine bracelet. It seemed of little consequence, so I kept it. When he did you that great favour, I took four gifts to visit him, specially to return it. Again he refused.” “What was his reason?” Zhang was more interested than ever. “I should like to hear what he had to say this time.” “He said he had helped you out of loyalty to a fellow banker. Besides, a man’s business outside the home did not concern his wife. He could not cross off his debt to me against that account. I was to keep the bracelet. One day he would do something to please me, and only then would my kindness be repaid.” “There is sense in that. This is what makes Xueyan such a fine fellow. He plainly does you a favour and makes you feel comfortable about it too. But enough talk. Let us consider how to hand over this grocery.” Zhang frowned. “Small as a sparrow, but with all its organs intact! All the little sums owing to us and owing from us—it will give me days of headaches to sort them out.” “Headaches? Why should it? Everything is in the books. Transfer the premises, the stock and the accounts in one lot. We shall abdicate and surrender our little kingdom, hand everything over and brush ourselves off as we walk away. What could be easier?” Zhang was delighted. “Exactly! You are the decisive one. When Xueyan asks tomorrow how much I want for the shop, I shall say that I started it with sixteen hundred silver dollars and will take sixteen hundred for it.” This accorded perfectly with his wife’s wishes. In those few moments, they decided to part with the business they had built for three or four years, and neither felt any reluctance. Their attachment to the old trade was deeply rooted, after all, and he would be working with Hu Xueyan. Beside that prospect, the little grocery was no chicken rib, too poor to relish but too good to throw away. It was a worn-out shoe, to be discarded without a thought.
