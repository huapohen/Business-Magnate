HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 7 — Part 3 of 4

The three women exchanged glances. Each guessed what had happened. In such a situation the Seventh Lady remained calm and even comforted the maid. “Suxiang, do not be frightened. Tell us slowly.” “Madam has disappeared!” Suxiang said, almost in tears. “No one knows where she has gone.” Asked to speak slowly, she still began in the middle of the story. The Seventh Lady had to question her. “How do you know she has disappeared? When did she leave?” “Not long after Master went out, she sent me to buy silk thread in Xiangfen Lane and sent A’xiang to order rice and firewood. When we came back, she had already gone. No one knew when she had left, not even the man at the front door. Then we looked at the back door and found it half open. By three this afternoon she still had not returned. I went into her room: a small jewelry box was missing, and quite a few changes of clothes. That—that—” Too anxious to explain, Suxiang faltered. There was no need to say it: she must have gone to the elderly abbess. The Seventh Lady drew in a sharp breath and stared at her companions. The Second Miss asked, “Suxiang, does your master know?” “No. A’xiang and the chairmen have gone to look for him.” “Your master is at the bank,” the Seventh Lady said. “See whether any of the chairmen are still here. Send someone to tell him at once. Ask him to come here; I have something important to say.”

At that moment Hu arrived, accompanied by Xiao Jiaji. He knew the Second Miss well but had never met A’jin. There was no time for inquiries; indeed, the presence of a stranger made him all the more determined to remain composed and keep his domestic troubles from becoming public. He merely nodded and asked quietly, “How do you two come to be here as well?” “We happened to meet,” the Seventh Lady replied. “Let us talk inside.” In the sitting room she introduced A’jin. They were approaching matters that Suxiang and A’xiang ought not to hear, so she asked to speak with Hu privately. “I know where A’qiao has gone—to a convent in Fahua Town. We must lose no time; someone must go for her at once. I think—” She hesitated. “It had better be just Sister A’jin and me. You ought not to meet her.” Hu was utterly bewildered. “What has happened? How do you know where she is? And who is this Sister A’jin?” “There is no time to explain everything now. Little Uncle, just arrange for us to get to Fahua.”

“The whole Fahua district is occupied by Anhui Army troops from Anqing. I do not even know whether we can get through.” “That does not matter,” Xiao said. “I shall go.” “Excellent! You are the best person.” The Seventh Lady was delighted. Xiao knew the Anhui Army commanders well, and his presence would make many things easier. “Seventh Sister, I still think I ought to go,” Hu said. “I need not see her, but at least let her know that I am not indifferent. What do you think?” “I was afraid you would quarrel if you met and make matters impossible to resolve. Since you put it that way, Little Uncle, there is no harm in your going.” It was dusk when they reached Fahua Town. Xiao sought out a battalion commander named Zhu, who served under the Anhui Army general Cheng Xueqi. A frank and helpful man, Zhu heard their errand, gave them a meal, then ordered a subordinate officer, a bazong, to fetch the local constable, Old Hu, and explain what was needed.

“Certainly, certainly. I shall show the way,” Old Hu said. “The three of you, please follow me.” They headed east in the moonlight and soon turned into a lane. At its far end stood a residence behind a whitewashed wall, with luxuriant flowers and trees inside. In the faint light of the new moon, the shapes were indistinct. A warm breeze carried out the rich scent of night-blooming flowers. Everyone felt suddenly refreshed, yet for no reason their hearts grew restless, filled with a swelling emotion they could not name. Xiao felt it most strongly. “What is this place?” he could not help asking. “Here? White-Robed Convent. At night we use the side entrance.” The side entrance had a solid wooden door. Above it, within reach, was an inconspicuous flattened iron ring. Old Hu reached up and pulled it twice. Clang, clang. After a little while came footsteps; the door opened a crack, and someone asked, “Who is it?” “Xiaoyin, it is me.” “Oh. Old Hu, what brings you here at this hour?” “Can you not see the visitors?” He pointed behind him. “Tell Sister Liaochen that I have brought them.”

The door swung open with a creak. By the light they saw Xiaoyin, a girl in ordinary secular dress with her hair hanging loose. Once the visitors were inside, she shut the door and led them forward without a word, along a flower-lined path and through two covered passages to a monastic room apparently used for receiving guests. There she stopped and looked at Old Hu. He hesitated a little, then bowed to the officer. “Sir, shall I keep you company here for a while?” There was scarcely any need to ask; the officer naturally agreed. Old Hu whispered a few words to Xiaoyin, then motioned Hu to follow her. Beyond the room lay a large courtyard. As they turned into the western covered walk, their shadows mingled with those of the flowers upon the whitewashed wall. There was also the shadow of a cat, arching its back and yowling for a mate on the roof to the east. Xiao nudged Hu gently with his elbow and murmured a couplet: “A winding path leads into seclusion; the monks’ quarters lie deep among flowers and trees.” Hu too could see that something about this convent was unusual. But Xiao’s behavior verged on frivolity and was scarcely respectful in a Buddhist sanctuary. He coughed to warn him to restrain himself. They followed Xiaoyin silently into another courtyard, planted with trees and facing an elegant three-bay retreat. Sandalwood incense mingled with the scent of flowers. Xiao exclaimed involuntarily, “What a refined place!” “Please sit inside,” Xiaoyin said, lifting the curtain to admit them. “I shall fetch Sister Liaochen.” Then she went off on her own.

Entering, the two men saw a white porcelain Guanyin enshrined at the upper end of the room. To the east stood a row of tables and chairs of unpainted cypress; to the west, an enormous wooden couch covered with padded Sichuan brocade. Flowers glowed in vases, incense curled from a burner, and an ancient qin completed an exceptionally tasteful arrangement. Yet none of these things caught Xiao’s attention as much as the horizontal scroll hanging above the couch.

“Mr Hu!” Xiao sounded excited. “Look!” The scroll contained three poems. Hu could at least make out where the lines began and ended: “At leisure I knock at the Buddhist gate to visit a moonlike beauty; / Pine and creepers cover the ritual altar and the garden of herbs. / A courtyard of osmanthus flowers falls to greet the visitor; / Books and paintings fill the walls, rich offerings to the Buddha. / In writing rhapsodies I must blush before Song Yu; / With a flower in your hand, you should be Vimalakirti’s companion. / Coming here, my worldly thoughts all melt away; / Yet savoring our former ties, I find them all temptation.”

“Old legend tells of Chang’e’s flight up to the moon; / Now I knock at a cloudlike cell enclosed in clinging vines. / Your gifts need yield nothing to Xuanji’s; / Your romantic ardor seems greater even than Sun Chuo’s. / Who has attained even half a lay disciple’s practice? / We wait to grasp the Three Vehicles and the Agamas. / When shall we share a Buddhist vegetarian feast, / And in this cool, pure world dispel the demon of verse?”

“On the register of flowers are many traces of smiles; / Today I visit this cloudlike cell in our native place. / Cherishing talent, you have left fine lines behind; / I once beat time in praise as I heard you sing aloud. / Cool shade finds distant shelter in the holy mountain’s bamboo; / Lush beauty trails low among a thatched hut’s vines. / Chrysanthemums beneath the hedge adorn the autumn light; / All wandering thoughts I give to the demon of meditation.”

In literary matters Hu knew even less than Xiao. He had no idea who Song Yu and Sun Chuo were, nor that Xuanji meant Yu Xuanji, the Tang dynasty Taoist nun. The allusions to Buddhist scriptures were wholly beyond him. Yet he could tell that the tone of the poems was hardly one in which to address a nun who kept strict vows. Astonished, he asked, “What is all this?” “Wait and see,” Xiao answered softly. “This Sister Liaochen must come from Jiaxing or Kunshan. If neither, then Zhenze or Shengze.” Hu knew nothing of what distinguished the nuns of Kunshan, but he had personal experience of the convents of Jiaxing. When staying at Tongli in Wujiang, he had also heard about the customs of Zhenze and Shengze. Both were prosperous silk-producing towns, luxurious in their habits. In Shengze it was fashionable to entertain guests at convents, where a vegetarian banquet cost more than a feast featuring bird’s nest. The dishes were said to be flavored with stock made from plump chickens and the finest ham, which accounted for their extraordinary savor. The convents of Zhenze were equally famous for their cooking: meat and vegetarian dishes alike rivaled the celebrated boat cuisine of Wuxi. Besides the delicacies on the table, of course, there were beauties to feast one’s eyes upon. Remembering all this, Hu thought back to Old Hu’s assurance when they first met that there was “no inconvenience” in visiting White-Robed Convent at night. Everything from that moment to the scene before him seemed to confirm Xiao’s view. His curiosity rose, and he longed to see what Liaochen looked like. Xiao, however, had grown more sober than he. “Mr Hu, I am afraid there has been a mistake. A’qiao cannot be here.”

“Why do you say that?” “Is this a place for shaving one’s head and practicing religion?” Hu was about to reply when he glimpsed a white gauze lantern floating toward them beyond the glass window. He fell silent and rose to wait. The curtain lifted; first came Xiaoyin, then Liaochen. Had they not known beforehand, they would never have believed her to be a nun. Her appearance was not entirely secular, certainly. She wore neither an ordinary woman’s three-part coiffure nor separate upper and lower garments. Her hair reached her shoulders, and she had on a long, round-necked robe. One might have called it a nun’s robe, but a nun’s robe would hardly be made of such glossy soft black satin, still less be cut so closely to the body, with a narrow waist and slender sleeves. Her face looked less monastic still. Though she wore no powder, she had applied rouge; her naturally fair skin needed no powder. Above all there were her eyes: at first glance clear as water, but at a second revealing an undertone of amorous feeling.

With such a person, excessive solemnity and reserve seemed unnecessary. Smiling, Hu brought his palms together in greeting. “Are you Sister Liaochen?” “I am Liaochen. May I ask your surname, benefactor?” “Mine is Hu. This gentleman is Mr Xiao.” Liaochen greeted them in turn and invited her “benefactors” to sit. She herself sat cross-legged on the wooden couch and asked what had brought them. “We originally came to see the elderly abbess,” Hu said. “But Old Hu, the constable, tells us that you are really in charge here. There is a small matter we wish to ask about. Perhaps my young friend Xiao had better explain.” Xiao nodded. Instead of coming to the point, however, he first asked, “Judging by your accent, Sister Liaochen, are you from Zhenze?” She blushed. “Yes.”

“These three poems,” Xiao said, pointing above her, “are very fine.” “They were written by three benefactors in moments of poetic inspiration. Such wild nonsense! What can one do with them?” Liaochen smiled faintly. “Have you lived in Zhenze, Mr Xiao?” “Yes, for more than a year. But I was only a child then and understood nothing.” “Meaning that you understand everything now?” So direct a question had something challenging about it. Xiao was no weak opponent and had no difficulty answering with composure. “Not everything, even now. I shall return another day for instruction. Today there is something in which I must ask your help.” “You put it too strongly. Please tell me what you wish. I only fear there may be little I can do.” “If you are willing, a word is all it will take. Has a lady come to White-Robed Convent today to ask the elderly abbess for refuge? She has had domestic difficulties and, in a moment of distress, could see no way out. She may have asked the abbess to conceal her whereabouts. If so, she has done your convent a disservice.” Liaochen’s expression changed; she looked frightened. She glanced from one man to the other, then finally nodded. “Yes, someone has come. Is she perhaps Mr Hu’s lady?”

So she was here after all. Once the news they had sought so eagerly was confirmed, the two men found themselves at a loss. Xiao and Hu looked at one another in silence, their glances asking the same question: what had A’qiao meant by coming to this convent? If she sought religious discipline, Xiao’s question was justified: “Is this a place for shaving one’s head and practicing religion?” If she did not seek that, she must be choosing the other path and becoming another Liaochen. Until they understood which it was, they could decide nothing; yet they did not know how to begin finding out. At last Hu made a decision. “Sister Liaochen, I should like my young friend Xiao to see my lady first. Would that be possible?” “Why not? That would be best. But I must ask her first.” Since Liaochen approved of Xiao’s seeing A’qiao, they could guess that “asking her” really meant persuading her. Provided Liaochen would help, the meeting was assured. Hu and Xiao said no more and were content to wait. As soon as she had gone, Xiao asked, “Mr Hu, what shall I say when I see A’qiao?”

“What puzzles me,” Hu said, without answering, “is whether that woman A’jin knows nothing of the kind of place this is.” “There is no time to investigate that now. Just tell me what I ought to do when I see A’qiao.” “You need say nothing else. Only ask what she means to do. Once you know, we can go back and discuss it with your master’s wife.” Xiao met A’qiao in the courtyard where the elderly abbess lived in quiet retirement. Its furnishings were less fine than those in Liaochen’s rooms, but still more elegant than those of other convents. Plainly White-Robed Convent was prosperous. Either it owned extensive property from which it received regular rents, or its income from devotees’ offerings was substantial.

A’qiao looked worn and haggard, and her eyes reddened at the sight of Xiao. After greeting her, he came straight to the point. “A’qiao, whatever made you come to this place?” “I have wanted to come for a long time. Life has no savor. I might as well cultivate merit for the next.” So she had intended to become a nun. Xiao asked, “Have you been here before?” “No.” Afraid of listeners beyond the wall, he could not speak openly. After a moment’s thought he remembered Hu’s question. “What about A’jin? Has she been here?” He meant that, if A’jin had visited, she must know what went on here: had she not told A’qiao? A’qiao shook her head. “No, she has not either.” “That explains it.” He could say no more. A’qiao seemed to understand. She gave a long, soundless sigh, as though she too had much that she could not freely express.

“What now?” Xiao asked. “You must have some plan.” “I—” A’qiao paused. “I shall stay here for the present and decide in time.” “Very well. My master’s wife will come to see you tomorrow.” “No!” A’qiao answered decisively. “Please tell her not to come.” That was extraordinary. She would receive a male visitor with whom she had no particularly close connection, yet refused to see the Seventh Lady, who had long been her friend. Such an emphatic refusal must have a reason. Xiao thought quickly, widely, and deeply; in matters of this kind he was especially cautious. After considering, he said, “A’qiao, I know you have always been very close to my master’s wife. Am I to repeat exactly what you have said?” “Why should you not?”

“Then if she asks why you do not want her to come, what am I to tell her?” At this question a look of resentment came over A’qiao’s face. “I have broken with all my worldly relations and friends. That is why I do not want her here. If she comes, I shall refuse to see her.” Her voice was more determined than ever; coupled with her expression, it suggested an irreconcilable hatred of the Seventh Lady. Xiao was deeply alarmed, but spoke all the more cautiously. “A’qiao,” he asked indirectly, probing for the truth, “am I not a person of the world too?” This exposed a flaw in her argument that she could not explain away. Her expression changed back and forth for a long time before she answered. “Young Master Xiao, to tell the truth, I am afraid of your master’s wife. Her methods are formidable; I am no match for her. And to tell you another truth, this is precisely what has made life seem so empty and disheartening. I thought she was a friend who gave her heart for mine. Who could have known she was two-faced, helping others scheme against me? My heart has turned completely cold. You must find this strange; you must refuse to believe it. But think: I am a woman in my thirties and have seen every sort of thing in the world. Surely I am not so foolish that I cannot tell a good person from a bad one, or would accuse your master’s wife without reason. She is really—” A’qiao shook her head and would say no more. Xiao was shaken to the core. He could neither understand nor accept so harsh a judgment of the Seventh Lady. For a long while he sat stunned. At last he said, “A’qiao, what is this all about? I truly cannot understand. Please tell me. If my master’s wife is in the wrong, those of us of the younger generation would naturally not presume to criticize her. But we know right from wrong in our hearts.” “Young Master Xiao, if you will swear before the Bodhisattva to keep everything I say to yourself, I shall tell you.”
