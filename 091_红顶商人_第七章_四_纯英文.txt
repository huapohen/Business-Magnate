HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 7 — Part 4 of 4

“You mean I must not tell my master or his wife what you say?” “Exactly.” “Very well, I swear it: if I tell either of them what A’qiao tells me, may Heaven strike me dead with lightning.” A’qiao nodded, satisfied. “Your master’s wife really does play both the spirit-medium and the ghost—” Beginning with this harsh judgment, she recounted in detail the several occasions on which the Seventh Lady had advised her, interweaving the conversations with comments of her own. In her view, the Seventh Lady had deliberately set out to break her bond with Hu. Her advice that A’qiao swallow her pride, enter the household, make the proper obeisances, and formally become Hu’s concubine only appeared well meant. It was false kindness. Knowing perfectly well that A’qiao would never agree, she could safely urge it upon her and thereby force her to ask to leave.

As for Hu, the Seventh Lady had repeatedly called him “slippery,” a man with “no constancy, who falls in love with every woman he sees.” It sounded like abuse of Hu, but in fact it helped him. “Think, Young Master Xiao. Your master’s wife is forever saying ‘Little Uncle’ this and ‘Little Uncle’ that. She reveres him as though he were a bodhisattva. Even if he really were slippery and inconstant, how could she speak of him that way?” A’qiao had grown agitated. “At first I thought she was naturally outspoken and was truly indignant on my behalf, so she said whatever came into her head. Later, the more I thought, the less it seemed right. I went over everything again and again until I understood. All she meant was that Hu was such a scoundrel that he was not worth staying with!” Her accusations against the Seventh Lady were not without reason. Yet the more reasonable they seemed, the more painful Xiao found them. He regarded his master’s wife as a young brother regards an elder sister, with both respect and affection. For years he had cherished a perfect image of her as forthright, honest, warmhearted, and generous. Now he seemed to see a crack in it. How could that not hurt? He could not utter a word. Neither comforting A’qiao nor defending the Seventh Lady seemed quite appropriate; besides, he was too dejected to feel like saying anything at all.

The sight of Xiao’s face gave Hu a shock. He looked as though he had been ill. What could one meeting with A’qiao have done to leave him so visibly shaken? Hu was troubled and bewildered, yet could hardly question him closely here. He merely asked, “Have you seen her?” “Yes. Let us thank Sister Liaochen and take our leave.” Liaochen had recovered her composure. She too said nothing of A’qiao, only warmly invited the two men to return and “share in the religious joys” of the place. Why should men be invited to enjoy religious visits to a convent? The words had an odd sound, but there was no time to consider what lay beneath them.

Hu, however, never neglected the niceties of social dealings. Remembering something he ought to do, he asked at once, “Where is the book of donations?” “There is no need for such ceremony.” He had already spotted the yellow-covered book hanging on the wall. Taking it down, he handed it to Xiao. “Please enter a hundred taels for me.” “That is too much!” Liaochen interposed. “If you mean to give so much because your lady is staying here, there is no need. Those who have left the world receive support from all quarters and offer support to all quarters. You need not concern yourself.” “Those are separate matters,” Xiao replied on Hu’s behalf, going beyond his own role. “Each of us does what is in his heart.” He used the brush and ink already laid out to make the entry. Hu put a banknote for a hundred taels inside the book, placed it on the table, and took his leave. They returned to the camp to thank Commander Zhu, then rode back to Shanghai under the same escort. They traveled too fast for conversation. Only when they reached the busy streets did Xiao rein in his horse. “Mr Hu, let us go to your house and talk at length.”

They dismissed the mounted orderly and went together to the house Hu had shared with A’qiao. Her powder box still held its fragrance; the bright mirror stood as before. Yet the rooms had taken on a desolate air. Hu chose another room, shutting himself in his study with Xiao for a private conversation. When Xiao repeated A’qiao’s indignant words, Hu understood why he had looked so distressed. He too was deeply troubled. In all the time he had known the Seventh Lady, he had never heard anyone criticize her so harshly. Now, because of him, A’qiao had branded her a treacherous schemer. He felt he had done the Seventh Lady a great wrong. “Mr Hu,” Xiao asked, voicing a question that had occupied him all the way back, “has my master’s wife really been deliberately maneuvering things as A’qiao says?” “Yes.” Hu nodded. “She is too eager to help. Most of what A’qiao says is true. She has misunderstood only one point: your master’s wife is doing it entirely for her sake.” He went on to explain. The Seventh Lady was sincerely considering A’qiao’s lifelong welfare. Since she would not become a concubine, she would do better to part from him and choose another man. Hu did not know that the Seventh Lady intended to match her with Dr Zhang, but he believed that, with her warm concern for others, she would certainly find A’qiao a suitable home.

Xiao could accept this explanation completely. Indeed, it was precisely what he had hoped for: an explanation that would clear the Seventh Lady of the charge against her. His manner changed at once. He became cheerful and relieved, as though a heavy burden had slipped from his shoulders. “That is what I thought! How could my master’s wife do such a thing? If she heard how deeply A’qiao misunderstood her, there is no knowing how angry she would be.” “Exactly!” Hu suddenly took alarm. “You must on no account tell her what A’qiao said.” “But if we say nothing, how are we to explain?” They discussed how to put the Seventh Lady off. If they said they had not found A’qiao, she would ask A’jin to look again. If they said A’qiao had shaved her head and absolutely refused to return, she would still persist and go to White-Robed Convent herself, only to be rebuffed. However much they thought, they could find no satisfactory answer.

Leaving that difficulty aside, Xiao asked, “Mr Hu, what do you mean to do about A’qiao?” That too was hard for Hu to answer. He turned it over in his mind for a long time, then sighed. “I can only resign myself to being cursed.” “You mean you have decided to give her up?” “What else can I do?” “Then leave the matter entirely alone,” Xiao said. “If you are to harden your heart, harden it all the way.” “It is a matter of my own conscience,” Hu replied. “Ignoring her altogether hardly seems right.” “What would be right, then? There is no way to make A’qiao agree willingly to the separation.”

“I do not ask for willing agreement. I only hope she can swallow the bitterness.” Hu made his decision. “This is what I propose—” He would first gain time and keep the Seventh Lady quiet by saying that the abbess of White-Robed Convent had sent A’qiao to another place far away, and that someone was already following to persuade her to return. At the same time, he would ask the Second Miss to enlist A’jin’s help. First, A’jin must support the story and never reveal the truth to the Seventh Lady. Second, she must see A’qiao and deliver a message: whatever A’qiao wished to do—take the tonsure, marry respectably, or even follow in Liaochen’s footsteps—Hu would not interfere. He was also ready to give her a large sum of money. Once he had explained the plan, Hu too felt relieved. After so many days of entanglement, he had at last found a way to cut through it. Xiao did not consider it a good solution, but could think of no better one. Besides, it was not his own affair; however much he wished to help, there was little he could do. He could only nod his agreement. “We must act at once,” Hu said. “Your master’s wife is still waiting for news. Let us do what needs doing. I must trouble you with more work tonight.” “Your business is as much my concern as my master’s.” Xiao thought for a moment. “Let us see the Second Miss first.”

At the Second Miss’s establishment, lamps glowed red, wine flowed, and the night was still young. But she was the proprietress and had a separate little house of her own. Fortunately it was nearby; an attendant led them there in a few moments. As they entered, they heard the clock in the sitting room strike twelve. The Second Miss had not gone to sleep, though she had retired upstairs to her bedroom. When her maid announced the visitors, she invited them up. With the Dragon Boat Festival approaching, the weather had grown close and hot. She received them in an informal lined silk jacket whose broad, short sleeves exposed her white forearms. On one arm she wore a gold bracelet, on the other a jade bangle. Her full face and abundant, carefully dressed hair gave her a look of prosperity and good fortune. The sight stirred Xiao again: by comparison, A’qiao seemed all the more worn and pitiable. Since this was the two men’s first visit to her house, the Second Miss had to observe the usual courtesies. She poured tea, set out dishes of fruit, and even offered to light the opium lamp and let them recline for a while. Before such hospitality, the guests too had to make a show of being at leisure. They began with irrelevant conversation before turning to their purpose.

Xiao had barely said, “A’qiao was at White-Robed Convent after all,” when the maid came in with a tray. Over a little wine and a late supper, he continued his account of the visit. When he finished, Hu asked the Second Miss to act as intermediary. She had listened without a word and did not answer at once. The whole affair was so strange that she could scarcely make sense of it. For a while she blinked in thought, then shook her head. “Mr Hu, I do not think that is the way to handle matters. We cannot do without the Seventh Lady in this.” She told him about Dr Zhang and argued that the Seventh Lady’s course was the right one. A’qiao’s misunderstanding could surely be explained away. What they must do now was persuade her to return, clear up the misunderstanding, dispel her resentment, and arrange her marriage into the Zhang household. If everyone worked together patiently, there was every prospect of a happy conclusion. “There is no need to involve A’jin, and you certainly must not send a message that closes every door. A’qiao’s thoughts have taken a wrong turning; we must patiently guide them back. The Seventh Lady may be hasty, but no one is more considerate or more willing to think of everyone’s interests. She will forgive A’qiao’s misunderstanding and put up with some unfairness. We can explain matters to her openly; there is no reason to let her go to the convent and be rebuffed. As I see it, Mr Hu—” She deliberately stopped, hoping that Hu would understand and volunteer the necessary offer. But his mind was in turmoil and he had no patience for subtlety. “What do you mean, Second Sister?”

“I mean that you should put your pride aside a little. Go to White-Robed Convent again yourself tomorrow, smile, say a few kind words, and first persuade A’qiao to return. We can consider the rest afterward.” Hu could not agree. This repeated and endless entanglement had forced him to neglect a great deal of important business; he was thoroughly weary of it. Now that he had finally found a way to cut himself free, he was to be prevented from using it. Instead, he must smile at A’qiao and speak ingratiatingly. He resented that; worse, he feared she would think he had changed his mind and could not do without her. She would cling to him still more tightly. Would that not merely invite further trouble? Seeing his reluctance, the Second Miss grew anxious. “Mr Hu, in every other kind of judgment I am far below gentlemen in official life such as yourself. But on this matter I am confident. Why? Because only a woman understands a woman’s thoughts. Besides, A’qiao and I lived together for more than a year. Naturally I know her disposition. I am giving you good advice. If you refuse it, you will regret it.” Hu already suspected the Second Miss of being partial to A’qiao, and her words only strengthened his prejudice. He made no commitment, merely adopting a thoughtful expression to indicate his disagreement.

As an onlooker, Xiao could see more clearly. The Second Miss had spoken rather bluntly, but her approach was sound. Yet he also understood Hu’s state of mind: pressing him now would only make things worse. Fortunately, they knew where A’qiao was, and there was no harm in her remaining at the convent a little longer. To avoid a deadlock, they must follow the maxim that matters settle more smoothly when given time. “Mr Hu has his own difficulties. But your intention is right.” Xiao emphasized the words and gave the Second Miss a meaningful look. “Let us take things slowly. Sooner or later we shall find a way through.” “Very well. Please try to persuade him, Young Master Xiao.” “I will, I will,” Xiao promised repeatedly. “I shall bring you an answer tomorrow. It is late now. We should be going.”

They took their leave. Hu walked unsteadily, utterly exhausted in body and mind. Xiao could hardly add anything; he only asked, “Where will you sleep tonight, Mr Hu? I shall see you there.” “At the bank. Will you still go to your master’s wife tonight?” “There is no need now. It is so late.” “Very well.” Hu pondered a moment, then frowned and shook his head, looking unbearably harassed. “Let us leave it for a day or two. My mind has truly gone dull. I have never known such a tangle of trouble, with everyone pulling at everyone else and nothing ever settled.” “Then,” Xiao asked in a subdued voice, almost as though he himself had caused the trouble and was begging for advice, “what should I say when I see my master’s wife tomorrow?” This time Hu answered promptly and decisively. “Say anything you like, provided you do not hurt her.” Back at the bank, his vexation kept him tossing in bed. Only as the sounds of the city began to rise did he drift into an uneasy sleep.

He was deep in a dream when someone suddenly shook him awake. Opening his heavy, sore eyes, he saw Xiao standing beside the bed with a broad smile. “Mr Hu, your family has arrived!” All sleep vanished. Hu sprang up, throwing back the quilt. “Where are they?” “They went first to my master’s wife. She checked the almanac and found that today is an auspicious day for moving into a house, so they decided to go straight to the new home. She sent me to tell you.” Mother and son, husband and wife, father and daughter were reunited as though after a lifetime apart. Everyone in the Hu household sobbed, and the Seventh Lady wept beside them. At last, one by one, they stopped crying and began to describe what had happened since their separation. At every painful recollection the tears started again. Thus they talked and wept, wept and talked; not until the third day did Hu and his mother begin to regain their composure. During those three days, the busiest person was naturally the Seventh Lady. Newly arrived in Shanghai, the Hu family found everything unfamiliar and depended upon her guidance and care at every turn.

Yet whenever there was a quiet moment, she thought of A’qiao: a woman forsaken in middle age, taking refuge in a convent. Putting herself in A’qiao’s place, she could see little joy left in life. From time to time an alarming thought struck her: what if A’qiao took her own life? Her anxiety increased each day until she had to consult Liu Bucai. “Do not worry,” he replied. “I shall go there with Xiao Jiaji, and we shall see how matters stand.” He found Xiao, and they set out for White-Robed Convent along the now familiar route. When they knocked at its gate, Xiaoyin again answered. “Oh, Mr Xiao!” She still recognized him. “A’qiao has gone to Ningbo.” The news was entirely unexpected. “What has she gone to Ningbo for?” Xiao asked. “My teacher will tell you. She said you would certainly come again, and she was right. Please come in.” She conducted them to the elegant retreat Xiao had visited before. They had scarcely sat long enough to drink a cup of tea when Liaochen appeared with a graceful step. Liu’s eyes brightened, and he rose involuntarily with a smile.

“Sister Liaochen,” Xiao said by way of introduction, “this is Mr Liu, a senior relation of the Hu family.” “Ah, please sit. I suppose you have come to persuade A’qiao to return?” “Yes. The young sister tells us she has gone to Ningbo. Is that true?” “She left the day before yesterday—to seek a settled home.” Xiao was delighted and surprised. “Then I presume you know all about A’qiao’s life?” “I do. It was precisely because I knew her story that I urged her to go to Ningbo.” “So it was your boundless spiritual power that persuaded her to turn back!” Liu brought his palms together, closed his eyes, and murmured, “A great deed of merit, a great deed of merit.” He looked rather comical. Liaochen could not help smiling behind pursed lips and seemed to find him distinctly interesting.

“It is indeed a great deed of merit,” Xiao said. “May we hear the instruction with which you persuaded her?” “I moved her simply by speaking of causes and affinities. I told her that her bond with Mr Hu had run its course and must not be forced. Having sown that cause in the beginning, she must now reap this result. With Dr Zhang, however, she has sown a new cause, and that too must bear fruit. If it does not ripen in this life, it will do so in the next. Our passage through worldly lives is nothing but this endless chain of cause and effect. Better to complete this bond in the present life; with no old karmic debt carried into the next, she need not suffer. That is the course of a person of great wisdom and complete understanding.” Liaochen continued, “We talked from facing couches in my quiet retreat. For three whole days I reasoned with her, until at last she woke to the truth.” “Wonderful! Wonderful!” Liu exclaimed. “The sea of suffering is boundless, but turn your head and there is the shore. Only when one person of great wisdom meets another can such perfect merit be achieved.” “Mr Liu truly has the gift of unhindered eloquence.” Liaochen smiled, cast him a glance, then lowered her head and smiled faintly again for no apparent reason.

“You praise me too highly, Sister Liaochen. Still, I have dipped into the Buddhist scriptures myself. One day I must seek proper instruction from you.” “If you truly desire goodness and are sincere in your devotion, come whenever you wish.” “I certainly shall, certainly!” Liu looked about him with open admiration. “To discuss meditation with you on facing couches in such a blessed retreat—who knows how many lifetimes of merit it would take to earn such pure enjoyment?” Liaochen answered with another reserved smile. Afraid Liu would go on indefinitely, Xiao quickly intervened. “May I ask, Sister Liaochen: will A’qiao return?” “No.” “Then what of her luggage? Has she taken it all to Ningbo?” “No. She went ahead alone. Dr Zhang will send someone for it later.” “When his man comes, could you ask him to be sure to call at Fukang Bank?” “There is no need,” Liaochen replied. “Once it is ended, let everything end. Please tell Mr Hu when you return that the bond has run its course. He need not go on seeking troubles for himself.” “Excellent! Excellent!” Liu intoned loudly. “‘To rid yourself of trouble, let the self be gone; each has his own affinities—envy no one else.’”

Xiao could not help growing angry. Liu was simply making mischief. In a fit of annoyance he abandoned his remaining questions and stood. “Thank you, Sister Liaochen. We shall take our leave.” Liu still seemed reluctant to go, but Xiao allowed no discussion and pulled him away. Back at home, he gave a full account of their visit. Gu Yingchun and his wife were delighted beyond expectation, though the Seventh Lady remained somewhat dissatisfied. “You ought to have asked for more details,” she complained mildly. This touched Xiao’s own grievance exactly. “How could I get a word in?” he asked, pointing at Liu. “Third Master Liu was busy making eyes and flirting with Liaochen!” He proceeded to imitate Liu’s words and gestures. Gu and the Seventh Lady burst out laughing; she laughed until tears came. Liu waited for them to stop. “May I speak now?” “Speak, speak!” the Seventh Lady said, still laughing. “Go ahead, Third Uncle Liu.” “Jiaji cannot keep his patience. What is there to be so anxious about? Tomorrow I shall go and ‘discuss meditation’ with Liaochen. What is there that I cannot ask her then?”

“Exactly! Third Uncle Liu, please do ask her—the more detail the better.” Gu said nothing at the time, but afterward spoke privately to Liu. “Your words were right: ‘To rid yourself of trouble, let the self be gone; each has his own affinities—envy no one else.’ For Little Uncle’s unhappy bond with A’qiao to end in this way is an excellent outcome. There is no need to stir matters up again. And let me give you a word of advice, Third Uncle: do not go there to discuss any meditation.” “I was only joking.”
