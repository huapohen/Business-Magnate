HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 8 — Part 3 of 9

Jiang Yili thoroughly agreed. The special mission to bring Hu back therefore fell to young Zhang. On reaching Shanghai, he settled at the Changfa Inn, a lodging favoured by travelling officials. He then found the address Liu had given him and sent a junior attendant from the inn to fetch him. “I reckoned you must be due, and here you are.” Without wasting words, Liu said exactly what Zhang wanted to hear. “I shall take you to see my relative first. Once your business is settled, you can enjoy yourself to your heart’s content.” “Old Liu, I am now a member of the Zhejiang Reconstruction Bureau, with the seventh rank. Provincial Treasurer Jiang has specially sent me to invite His Honour Hu back to Hangzhou. That is all I have to say.” “Good. I understand. Let us go at once.” Zhang took out his seventh-rank official robes and changed in front of his visitor. Once dressed, he looked embarrassed, feeling rather like a monkey in an official hat. Liu did not laugh. “Have your servant bring a change of clothes,” he said. “That will save us returning here when you want to dress informally.” Zhang’s attendant, Zhang Sheng, was accustomed to serving officials. He already had the visiting cards and clothes bundle ready. The three took a carriage straight to Fukang Bank.

Hu was at a crucial stage in negotiations with several rice merchants. But Zhang had come from a distance and was calling in official dress. Hu had to apologize to his earlier visitors and leave them to greet the newcomer. Zhang had seen him before. As soon as Hu entered the small reception room, without waiting for an introduction, he cried, “Uncle Hu!” and respectfully knelt to kowtow. “You are too kind! My young friend, you are much too ceremonious.” Hu hastily knelt as well. After exchanging bows and helping one another up, they made the usual inquiries after the family, then turned to business. When Zhang had explained his mission, Hu replied, “It is a duty I cannot refuse. I am already making preparations. Enjoy a few days in Shanghai, and we shall travel together.” “Yes, sir.” “Good!” Liu interposed. “The business is settled. Change your clothes, and we can go.”

Liu took Zhang sightseeing in the dazzling foreign settlement. In the evening they ate a Western dinner and watched Peking opera. Zhang’s eyes were opened to an entirely new world. Though tired late at night, he remained excited. Liu stayed with him at the Changfa Inn, and they talked beside their beds until nearly dawn before falling asleep. Hu was still awake. He needed to ship ten thousand shi of rice to Hangzhou. Several merchants had agreed to supply it, only to change their minds at the last moment. He had no choice but to seek out You Wu. Finding him only in the middle of the night, he stated his need directly. You knew the rice trade, but the rice firm collectively run by the Grain-Boat Guild in Songjiang had long since closed. He had been out of touch for some time, and such a vast quantity could not be produced at a word. Over the past few years he had kept out of sight, guarded his speech, and grown increasingly cautious. He dared not promise what he could not guarantee. “Little Uncle, naturally I would not refuse an instruction from you. But you know my circumstances. To buy rice now, I should first have to find the people. Without my old team ready to hand, I cannot be certain of the time. When you could not get your rice into Hangzhou and diverted it to Ningbo, did they not promise to replace the full quantity whenever you needed it?”

Yang Fang had indeed made that agreement with Hu to obtain relief for his native place. But Yang’s position had changed. Implicated in the affair in which Burgevine seized military funds and assaulted an official, he had fallen badly from Li Hongzhang’s favour. His suspension and investigation had not yet been lifted. He had neither the inclination nor the means to fulfil his promise. Hu would not take advantage of a man in distress and had decided to find his own solution. After hearing the explanation, You said admiringly, “Little Uncle, you truly stand by your friends. But Yang is not like you. He made a considerable fortune out of the Ever-Victorious Army, which is also why Governor Li will not let him off. Even if you do not require replacement rice, he should repay the money. It cost a little over two taels a shi then. Now it is nearly six, and hard to buy even at that price. Surely you do not mean to settle with him at the old rate?” “Certainly not. He must pay me at the current market price. Otherwise I shall proceed against him through official channels and see whether he can bear it.”

“Then the money need not worry us,” You said, nodding. “But, Little Uncle, finding ten thousand shi will be extremely difficult. Jiangsu has been short of grain these past two years and has depended on shipments from Huguang and Jiangxi. Last year, when Governor Li bought rice to send to the capital, he had to purchase foreign rice as well. Thirty thousand men worked for two months before the quantity was complete. I fear you cannot obtain ten thousand shi within a month now.” “It is not a month. The month must include delivery to Hangzhou. The purchase must be complete in twenty days at most.” “That is harder still. I doubt even the authorities could manage it.” “If we can do what the authorities cannot, that will show our ability.” The remark put You’s capabilities to the test. Having spoken of difficulty twice, he could hardly do so a third time. He remained silent, considering every possibility at length, before sighing. “We can only all put our heads to it.”

They went off in different directions to seek help. In the end it was the Seventh Lady who found the proprietress of Dafeng Rice Firm, known as the Fair-faced Tigress. She combined rice due for delivery to the capital with two thousand shi of foreign rice held at Jardine Matheson, making eighty-five hundred shi for Hu. You found the remainder, quickly bringing the total to ten thousand. Negotiations with Yang yielded fifty thousand taels towards the purchase price. Hu temporarily made up the shortfall from the hundred and twenty thousand he was raising to return to Wang’s surviving family. Everything went smoothly. Within thirteen days, the sea junks had spread their sails. Following the same route as the first expedition, they sailed by way of Haining and the Qiantang River to the waters outside Hangzhou’s Wangjiang Gate.

Zhang went ahead to Hangzhou. At Hu’s suggestion, he said only that a few hundred shi of rice were to be donated to the authorities. With a handsome gift he then cultivated Battalion Officer Zhang, who commanded at Wangjiang Gate, and arranged how they were to be received. He came out in a small boat to meet them and report. His detailed account of conditions in Hangzhou was far from encouraging. Hu listened, depressed, and remained silent for a long time. As a close relative and an elder, Liu could speak to him with comparative freedom. “Xueyan, are you afraid someone is plotting against you?” “Do you mean someone might accuse me to Governor-General Zuo? That is nothing. They cannot harm me that way.” “What troubles you, then?” “How can I be easy? Great difficulties lie ahead, and danger is before our eyes.” The polished phrasing was unlike Hu’s usual speech, making both men more curious. Liu understood him better than Zhang did. He knew what the future difficulties meant: Hu had often spoken of the countless tasks of reconstruction after recovery—caring for the dead, relieving the living, and restoring commerce. But what danger threatened them now? “I never imagined the government troops’ discipline would be little better than the rebels’,” Hu said. “What young Zhang has just told us about the city gives real cause for anxiety. Daylight may be safe enough. But once night falls, I fear there will be robbery, arson, rape, and looting.” No wonder he was so troubled. Whether or not he exaggerated, precautions were plainly necessary. Zhang, whose home was in the city, was especially alarmed. “Mr Hu! What can we do?”

“There is one way. But it will be of use only if I can see the man in charge.” Jiang Yili now controlled the whole city. Zhang thought for a moment. “May I ask your wishes, Mr Hu? When we enter, shall we see my father first, or go directly to the man in charge?” “The man in charge, naturally.” “Very well.” Zhang could be decisive too. “Old Liu, let us divide the work. Once we land, please help Battalion Officer Zhang with the unloading. Everything is disordered just now. By helping, I mean directing the labourers. There is no need for you to concern yourself with anything else.” Liu understood the implication. He was not to assume responsibility for guarding the grain. If stragglers or wandering soldiers tried to extort it by force or persuasion, he could leave the matter to the officer. “I understand. Let us agree where to meet afterwards.” “At my house,” Zhang replied. “I shall ask the officer to have you escorted there.” Hu opened a small chest containing a serviceable suit of official robes and the hat insignia of the third rank. By the time he had changed, they had reached the shore.

Reached it, that is, as nearly as they could. Laden with rice, the sea junks drew considerable water. The landing outside Wangjiang Gate had fallen into disrepair, and silt had made the shallows dangerous. Coming too close would risk running aground. Fortunately, a generous reward inspired willing service. Battalion Officer Zhang had taken considerable pains. He had found a large abandoned house, posted soldiers to clean and guard it as a warehouse, and held small boats ready to transfer the cargo. Now, on examining the conditions, he changed his plan and ordered the soldiers to wade through the shallows with the loads, which would be quicker and simpler. Only one small boat was used, carrying Hu, young Zhang, Liu, and Hu’s attendant Changgui ashore. Once the arrangements had been explained, Hu and Zhang entered the city, accompanied by Changgui with the visiting-card case under his arm. At the sight of banners fluttering above the walls, emotion overcame Hu and tears streamed down his face. He was thinking of Wang. How wonderful it would have been if the victorious commander entering the city today were Wang, not Jiang! People naturally judged heroes by success or failure. But would the victorious men remember how Wang had clung to the doomed city, exhausting not merely his strength and spirit but his very blood and tears? His suffering and exertions had far exceeded theirs. Hu longed to go first to the place where Wang had died and weep aloud. But the people were still in dire distress. There was no time to release his own grief and indignation. He wiped his eyes, straightened his shoulders, and walked on.

The guard had changed, and a fourth-rank military officer now commanded the gate. Seeing Hu’s third-rank civil robes—the same rank as Jiang’s—he dared not be discourteous. He came forward, saluted, and asked his official title. “This is His Honour Hu,” young Zhang explained. “He has come urgently from Shanghai to discuss important official business with Provincial Treasurer Jiang.” Changgui had already taken a visiting card from the case and handed it over. The officer could not read. He accepted the card, looked at it upside down, and said with an air of comprehension, “So His Honour Hu wishes to see His Honour Jiang! May I offer an escort?” “Nothing could be better,” Zhang replied. “Most importantly, could you find two horses?” “There are no horses. But His Honour Hu could take a chair.” A sedan-chair firm stood beside the gate, with two empty chairs still available. There were naturally no bearers. The officer obligingly offered to press some townspeople into service, but Hu firmly declined. To ride in a chair at a time like this would be utterly heartless.

Without horses, and unwilling to use a chair, they had to depend on their own legs. The officer’s soldiers escorted them, however, and they passed everywhere without obstruction, reaching the Sun residence at Sanyuanfang, where Jiang had established his headquarters. When Hu’s card went in, the central gate was opened wide and Jiang’s staff officer came out to conduct the guest inside. Approaching the hall, Hu saw a commander in a yellow riding jacket standing beneath the eaves. Taking him for Jiang, he bowed deeply. “Congratulations! Congratulations!” He was saluting the victory. Jiang returned the bow with clasped hands. “A joy we share!” he replied repeatedly. Young Zhang stepped forwards to make a formal introduction. They entered the hall and sat opposite one another as equals, host and guest, beginning with the customary civilities. Speaking as a member of Zhejiang’s gentry, Hu thanked Jiang. He then observed that, among the southeastern provinces devastated by war, Hangzhou had suffered most. From there he turned to Jiang’s own interests, beginning with a compliment.

Jiang’s courtesy name was Xiangquan, so Hu addressed him respectfully as “Mr Xiang.” “With such a great achievement to your credit, your further promotion to governor of Zhejiang must be close at hand.” “Not necessarily. I would not dare entertain such a vain hope,” Jiang replied. “Ninth Marshal Zeng has a fine elder brother. Once Jinling falls, he will come to take office, and I shall still have to present myself as his subordinate.” Zeng Guoquan held the appointment as governor but had never assumed his duties; Zuo was acting in his place. Jiang understood his own position. In military distinction and in connections alike, replacing Zeng would be no easy matter. Hu saw things differently. “Ninth Marshal Zeng is a great general. Once Jinling is taken, the court will surely have important work for him elsewhere. I believe his governorship of Zhejiang is merely an appointment held from a distance for the present. He will not come to take it up. Do not lose heart, Mr Xiang.” “Oh?” Jiang leaned forwards involuntarily. “May I ask what makes you think he will not come?”

“The reasons are plain. First, Ninth Marshal Zeng has no previous connection with Zhejiang. He knows neither the place nor its people, which makes the appointment unsuitable. Second, his elder brother is modest and most considerate of others. Governor-General Zuo has secured Zhejiang; Commander Zeng will never allow his younger brother to share out Zuo’s territory.” “Ah!” Jiang’s spirits rose. “You see the matter very clearly, Mr Xueyan.” “In my view, the reconstruction of Zhejiang, and especially of the provincial capital, will depend entirely upon you.” Hu paused. Seeing Jiang listen intently, he knew the moment for his real advice had come. Reinforcing his words with a gesture, he spoke earnestly. “The welfare or misery of Hangzhou’s people lies in your hands. Every measure of strength preserved now will save a measure of effort later.” “Quite so!” Jiang rubbed his hands, showing a little anxiety. “Please advise me. I shall spare no effort in preserving that strength.” “With such words, you truly are the saviour of Hangzhou’s people.” Hu rose and bowed. “On their behalf, I thank you.” Jiang hastily returned the courtesy. “You honour me too much!” Touching his chest, he added, “Tell me, Mr Xueyan: where should we begin in preserving what remains after this calamity?”

“Forgive my frankness. You may not be fully aware that men from the various camps are still disturbing the inhabitants.” “Well—” Hu knew that Jiang was in a difficult position. To win battles, commanders inevitably recalled the old saying that generous rewards produced brave men, and promised rewards beforehand. Yet when even military pay and provisions were hard to obtain, where could they find another great sum for bonuses? They were consequently generous at other people’s expense. Whether by hint or tacit permission, soldiers might be allowed, for three days after taking a city, to disregard the two military prohibitions against robbery and rape. Jiang might have made such a promise. To issue a proclamation forbidding these acts now would make him a commander who broke his word and would hamper his ability to lead the troops in future. Hu therefore interrupted quickly. “Mr Xiang, there is more I must explain.”

“You speak too gravely,” Jiang said. Fearing further unwelcome words that might embarrass them both, he tried to forestall them. “Your reproach is entirely just. I can only feel ashamed.” He spoke of shame, not of enforcing discipline. Courteous on the surface, the answer quietly rejected the criticism. Hearing his tone, Hu felt still more certain that his own plan was the wiser course. He could also speak more directly. “As you know, I am a merchant. In business, we speak of fair exchange. The customary courtesy of giving and receiving rests upon the same principle. Your men risked their lives to save Hangzhou’s people. Their exertions and achievements were great. The court may reward them, but if the locality offers no token of appreciation, it is unfair and does them an injustice.” Jiang was puzzled. The opening sounded very worldly, the conclusion exceedingly polite. What was the purpose? He needed to think before answering. This was an impressive man, but a formidable one. Handle him badly and a friend might become an enemy, with considerable consequences. After reflection, Jiang understood. He replied modestly, “You praise us too much. Clearing out bandits for the people is simply our duty. What merit can we claim?”

“Now it is you who are too modest. Since we meet as though we were old friends, I shall speak plainly.” Hu continued with composure. “Our people are famous for their stubborn Hangzhou heads, but they know how to distinguish kindness from injury. The government troops have served us well and deserve a reward. At present, however, nine homes in ten stand empty, and the rebels have stripped the place bare over the past two years. There is really nothing with which to honour the army. Fortunately, a number of Hangzhou gentlemen took refuge elsewhere, and I can reach most of them. I have ventured to take the responsibility of raising a hundred thousand taels and placing it in your hands, so that you may thank the men on our behalf.” Jiang found it difficult to answer. Refusal seemed discourteous, acceptance impossible. Hu’s intention was unmistakable: a hundred thousand taels to purchase absolute freedom from molestation. That was his “fair exchange,” his “giving and receiving.” But although a hundred thousand sounded enormous, divided among several tens of thousands of men it would give each only a modest amount. Whether it could settle the matter remained very doubtful.
