HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 8 — Part 4 of 9

Hu could guess the reason for Jiang’s hesitation. To ask, “Is it too little?” would be discourteous; to increase the amount unprompted would make him seem petty, as though haggling over a price. After consideration, he could see only one course: offer a further benefit indirectly. “As for a token of respect to yourself, Mr Xiang, separate arrangements will naturally be made—” “No, no!” Jiang interrupted. “Do not include me. When matters have settled a little and your province’s gentlemen write to their friends in the capital, if they can say that Jiang Yili has done his duty by Zhejiang, I shall be more than grateful.” “That need hardly be said. Mr Xiang, if you do your duty by Zhejiang, Zhejiang will certainly do its duty by you.” “Good! That is well spoken.” Jiang decided. “Then let me thank you for your generosity on behalf of all the men.” He called for an attendant. “Ask Mr Liu to come.” Mr Liu held the provincial examination degree and had purchased entry to the ranks eligible for prefectural and county appointments. Jiang relied upon him as an adviser and employed him as a drafting officer. He was summoned to discuss a proclamation enforcing military discipline and strictly prohibiting interference with the inhabitants.

That was Jiang’s business; Hu need not concern himself with it. His own problem was how to fulfil his promise: how to deliver a hundred thousand taels of shining silver to the provincial treasury for distribution among Jiang’s men. At the thought of the treasury, an inspiration flashed through his mind, like a light seen by someone lost at night. Though it vanished at once, he was certain it had not been an illusion. There must be a way forward. He composed himself and thought quietly for a while. A plan began to take shape. When Jiang’s discussion with his adviser reached a pause, Hu asked, “Where is your military supply office, Mr Xiang?” “Zhejiang’s main supply office is at Yuhang with Commander Zuo. I have a smaller one at Pingyao. He”—Jiang pointed to young Zhang—“is also an officer concerned with supply.” “And the provincial treasury?” “The treasury?” Jiang laughed. “I do not even know whether the provincial treasurer’s office still stands. How can we speak of a treasury?” “The treasury controls the revenue and expenditure of the entire province. It is of the greatest importance and should be restored as quickly as possible. Once its sign is up, people with money to remit will find their way there. Otherwise it is like the saying: carrying a pig’s head for an offering, but unable to find the temple gate. Will that not delay receipts?”

Jiang did not know who might have money to remit at such a time. Still, Hu’s advice sounded entirely reasonable: the treasury ought to be restored promptly. How to restore it, and whom to put in charge, he had no idea. Hu therefore explained the established practice and advantages of a private bank acting for a public treasury. Fukang had formerly handled Zhejiang’s provincial treasury and was willing to serve again. But its old debts and credits still required settlement. To keep the old and new business distinct, he proposed to establish another bank, called Fufeng. “Fufeng is Fukang, with an additional signboard. They will be distinct to the outside world, but internally the same. Either name will do. Mr Xiang, I am doing this entirely for the public service. When Fukang collects outstanding debts and transfers the proceeds to Fufeng, that means transferring them to you. As for what the old treasury owed others, we can judge each case: pay what must be paid and postpone what may wait, giving urgent public needs priority over private claims. Will that not leave us considerable room to manoeuvre?” “Excellent!” Jiang was delighted. “I shall certainly entrust it to you. Fufeng or Fukang, it makes no difference. You are the man I rely on.” “Since you honour me with the commission, I shall do my utmost.” Hu paused briefly. “I shall arrange the hundred thousand taels I have promised, seeing how much ready silver can be raised in Hangzhou. I shall advance the shortfall myself. For convenience, I propose an adjustment between the accounts. That will suit both the supply office and the treasury.” “How would the accounts be adjusted?”

“Suppose we can raise half the amount in silver here. I shall deliver that first. For the other half, I shall give the supply office a note redeemable at any time at my Fufeng bank in Shanghai. If communications are difficult and the money cannot immediately be collected, that need not matter. As Fufeng will be acting for the provincial treasury, any expenditure it advances on the treasury’s behalf will count as cash paid against the note. The treasury and the supply office need only make the corresponding adjustment. Each advance will be deducted, with the accounts settled monthly.” Hu made it sound entirely orderly. Jiang found the argument persuasive, but needed to think carefully before he understood how the entries would work. When he did, a question remained. “What about the treasury’s receipts? Will those first repay your advances?” “How could I do that?” Hu suddenly leaned back against his chair, shaking his head repeatedly, as though the very suggestion were beyond ordinary reason. That gesture alone won Jiang’s complete confidence. He felt that Hu was not merely honest but kind-hearted, genuinely able to treat another man’s interests as his own. Yet the offer seemed almost too generous to believe. Jiang was sure he must have misunderstood and thought it best to clarify. “Mr Xueyan,” he asked cautiously, “do you mean that, up to the amount of the note you give the supply office, your advances for the treasury will count as successive payments redeeming it? Meanwhile, you will still remit the treasury’s receipts in full?”

“Yes, exactly.” Hu continued at once. “Even after that account has been cleared, Fufeng, as agent for Zhejiang’s treasury, must uphold the credit of Zhejiang’s provincial treasurer. We shall continue to make advances.” Jiang was now not merely convinced but grateful. Clasping his hands, he said, “We shall depend upon you in everything. Your bank shall act for the treasury. Would you like an official letter of appointment?” “You are a high provincial official appointed by the court. Your word is sufficient. To me, the presence or absence of a document makes no difference. But to establish confidence with others, I should like a proclamation announcing Fufeng’s appointment.” “Easily done. I shall have it prepared at once.” “I too shall have preparations made overnight, and tomorrow we can post the notices. But”—Hu lowered his voice—“as to which payments should be made, which should wait, and which really must not be made, Fufeng will obey your instructions. Please give us a private sign to guide us.” “A private sign?” Jiang scratched his head, looking perplexed. Young Zhang understood this better than he did. “Your Honour!” he said.

Now that he held office, Zhang performed its courtesies, and “Your Honour” came very naturally. When Jiang turned towards him, he continued. “It is difficult to be the man in charge. Sometimes men ask for provisions or pay, and although you know it cannot be given, you cannot conveniently refuse. You have to endorse the request for payment, and the supply office must of course comply. Yet if there is truly neither money nor food, it must find tactful ways of discussing the matter. Intendant Hu means to spare you embarrassment. With a signal agreed beforehand, he will know your wishes and can find a way to put the applicants off.” “Ah!” Jiang understood. “This has troubled me continually. These are old comrades who have faced death with me, and their pay is already in arrears. When they bring sealed claims for my endorsement, how can I refuse to approve them? But whether the supply office can bear the expenditure is another matter. Often those who should be paid receive nothing, while those who should not receive anything get their money. The supply office has complained to me countless times, sometimes stamping in exasperation, and I have been helpless. A method that lets me play the good man while someone else plays the bad one—that is excellent. Tell me what signal to use.” “You are too kind,” Hu replied. “The signal must change frequently to prevent people from discovering it. For the moment, let us begin simply. If you sign with the single character ‘Li,’ Fufeng will pay in full. If you write the two characters of your given name, ‘Yili,’ it will pay half. If you sign your full name, surname included, that will mean refusal, and Fufeng will find an excuse.” “Excellent!” Jiang clapped his hands. “One conversation with you is worth ten years in office!”

Host and guest looked at one another and laughed, feeling a complete understanding between them. With relations so warm, Hu thought there were matters he need no longer withhold. He glanced at young Zhang, whispered the single word “Rice,” and made a slight motion with his lips. Zhang was quick to read both words and expression. Leaning forwards, he addressed Jiang. “There is something I must report. Battalion Officer Zhang and Intendant Hu’s relative are unloading the few hundred shi of rice. It will be stored temporarily pending instructions. I did not previously explain where it came from. I should now say that Intendant Hu brought it, and the quantity is far greater.” “How much?” Jiang asked with intense interest. “Ten thousand shi or more,” Hu replied. “I brought it expressly to relieve the government troops and the people of Hangzhou. Strictly speaking, the proper course would be to deliver it to you. But I have a difficulty of my own. Could you do me the favour of leaving it temporarily in my custody? After I have seen Governor-General Zuo, it will in any case be placed at your disposal for public distribution. The delay will be only a day or two.” Jiang was astonished. Since the war began, especially in Zhejiang, death by starvation had become commonplace. The sudden appearance of ten thousand shi of rice was like a gift from Heaven. How could he fail to be amazed and delighted?

“Mr Xueyan, your rice is more than charcoal brought through the snow. It is rain in a great drought! I shall send soldiers to guard it and have Officer Zhang assist with the arrangements. At the same time, I shall send a reliable man to escort you to Yuhang to see Commander Zuo. But I hope you will return quickly. There are so many important matters here on which we need your help.” “I shall come back as quickly as I can.” “When do you intend to leave? It must be too late tonight.” “Yes. Early tomorrow morning.” Jiang nodded, summoned his staff and drafting officers, and assigned every task required for Hu. The escort to Yuhang was an officer named He, a nephew through Jiang’s cousin and a trusted subordinate. Hu thanked Officer He warmly, cultivated his goodwill, and arranged to meet him early the next morning at Zhang’s house for their departure.

At the Zhang house, the father naturally spoke to Hu with complete openness, declaring all former grievances forgotten. He honoured him as a distinguished guest, though circumstances allowed courtesy rather than elaborate food and wine. Hu had no appetite for either. After a hasty meal sufficient to fill his stomach, he had many important instructions to give during the one night available. Bitterly short of help, he had to treat young Zhang as a trusted assistant too. There was no time for leisurely discussion; he stated his requirements directly. “First, young Zhang, please consult your father and find a few respected local men. Talk to them and see how much silver can be raised to deliver to Provincial Treasurer Jiang as a temporary loan to Fufeng. Make them understand that this is to save the locality and themselves. Responsibility for the hundred thousand taels rests entirely upon me. If they are willing to share it later, so much the better. If not, I shall bear it alone. But I cannot bring the funds from Shanghai at this moment, and must ask for their help.” Zhang nodded repeatedly. “Leave that to my father and me. You have done everything a man could do for us, Mr Hu. Everyone is grateful. Anyone who has ready silver will certainly be willing to lend it.” “Next, Fukang must reopen at once, and Fufeng’s sign must go up. I should like Third Master Liu to handle the internal arrangements and you to deal with matters outside.” Hu turned to Liu. “First, inspect Fukang’s old premises. If they can be used, have them cleaned immediately. Write two notices on plum-red paper: ‘Fukang will reopen shortly,’ and ‘Fufeng acts for the provincial treasury.’ Post them at once.” “What about the treasurer’s proclamation?”

“Post that on the day we reopen. Next, prepare one or two thousand taels in cash. Most importantly, get several dozen sacks of rice into the premises. Then put up a red notice: ‘All former Fukang staff are requested to return immediately.’ As each man arrives, give him ten taels and five dou of rice. Then our performance can begin.” Young Zhang hurried to speak. “Mr Hu, I must make one thing clear. Since you think well of me, I would go through boiling water and fire at your command. But I must judge my own abilities. I know nothing of banking, and I fear I may not have enough time. I must not delay your important business. Even if you were too kind to reproach me, I could not forgive myself.” “Never mind. I know you are busy. Do what you can.” Hu lowered his voice. “Why do I want to act for the treasury? To establish the name. Fukang has an excellent reputation, certainly, but only old residents of Hangzhou know it. I now want to attract new depositors, and I need another way of appealing to them. Acting for the treasury is the best possible recommendation. If all Zhejiang’s public funds can be entrusted to me, what doubt can remain about my reliability? Once the new depositors think that way, money will flow steadily into Fufeng. Then we shall have both the reward money promised to Jiang and the funds needed for advances on the treasury’s behalf.” With the instructions apparently settled, Liu had something private to say. He signalled to Hu, drew him aside, and spoke quietly. “Getting on well with Jiang Xiangquan will not help. I heard something today from a fairly reliable source. Governor-General Zuo intends to call you to account. He has already spoken of it. If matters go badly, he may name you in a severe memorial of impeachment.” “Do not worry.” Hu remained wholly untroubled. “There is no account of mine that cannot be explained. You cannot believe all the talk outside.”

Seeing his confidence, Liu felt reassured. Young Zhang still had a question. “Your calculation is excellent, Mr Hu. But after all this, who exactly are these new depositors?” “The rebels,” Hu answered. “Once they surrender, they cannot carry away all the silver they have squeezed out of people these two years. They must find somewhere to deposit it.” The new customers Hu meant to attract were the rebels themselves! Neither Zhang nor Liu could have dreamed of such an idea. Both thought his conception remarkable, but both also foresaw considerable trouble. Their astonished and puzzled expressions told Hu what questions lay behind them. He understood perfectly. But there was no time to debate at leisure, and he could only adopt a somewhat peremptory tone. “The plan is sound. Both of you should think along the lines I have laid down. Think about what? How to make them fully confident in entrusting their private savings to Fufeng. Do you understand?” “I understand. But—” Liu stopped. “So do I,” Zhang said. “I know conditions in Hangzhou better. If I find a few people to solicit these deposits, they certainly will not return empty-handed. But before depositing, the men will ask whether their money will be lost at Fufeng. How should I answer?”

“Tell them it will not be lost. But the court’s law must also be respected. If a man himself thinks his deposit may one day be seized by the authorities, he must consider that for himself.” Hu paused, then continued, “In a word, we can offer convenience and accommodation; we cannot break the law. There is no need to force anyone to open an account. We must establish our standing and our credit. Once we have credit, people will come even without a passbook, trusting our word alone.” Neither Liu nor Zhang could immediately fathom this. It seemed somewhat inconsistent. But they could not question him closely now, and the matter was not so urgent that its every implication must be settled that night. They exchanged a glance, reached a silent understanding, and decided to discuss it later.

“Doing the work is easy; conducting oneself is hard.” After a brief silence, Hu unexpectedly began with this complaint, leading into an important explanation—and a warning. “From today, we have much hard work ahead, but it will be worthwhile. Whether it proceeds smoothly depends entirely on how we conduct ourselves. Young Zhang, tell me: how should we behave now?” Zhang considered, then smiled slightly. “Conduct comes down to good faith and loyalty. Since we are helping Governor-General Zuo, we should clench our teeth and help him to the end.” “If we intend to help him through to the end, we must stand openly on his side,” Liu added. “But that will offend a great many people.” “Try to please everyone and you please no one,” Hu replied. “We need only examine our hearts. If we can say we have done our duty by the court and the people, with a clear conscience, there is nothing to fear. It is late. Let us go to bed.” None of them slept well that night. There was too much to think about, and still more emotional excitement. Shanghai and Hangzhou had both been secured; the conclusion of the siege of Jinling could not be far away. What would the situation be then? How were the scattered and unattached soldiers to be dealt with? Should they be discharged or retained? Everywhere were questions, perplexing beyond measure.
