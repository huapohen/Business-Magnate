HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 8 — Part 5 of 9

Suddenly Hu Xueyan heard someone outside the wall striking a gong and wooden clapper. A watchman was making his rounds. The long-besieged city had only just been recovered; everywhere still bore the confusion of war, yet a night watchman was abroad. As the “tok, tok, clang; tok, tok, clang” drew nearer, Hu felt the joy and gratitude of a man hearing footsteps in an empty valley. His mood changed. Everything before him vanished from his mind, and he remembered his boyhood: snug beneath his quilt on a cold night, hearing a distant cry borne on the northwest wind—“In this bitter winter weather, take care with fire and candles!” How incomparably peaceful it had felt. That was the sound of settled times, and now he heard it again. Suddenly invigorated, he could no longer remain in bed. All three men slept in the same room. He rose quietly, unwilling to disturb Liu Bucai and young Zhang, but Zhang had already noticed. “What are you doing, Mr Hu?” “Have you not fallen asleep?” “No. Have you?” “Neither have I.” “We are all alike,” Liu said from behind his bed curtains. “I have been listening all along. There is quite a stir outside. Treasurer Jiang has kept his word; his efforts to restrain the troops are already taking effect.” “Mr Hu has earned merit in Heaven’s hidden account.” Young Zhang, suddenly inspired too, sprang from bed. “There is more to do these next two days than we can finish. Who has time to sleep?” As soon as they rose, a light appeared in the Zhang family’s kitchen. They washed and drank tea. Zhang expected Hu to return to their unfinished business, but he asked instead, quite at his ease, “Did you hear the watchman’s clapper just now?” “Yes. Everything in Hangzhou has changed except Old Zhou the watchman. He has made his rounds every night, without missing a single one.” Hu’s expression grew solemn. “Remarkable! Truly remarkable! How old is he?”

“Over sixty. He was still strong, though I do not know how he is now.” “He has escaped starvation and still makes his rounds every day. He must have an unusually blessed constitution,” Liu observed. “A pity he is only a watchman.” “Third Master, that is not the way to look at it. There are many tasks in the world that require no special talent. Anyone can do them. The question is whether you are willing to do them, and whether you take them seriously. A man who does that is remarkable.” Hu turned to Zhang. “Do something for me: ask Old Zhou whether he would like to change his occupation.” “Change his occupation? Do you mean to advance him, Mr Hu?” “Yes. Or you might say I mean to make use of his strengths. We are short of hands. We cannot let a man so faithful to his duty pass us by. I should like him to help us.” “I am sure he would be willing. I only fear there is little he can do.” “I shall put him in charge of a warehouse. If he cannot manage, I shall give him someone to help. He need only inspect it at the proper hours, as he makes his rounds.” At this point, the Zhangs’ manservant laid the table for breakfast. It was only leftover rice boiled into a pot of thin porridge, with a single dish of salted greens. But the hungry are easily fed, and after their half-night’s rest the men’s appetites were keen. Everything tasted unusually good. “I have not eaten anything so delicious in years!” Hu declared contentedly. “Third Master Liu was right: ‘If it serves the purpose, it is good.’ At a time like this, rice in hot water and salted greens are more precious than every delicacy of land and sea.” Young Zhang gained another deep insight. The art of employing people was not bound by a single pattern. Its secret was to adapt to time and place. He nodded thoughtfully, knowing now where to look for people Hu could use.

Officer He arrived at dawn with two mounted orderlies and an extra horse. The animal had a distinguished history: a gift from the Mongol prince Sengge Rinchen, it had been carefully tended on Jiang Yili’s orders as a mount reserved for Zuo Zongtang. Now it was being lent specially to Hu. He also brought news. The Longhairs in Yuhang had abandoned that city the previous day and fled towards Huzhou. Zuo was pursuing them in person and was now encamped before Anxi Pass, north of Pingyao. To visit him meant risking arrows and blades. What did Hu wish to do? “Life and death are decreed. If the Commander-in-Chief can go there, so can I. There is no need for fear.” “But it is a long journey. We cannot arrive in a day, and there is nowhere to lodge along the way. That is another difficulty.” “We shall go as fast as we can. If we cannot arrive, there is nothing to be done. With you beside me, I feel quite at ease.” It was merely a casual reply, but He heard it as a deeply sincere expression of trust. He therefore took particular care over the arrangements. After some thought, he asked, “Your Excellency, can you ride at speed?” “I can manage.” “And your steward?” “I am afraid he cannot.” “Then there is no need to bring him. We have four men here; let them attend to anything you require.” He added, “We can use the relay system, changing horses. That will be faster.” An urgent dispatch travelled by changing horses at each post. Since each animal covered only one stage, it could be driven at full speed; this was much faster than riding the same horse all the way. Fine as the prince’s mount was, it could serve for only one stage, and if it were lost in an exchange it would be troublesome to recover. Hu therefore suggested finding another. “Easy enough. We shall exchange it at the stables before we leave.”

Hu took leave of the Zhang household, saying that he would certainly return on the morning of the third day. He and Officer He first exchanged the horse at the provincial treasurer’s headquarters, then rode out through Wulin Gate and galloped to Gongchen Bridge. There He found a military camp he knew, obtained fresh, good horses, and set off northwest. There were naturally checkpoints, delays and moments of danger, but they came safely through them all. At three in the afternoon, they reached Pingyao and at last stopped for food and rest. Enquiries placed Zuo at Anxi Pass, eighteen li farther north. “It is a mountain road, and a difficult one,” He urged earnestly. “Your Excellency, to speak frankly, you are a southerner. As the saying goes, southerners sail boats; northerners ride horses. You do not ride very well. For safety’s sake, we should rest a night before continuing. What do you think?” Hu reflected that he knew neither the place nor its people and could not insist on having his way. Even if they reached Anxi that night, he could not see Zuo. He agreed, with one condition: “We must leave at first light tomorrow.” “Of course. I shall not delay your business.” With that settled, they could rest at leisure. Imperial troops had long been stationed at Pingyao, and its market was fairly prosperous, with many food stalls. Hu never put on official airs and personally invited the four orderlies to drink with him. They were so ill at ease, however, that he finally let them sit at another table. He drank with Officer He and listened to everything he had to say about Zuo.

“Our Commander-in-Chief is excellent in every respect but his temper. When he flies into a rage, though, you must not be afraid. The more frightened you are, the worse it gets.” “He responds to firmness rather than submission,” Hu said. “That sort of man is easier to deal with.” “Yes. But you must not try to outdo him in firmness. The best thing is to take no notice, let him finish his scolding and say all he has to say, then present your own reasons. He will regard you differently.” Hu found this advice valuable and raised his cup to He. Then he asked, “How many years have you been with Treasurer Jiang?” “We are close relations. I have always been with him.” “Forgive a presumptuous question: how does the Commander-in-Chief regard him? Does he consider him a possible replacement for himself?” “Hardly!” He replied. “What sort of man is our commander? He thinks himself another Zhuge Liang. Who could replace him?” To another listener, those casual words might have meant little. To Hu, they led to a most important decision.

He had continually pondered his future course and the powerful support he would need to rebuild his fortunes and begin anew. Jiang Yili had seemed an honest, kindly man. If their friendship grew close, he might become a second Wang Youling, trusting Hu’s advice and treating him like a brother. That would be far pleasanter than serving Zuo, whose temper was notorious. Now He’s words brought sudden understanding. Zuo would never spare no effort to advance Jiang as He Guiqing had advanced Wang. A provincial governorship was no small appointment. Unless another suitable post could be found for Zeng Guoquan, Jiang himself proved fully qualified, and Zuo gave him exceptional support, the seal of the governor of Zhejiang was unlikely to fall into his hands. Hu therefore had only one course: devote himself wholeheartedly to Zuo. They rose in the middle of the night and set off at dawn, taking three hours to cover the eighteen li of mountain road. Zuo’s headquarters occupied a temple to Lord Guan. Even amid the haste of campaigning, he maintained considerable gubernatorial ceremony. A great sedan chair covered in green wool stood before the temple. Against the screen wall were several vermilion boards on tall poles, their inscriptions in imitation Song lettering picked out in gold. One read, “Imperially Appointed Superintendent of Military Affairs in Zhejiang”; another, “Concurrently Acting Governor of Zhejiang”; a third, “First-Rank Insignia, Minister of War, Concurrent Right Censor-in-Chief, Governor-General of Fujian and Zhejiang”; and a fourth, “Granted the Right to Wear the Peacock Feather”. The last was less glorious, and a lifelong grievance to Zuo: his examination distinction was merely “Successful Candidate in the Hunan Provincial Examination of the Renchen Year, the Twelfth Year of Daoguang”. He held only the provincial degree.

Looking inside, Hu saw two lines of armed guards stretching from the main entrance to the statues of Guan Ping and Zhou Cang before the great hall. Several military officers with blue hat-buttons were there too. Unwilling to affront Zuo’s imposing dignity, Hu led his horse aside, took out his formal visiting paper and asked He to present it. A long time passed before an officer of the headquarters guard emerged with it in his hand. Though he had plainly seen Hu, he called out in an official tone, “Which gentleman is Intendant Hu from Hangzhou?” Hu nodded, assumed an official manner himself, and advanced with measured steps. “I am.” “The Commander-in-Chief will receive you.” “Very well. Please lead the way.” They entered the gate but passed to the west of the hall through a side entrance into a small courtyard, also crowded with guards. An attendant in a grey cloth gown was more courteous: he lifted the door curtain and motioned Hu inside. A short, stout old man sat at a square table by the window, holding a long-stemmed pipe in his left hand while the brush in his right flew across the paper. He seemed unaware of Hu’s footsteps. Hu could only wait. When the brush was laid down, he gathered up his gown, made his formal obeisance and announced himself. “Hu Guangyong, expectant circuit intendant of Zhejiang, pays his respects to Your Excellency.” “Oh, so you are Hu Guangyong!” Zuo’s authoritative eyes seemed to flash as they surveyed him from head to foot. “I have heard of you for a long time.” This was no compliment, and Hu saw no reason for modest disclaimers. “Your Excellency has achieved an extraordinary victory. I have come expressly to congratulate you.” “Indeed? You are quick to anticipate the prevailing wind. No wonder you were known as a capable officer in Governor Wang’s day.”

Hu naturally caught the sarcasm, but there was no need to dispute it yet. The first necessity was to secure a seat. Zuo could not be ignorant of official etiquette: a civil officer visiting a governor or governor-general, however low his rank, was entitled to sit. His deliberate omission of the invitation was meant to humiliate Hu, who must first find a way to meet it. The moment he considered it, a solution came. Gathering his gown again, he made another obeisance. “I have come not only to congratulate Your Excellency, but to thank you. The people of Zhejiang have been hanging upside down in their distress, and you have rescued them.” Everyone spoke of Zuo’s “Hunan mule” temper, but this repeated courtesy at last turned the mule. “I do not deserve it.” His voice remained cool, suggesting that flattery would not move him, but he finally answered courtesy with courtesy. “Please be seated, Intendant.” An attendant had long been waiting with a tray, unable to offer tea while the guest remained standing. Now he placed a covered cup on the small table at Hu’s side. Hu inclined himself and breathed more freely. So long as appearances were not humiliating, he thought, they could talk.

“During my two years in Zhejiang, I have heard a great deal about you,” Zuo said without a smile. “I hear you are very grand.” “I would not presume to say so.” Hu leaned forward slightly. “May I ask what Your Excellency means by ‘grand’?” “They say you live and enjoy yourself like a prince. It may be exaggerated, but one can imagine something of the truth.” “Yes. I shall not conceal it. Compared with expectant officials living in hardship, I am very comfortable.” He admitted it frankly without explaining the source of his comfort, which seemed to leave Zuo with nothing to say. After a pause, Zuo spoke directly. “I have received a number of complaints describing your conduct. Such reports may not all be invention. I intend to investigate. If they prove true, my duty to reform the administration will oblige me to impeach you by name in the strongest terms.” “Certainly. If I have acted unlawfully, I shall willingly accept the punishment Your Excellency seeks. But I believe I have not dared commit evil or practise corruption for private gain. Governor Wang recognised and favoured me, and I swore to share life and death with him. In carrying out my duties, I shrank neither from toil nor from resentment. That may indeed have earned me enemies.” “Whether you committed evil or practised corruption remains to be established. As for your oath to share life and death with Governor Wang, that is difficult to believe. He has died at his post, while you remain perfectly well, do you not?”

“If Your Excellency reproaches me for failing to follow Governor Wang beneath the earth, I have nothing to say. But if you believe that dying for loyalty or honour has a rightful purpose, whereas dying merely to accompany a friend is lighter than a feather and no course for a gentleman, then I have a few words in my defence.” “Speak.” “You mean that I shared Governor Wang’s hardship in the besieged city but left him at the crucial moment, making my oath to share his life and death a deception?” “Yes!” Zuo stared hard at him. “How do you explain yourself? I should like to hear.” “First, may I ask Your Excellency this? When Hangzhou was surrounded and Governor Wang struggled to hold it, shedding blood rather than tears from his eyes, what was he hoping for?” “Reinforcements, naturally.” “Yes,” Hu said in a low voice. “Li Yuandu’s army was at Quzhou. We tried every means to hasten its arrival, but it never came. We therefore had no choice but to prepare for a prolonged defence. What does a beleaguered city need in order to hold out?” “Food, naturally. ‘To the people, food is Heaven.’” “True enough. When every last resource is exhausted, civilians may exchange their children to eat. But what happens when soldiers have no food? I need not say; Your Excellency knows far better than I do. Governor Wang discussed it with me and asked me to buy rice in Shanghai.” Hu suddenly raised his voice. “Though he had entered official life by purchase, Governor Wang had read books. He told me the story of the orphan of Zhao in the Records of the Historian. If the city could not be held, he said, its defender could only die, and that was easy. To buy rice in Shanghai was harder, like preserving the orphan’s life. He wanted me to be Cheng Ying, who saved the orphan of Zhao. That was, of course, his generous opinion of me. But consider, Your Excellency: as governor, he was responsible for defending his territory. Even if he could have procured rice, he could not leave Hangzhou. I was the only one who could go to Shanghai for it. I had no right to refuse.” “Mm. And afterwards?” Zuo asked. “Did you obtain the rice?” “Of course. But—” Hu’s voice sank sorrowfully. “It was of no avail.”

He then explained how he had brought the rice to the Qiantang River outside Hangzhou, how every effort to open a supply route had failed, how he had bowed towards the city and withdrawn in tears, and how he had used the rice to relieve Ningbo. He said nothing of the grave illness there that had nearly killed him; that would sound like a claim to merit and would only weaken his account with unnecessary detail. Zuo listened closely, then tilted his face upwards in thought for a long while. At last he said something Hu had never expected: “You seem to have read quite a few books yourself!” Hu was startled, then understood. Throughout their exchange his language had been rather polished, and he had referred to the story in the Records of the Historian. Zuo must suspect that he had consulted some learned adviser and prepared a set speech. There was a measure of truth in it: Hu had repeatedly rehearsed what he would say when they met. But he could not admit to having deliberately concocted pleasing falsehoods. “How could I claim to be a learned man? I merely know enough to respect those who are.” “That too is commendable,” Zuo said. “I shall investigate those accusations. If matters are indeed as you describe, that is naturally another question.” “It is more than that. Having received public money, I must account for its use in public business. The money did not come from Your Excellency’s hands, but you are now the head of this province. It is to you alone that I must render my account.” “Oh, you have come to account for official business? For that sum of public money?” Zuo asked. “How much did you receive?”
