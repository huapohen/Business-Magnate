HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 8 — Part 7 of 9

Another question followed. “Should this penalty contribution be reported to the throne? What do you think?” “If it is reported first, opposition may prevent the scheme from starting. If it is not, the ministry may later raise procedural objections, or a censor may impeach us. There are advantages and disadvantages on both sides. Your Excellency must decide.” “It must be done. But though I am not afraid of trouble, there is no reason to take the blame unnecessarily. Think again: is there a way to prevent interference while leaving us a defensible position?” “Act impartially in the public interest, and there will always be a way to defend ourselves. I suggest not reporting before we begin. Once the scheme has produced results, memorialise the throne with the amounts collected. Thereafter, notify the ministry directly for its records, providing the basis for the eventual accounting.” “Good! We shall do exactly that.” Zuo was delighted. “‘Act impartially in the public interest, and there will always be a way to defend ourselves’—very well said. But the results you mention are important too. The state once kept more than ten million taels sealed in the inner treasury for major campaigns. That great sum was squandered by Saishang’a and his fellow Banner grandees. So the saying ‘The emperor does not send hungry soldiers on his errands’ no longer applies. Now the court not only sends hungry soldiers and requires each province to find its own military funds; it also demands remittances for the capital’s military expenses. If we establish a productive tax or contribution and are forbidden to continue it, very well: let the court assign us a reliable source for the same amount.” That brought his argument to its conclusion. Hu now understood more deeply Zuo’s position, his thinking and his response to circumstances. Any method of raising military funds that was not utterly wicked could gain his approval.

Hu remained at Zuo’s headquarters for two days before returning to Hangzhou. His homecoming was far more splendid than his departure. An escort of Zuo’s personal guards was only to be expected. What surprised him most, outside Wulin Gate, was a welcoming party of distinguished-looking men. More than half were active members of Hangzhou’s gentry, including Licentiate Zhang; the rest wore official robes, though Hu knew none of them. A great green-covered sedan chair stood in the gateway. What was that for? Puzzled, he asked Officer He, “Have they come to meet me?” Before He could answer, the sight of Liu Bucai and young Zhang settled the question. Zhang ran up beaming, caught the ring of his horse’s bridle and cried, “The news reached us the night before last! We have awaited you like clouds and rain in a scorching drought.” What news? Why such eagerness for his return? Before Hu could ask, a guard officer bowed before his horse. “Please dismount, Your Excellency, and take the sedan chair.” Young Zhang hurried to explain. “Treasurer Jiang sent this officer. The green-covered chair is his own, brought specially for you.” “Yes.” The officer opened a visiting-card box and presented Jiang’s card and invitation. “My superior sent me to attend you. He expressly instructed me to say that he intended to come himself, but several urgent matters require immediate decisions, and he cannot get away. He asks to see you as soon as you arrive. There is much awaiting discussion.” Hu understood. The “news” was his appointment as director of the reconstruction bureau. Everyone’s eagerness, and Jiang’s insistence on seeing him at once, arose naturally from the urgency of relief. The work of reconstruction awaited his decisions before it could begin.

Precisely for that reason, he thought it unwise to see Jiang first. Yet Jiang was now effectively the head of the province. To reject such attentive hospitality would be discourteous; only an excellent excuse would do. Hu thought quickly. By the time he dismounted, his answer was ready. “Please tell your superior that I too am anxious to pay my respects and seek his instructions on many matters. But after these days of travelling back and forth, I am disgracefully dirty. To visit a superior in this state would be disrespectful. Let me wash and change into clean clothes, and I shall come at once. His green-covered chair is not one I am entitled to ride in, but it would be rude to refuse his kindness. Please ask the bearers to follow me with it empty.” He went first to the Zhang house for a short rest and wrote an outline of the principal reconstruction measures and the support he needed from Jiang. Only then did he attend the banquet. They met warmly. Jiang handed him the letter of appointment, and Hu took out a sheet of paper in return. It was headed “Urgent Reconstruction Measures” and contained seven proposals. First: bury the dead within half a month. Great epidemics followed great wars; this would both give the departed rest and prevent disease.

Second: provide free porridge for six months. The government should supply the grain, while impartial local gentlemen supervised its distribution. Third: invite merchants to bring in food, clothing, bricks, tiles, timber and other necessities, exempting these goods from likin to encourage trade. Fourth: investigate those who had died loyally in the disaster, collect accounts of their deeds, and petition the throne for a shrine commemorating the loyal dead. Fifth: trace the families of women rescued from the rebel camps and send them home with trustworthy escorts. Sixth: make every effort to arrange spring cultivation, on which livelihoods in the coming autumn and winter depended. Seventh: reopen the academies and give favourable treatment to scholars.

“Quite right, quite right!” Jiang said. “I agree to everything. If you need men, appointments or official papers, tell me, Mr Xueyan. Whatever lies within my power shall be done.” “Thank you, Mr Xiang, for helping the people of Zhejiang. But there is one matter on which I must ask your exceptional support.” Hu spoke plainly. “The troops must be kept under discipline.” Jiang reddened. He knew his men’s discipline was poor, but offered a defence. “To be honest, the men have gone hungry for so long—” “Mr Xiang,” Hu interrupted, “I shall take responsibility for their pay. Please take responsibility for their discipline.” Jiang reflected that Hu could now see Zuo directly and was said to have all his advice accepted. If he raised the matter with their superior and an order came back down, Jiang would lose face. Better to resolve it himself. He therefore decided on two measures: a proclamation reaffirming military discipline, with offenders to be executed on the spot; and, from the next day, his own presence throughout the day at Guanxiangkou in the city centre to enforce military law personally. Discipline did indeed improve considerably, and reconstruction became easier to undertake. The burden fell on Hu, who slept fewer than three double-hours a day and lost several jin in weight. Yet he remained vigorous and showed no fatigue.

Zuo reached the provincial capital on the second day of the third month. The first man he summoned after stepping from his chair was Hu. “Terrible!” There was an unusually dejected look on his face. “Since the war began, I have visited many places, but never seen one devastated like Hangzhou. What was its former population?” “Eight hundred and ten thousand.” “And now?” “A little over seventy thousand.” “A little over seventy thousand!” Zuo sighed. Then he looked up. “Mr Xueyan, you do not say eighty thousand or sixty thousand, but a little over seventy thousand. On what evidence?” “It is an estimate, but not an idle guess. I have calculated it from the tokens issued at the porridge kitchens and the places selling grain at reduced prices.” “Excellent!” Zuo was full of praise. “You truly combine great ability with care for detail. What do you consider the most urgent measures now?”

“Naturally, reviving trade. A market can flourish only if people are willing to come and do business. Merchants are timid. If Your Excellency can make them confident enough to come to Hangzhou, trade will revive, the people will have a livelihood, and public revenue from likin will rise. It will benefit both the government and the people enormously.” “That simply means taking still greater pains to enforce discipline. Tell the merchants not to be afraid. Let them come and trade in Hangzhou. If they suffer any wrong, they may complain directly at my office. I shall punish the offenders severely.” “With that assurance, they will dare come.” Hu added, “Reconstruction covers a vast range of matters. At present, relief and reassurance are the chief tasks. Might the reconstruction bureau be renamed the relief bureau?” “Yes. An excellent suggestion.” Zuo immediately issued a written instruction. Everything else remained as before; only the name changed. The bureau’s troublesome, minute affairs consumed so much of Hu’s time that he could never find a suitable opportunity for another meeting with Zuo.

Half a month passed in this way. Then the courier who had carried the special memorial announcing Hangzhou’s recovery returned from Beijing with good news for Zuo personally. “The Grand Secretariat has received an oral decree: Since taking charge of military affairs in Zhejiang, Zuo Zongtang, Governor-General of Fujian and Zhejiang, has successively recovered prefectural, subprefectural and county seats. He has now taken the provincial capital of Hangzhou and the county seat of Yuhang, demonstrating skilful direction. As a special favour, he is granted the title of Junior Guardian of the Heir Apparent and permission to wear the yellow riding jacket.” Jiang Yili too received the yellow riding jacket. “Zuo Zongtang shall investigate the services of all officers and men involved and recommend the most deserving for reward.” When the news spread, every civil and military official in the city entitled to see the governor-general dressed in formal robes and went to headquarters to offer congratulations. Wearing his new yellow jacket, Zuo received them in groups with generous words of encouragement. When he saw Hu kowtowing with the expectant circuit intendants, he specially instructed a guard to wait at the second hall and detain him. After the guests and staff had dispersed, the two met in informal dress in the garden reception room. Hu naturally congratulated him again. Zuo spoke of the weight of imperial favour and how difficult it was to repay. Then he apologised: the memorial recommending rewards for the recovery of Hangzhou was already drafted and about to be sent, but Hu’s name was not in it. This first list was confined to those who had stormed the city. When the reconstruction staff were recommended later, Hu would certainly head the list.

Hu thanked him and briefly reported progress. His guiding principles were “relief through work; revival through trade”, so that assistance to the needy also accomplished the work of restoration. Zuo nodded repeatedly in approval and asked what difficulties he faced. “There are naturally too many to recount. I would not trouble Your Excellency with them. Whatever I can manage, I shall attend to myself. But without foresight, trouble soon comes close. It is already late in the third month, and the hungry fifth and sixth months will be upon us before we know it. Every household must meet its immediate needs. In the gap between the old crop and the new, people endure because they have something to hope for: the autumn harvest, which will let them repay their debts and pass the New Year. Your Excellency, that may be difficult this year.” The words alerted Zuo. Startled, he rubbed his hands. “Yes! The autumn harvest depends on spring cultivation. This is the time to transplant rice seedlings. If we miss it, the consequences will be dreadful.” “With Your Excellency recognising that, the people of Zhejiang can be saved.” “Do not think it so easy. We must discuss it carefully. Mr Xueyan, how should we encourage cultivation?”

“Your Excellency has read the ancient books widely. Every dynasty has known great disorder. You must remember clearly how they helped country people return to their fields afterwards.” “Ah, yes. A sound observation. I do. The early Han handled this well. Empress Dowager Bo followed the teachings of the Yellow Emperor and Laozi: quietness and non-interference. That truly allowed the people to recover. I only wonder whether our present two empress dowagers can do as she did.” Hu did not understand how those teachings became government through non-action, nor did he know that Empress Dowager Bo was Emperor Wen’s mother. But he understood the familiar phrases “quietness and non-interference” and “allow the people to recover”, and took up Zuo’s point. “No one sees more clearly than Your Excellency. To bring life back to abandoned fields, the method is simple: do not disturb them. Your authority is as firm as a mountain; your orders are obeyed. One command will bring the people boundless benefit.” “Certainly. That command must be issued. Tell me what it should forbid.” “First,” Hu said after a moment’s thought, “military funds come from likin and contributions from wealthy households. They have nothing to do with ordinary farmers. I presume Your Excellency intends to seek remission of this year’s land taxes. My fear is that county revenue clerks may demand supposed arrears under false pretexts. That will frighten the people away from their fields.” “Impossible!” Zuo’s expression hardened. “Anyone who did that would be utterly heartless. He would be executed without pardon.”

“Second, I fear the troops may seize people for forced labour.” “They will not. I prohibited it long ago. Requisitioned service must be paid for. I can reaffirm the order now: during the busy farming season there must be no disturbance at all, and the farmers must be protected. What else?” “The soldiers may slaughter plough oxen.” “They will not do that either. Whoever kills a plough ox, I shall kill him.” “With such protection, there is hope for this year’s harvest. As for seed and implements, I shall procure them. Whether they are to be lent by the government or sold for cash at fair prices, Your Excellency can settle the rules later. Whatever the decision, it cannot be wrong to have them ready.” “Quite right. Please obtain them—and advance the money too. Apart from money, there is nothing here that cannot be discussed.” “Yes,” Hu replied. “And apart from money, there is nothing I need not discuss with Your Excellency. I ask you to stand behind me.” “Of course. If you need men or official authorisation, you have only to speak.”

“There is one matter to discuss. Silk from the counties of Huzhou is the lifeblood of northern Zhejiang. Raising silkworms is extremely troublesome. They are called ‘silkworm babies’ because they are so delicate. Families shut their doors, take turns watching at night and feed them at fixed hours. They will not even receive unfamiliar visitors. Treasurer Jiang is now leading his forces against Huzhou. Wherever a great army passes, the people may have to prepare tea and food, and then the silkworms cannot be tended. Moreover, silkworms depend entirely on mulberry leaves. If the soldiers cut down the trees for firewood, the creatures will starve.” “Oh!” Zuo listened intently. “I have always taken an interest in practical statecraft, but I did not know that raising silkworms required so much care. As you explain it, the matter is vital. I must tell Jiang Xiangquan at once to provide special protection. Besides preventing interference by our troops, we must above all guard against Longhairs breaking out of Huzhou and scattering through the countryside, harming the silkworm households.” “With such an order, all will be well.” Hu was relieved. “In the lower Yangtze region, the fourth month is the crucial one. As the country song says, ‘It is hard to be Heaven in the fourth month.’ Rice transplanting and silkworm raising both take place then: one needs rain, the other clear skies. With the court’s blessings and Your Excellency’s authority, may next month bring favourable weather and success in the campaign. If we can pass that month in peace, Zhejiang’s suffering can end.” “I understand. I shall do what I can to fulfil everyone’s hopes.” Then Zuo’s brow tightened. “But there is a matter on which I must consult you, Mr Xueyan. Have you a clever way of dealing with those verminous clerks?” Hu did not understand the expression and stared, unable to answer. Only after Zuo explained further did he realise that he meant the clerks in the Ministries of Revenue and War in Beijing.

“The clerks in those two ministries long for the Longhairs’ defeat more eagerly than anyone. To them, the end of the Hong-Yang rebellion means the arrival of their chance to grow rich. On the twenty-first of the first month, Zeng the Ninth took Tianbao Fort. Jinling is encircled, and Hong Xiuquan is like a doomed spirit beneath the cauldron. Li Shaoquan’s Huai Army will take Changzhou before long. Once it falls, that army can sweep west, join Feng Zicai, who has held on so stubbornly at Zhenjiang, and go through Danyang to support Zeng. It seems they may be eating Dragon Boat Festival dumplings in Jiangning.” “Not so soon!” Hu answered immediately. Zuo was startled and displeased. “Why do you say that?” Hu knew he had spoken too bluntly. Zuo had left one question unspoken: “Am I less qualified than you to judge military affairs?” Hu therefore changed his approach at once. “Your Excellency’s conduct of war is inspired. How could I presume to offer a careless opinion? But after two years of seeing and hearing things in Shanghai, I believe I understand Governor Li’s character thoroughly. Even if Changzhou is taken, he may not lead his men west. He is not foolish enough to claim a share of the great victory that Marshal Zeng the Ninth is determined to win alone.” “Ah!” Zuo struck his thigh hard. “You think so too?”
