HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 9 — Part 2 of 7

The report stated that after the fall of Jiangning, the Young Heavenly King, Hong Tianguifu, had been escorted by King Gan, Hong Rengan; King Yang, Ji Qingyuan; King Yu, Li Ruisheng; and King Yang, Li Mingcheng. They reached Guangde on the twenty-first day of the sixth month. Five days later Huang Wenjin, King Du, who held Huzhou, personally welcomed him into the city. Learning that the Loyal King, Li Xiucheng, had been captured by the imperial forces, the young ruler had appointed Hong Rengan chief military counselor. Zuo thought he could now put the Zeng brothers to shame. He immediately ordered a secretary to draft a memorial, intending to send it by express rider and expose Zeng Guofan’s false claim that the young rebel had died in the fighting. Just as he was exulting and laughing openly, Hu arrived at his temporary headquarters. On hearing the news, Hu felt obliged to dampen his pleasure with a word of caution.

“My lord, might this memorial wait a little?” “What is there to wait for? Now that the principal rebel’s whereabouts are known, how should I dare conceal them?” Zuo answered with righteous confidence. Hu knew that an appeal against quarrels among commanders as harmful to the state would go unheard. He therefore appealed to practical consequences. “We have a saying in Hangzhou: lift a stone yourself, and crush your own foot. My lord, this is a stone you must not lift. Handle it badly, and it may break your head.” “What do you mean?” “Consider what will happen when the memorial arrives. The court will naturally be delighted. ‘Excellent! You must capture the young rebel. On no account may he escape. Take him, and we shall make you a marquis.’ But suppose you fail?” “Ah! Ah!” Zuo suddenly understood. “Then I shall be the man who let the principal rebel escape!”

Hu deliberately said no more. A man as intelligent as Zuo needed only a hint, certainly; but Hu’s chief reason was another consideration. On his recent visit to Shanghai he had heard a great deal about the Zeng brothers and Li Hongzhang from their secretaries and trusted associates. Even remarks in private correspondence, which by rights no third person should ever have heard, had leaked out. Evidently Zeng and Li had spoken freely to those about them. The discreet passed the information only to people who could appreciate it; the shallow boasted of their intimacy with great officials and their access to inside news, adding flourishes until every story came vividly to life. Thus they created endless needless disputes and damaged relationships for no reason. This had made Hu wary of Zuo. His own lord was notorious for speaking without restraint. If Hu devised a scheme to sow discord between Zeng and Li, Zuo might one day tell someone exactly how he had done it. That would be curing one ailment by causing another. To offend both Zeng and Li for no good reason was hardly worthwhile. Zuo, however, had heard enough. Delighted, he kept saying, “I understand! I understand!” Hu found this less than satisfactory. He wanted a definite plan, so that he could grasp Zuo’s intention and help carry it out. He therefore asked, “Then does Your Excellency find something useful in my humble opinion?” “Humble opinion? Your judgment is excellent!” Zuo paused thoughtfully. “Still, I am not a man to change my face at a turn of the hand. Besides, Li Shaoquan has always been, to me—” He too left the sentence unfinished. Anyone familiar with his usual remarks could supply the rest: he had always despised Li Hongzhang. To seek friendship now seemed like asking a favor, and he resented it. Hu decided that open advice toward a proper end was different from privately criticizing others. Even if such words leaked out, they could stand in public without injuring his reputation. He resolved to persuade Zuo to cooperate with Li.

“My lord,” he asked deliberately, “what is the one urgent task before us?” “Do you mean public business, or my own affairs?” “It is the same in both. Two matters in one, and one in two: what is the great task that concerns both?” “Naturally, to clear all Zhejiang of rebels.” “Exactly. And there is only one obstacle left: Huzhou. Once it falls, you can report the province pacified. Then you too will be made a marquis and a grand minister.” “A grand minister is far in the future, and a marquisate is nothing remarkable. If such a grant of nobility is offered, I shall insist upon declining it.” Hu could not tell whether Zuo spoke from real feeling or affected indifference. There was no point in arguing. Only by following the direction of his words could Hu move him.

“A masterly move, my lord!” Hu raised his thumb. “To think nothing of a marquisate shows that wealth and rank are no more to you than passing clouds. That places you above both Grand Secretary Zeng and Governor Li. But unless the court first offers the honor, how can you display such superior character?” “There is truth in that.” Zuo nodded deeply. At last he had yielded a little, and Hu could breathe freely. How cooperation with Li was to be arranged no longer required Hu’s attention. Zuo understood the whole situation, and military strategy was not a subject on which Hu could presume to speak. He merely reminded Zuo of one point: Li had disobeyed the court’s orders to assist at Jiangning and now urgently wished to make amends. Even if Zuo refused cooperation, Li would send troops toward Huzhou on his own, demonstrating obedience to the repeatedly stated principle that provincial authorities suppressing rebels must not let territorial boundaries divide their efforts. Zuo, moreover, truly needed his support.

The first reason was manpower. Huzhou had become the refuge of the rebels of the southeast. Their scattered armies and defeated commanders had gathered there in numbers several times greater than Zuo’s forces. Pressed too hard, they would fight like trapped beasts and must not be underestimated. The second reason was terrain. Around Huzhou, waterways branched in every direction, offering ambush positions everywhere. The place was naturally easy to defend and difficult to attack. When Zhao Jingxian had held the isolated city, he had adapted his defense to the ground and used it to the utmost. Its present rebel commander, Huang Wenjin, was no weakling either. Acting in the Young Heavenly King’s name, he could issue orders readily. Reports also said that Huzhou held enough grain for a year, placing him in a much better position than Zhao had enjoyed. Zuo could overcome neither obstacle alone; only Li could help him surmount both. In manpower, the assistance of the Jiangsu forces was needed to complete the investment of Huzhou—not, of course, a four-sided encirclement like Zeng Guoquan’s siege of Jinling. Even if enough troops could be deployed for that, it would be unwise. Since ancient times, besiegers had often left one side open. Give the enemy no way to live, and he must fight to the death. Even if annihilated, he would inflict terrible losses. Leave an outlet, by contrast, and the hope of survival would weaken his will to resist. Moreover, ambushes could be laid along a carefully prepared escape route. That was the better calculation.

As for terrain, the first vital position outside Huzhou was Daqiankou, opening northward onto Lake Tai. Zhao Jingxian’s loss of Daqian on a snowy night had led to the fall of the city. The circumstances were much the same now: Huzhou could not be taken until Daqian was seized. It was far easier for the Jiangsu troops to cross Lake Tai from the north than for Zuo’s forces to march around to it. Most importantly, an attack on Daqian required a naval force—the Jiangsu army’s strength and Zuo’s weakness. Li would naturally use that strength to the full, both to atone for remaining idle at Changzhou and to justify his newly bestowed title. After his long talk with Hu, Zuo considered matters quietly. In local advantages and connections, his position was inferior to Li’s. Unless he could undertake the whole task himself and promise to capture Huzhou unaided within a fixed time, he could not prevent Li from advancing against it from the north. Without cooperation, the two armies would compete for glory. If Li lost the contest, little harm was done to him. If Zuo lost and allowed his guest to displace him as host, the reputation of a lifetime would be ruined. After much thought, Zuo decided that the best course was to achieve his purpose with Li’s assistance. If he fulfilled his duties as host, the visiting army would have to show him courtesy at every turn. Even if the Jiangsu troops entered Huzhou first, Li could scarcely send his own independent memorial. Provided the report of victory went through Zuo’s hands, he could control the account of the achievements.

His mind made up, Zuo would for the moment be a great man capable of bending as well as standing firm. He personally wrote Li a most earnest letter, discussing how to sweep away the remaining rebels of the southeast and hinting at his need for assistance. Li responded handsomely, promising to commit all four of his “Guo, Liu, Pan, and Yang armies” to Huzhou. These commands—Guo Songlin’s, Liu Mingchuan’s, Pan Dingxin’s, and Yang Dingxun’s—formed the backbone of the Anhui Army. In fact, Li sent more than those four. Liu Bingzhang, who had joined the army from the Hanlin Academy, was also ordered to assist, as was the naval force of Huang Yisheng, Jiangnan’s provincial military commander and a close fellow townsman of Zeng Guofan. They would advance by different routes. Li’s intentions broadly resembled Zuo’s. He meant to make a great display and treat the Huzhou campaign as scarcely less important than the recovery of Jinling. Like a gambler placing a wager, he hoped to capture the young rebel and uncover a hoard of treasure; at the same time, he wished to dampen the Hunan Army’s elation.

The rebels at Huzhou claimed two hundred thousand men and probably had at least sixty percent of that number. Zuo’s and Li’s regular forces together numbered no fewer than eighty thousand; with civilian officials, laborers, and other followers, the total exceeded a hundred thousand. The sides were roughly matched, and fierce fighting was expected. Yet the outcome was virtually predetermined, and even the general course of the rebel retreat could be foreseen. The ground constrained the directions of advance. Zuo’s troops would press from the southeast and southwest, while the Jiangsu forces attacked from northeast and northwest and moved directly south to block Daqiankou, preventing escape onto Lake Tai. East of Huzhou lay the richest country in the southeast, heavily defended; beyond it was the coast, offering no way out. The rebels’ only escape lay westward. If they broke through Guangde, they might join Li Shixian and Wang Haiyang in Jiangxi, both famous fighting commanders, and perhaps prolong their existence a little. A battlefield was like a chessboard. Deception flourished not only between enemies, but among allies competing against one another. Li did, after all, make a move for his own advantage. He sent Liu Mingchuan’s twenty battalions, one after another, toward the Zhejiang–Anhui border, ostensibly to attack Guangde and cut the rebels’ retreat. His real aim was to intercept Huang Wenjin, capture the young ruler, and seize the baggage train.

Huzhou was finally recovered on the twenty-sixth day of the seventh month. As predicted, Huang opened the western gate and fled. The main rebel forces escaped west in three columns. At Guangde they divided again into two routes: one led into southern Anhui; the other, taken by Huang with the young ruler, went through Ningguo, over the western Tianmu Mountains, and by Kaihua and Yushan into Jiangxi. Liu Mingchuan pursued relentlessly. The other armies, eager for credit, also pressed forward with all their strength. After five days and nights of pursuit, the rebels were scattered beyond cohesion, and Huang died amid the fighting. Hong Tianguifu, however, was still missing. The more credible reports said that he was moving south through Jiangxi to join Li Shixian and Wang Haiyang on the Guangdong–Fujian border. From there they intended to turn west toward Hubei, link up with King Fu, Chen Decai, and pass from the Jingzhou–Xiangyang region into Shaanxi, establishing themselves anew in Guanzhong. This was certainly a hopeful calculation. Yet even if it failed, such ceaseless movement was dangerous: a junction with the Nian rebels of Anhui and Henan would give grave cause for concern. The court was therefore deeply angered that repeated, relentless pursuit had still failed to capture the young rebel and bring him to justice. Zuo was angrier still. His memorial announcing “all Zhejiang cleared of rebels” had already gone forward, but with Hong Tianguifu uncaptured, the achievement remained incomplete. For the time being, he could hardly expect his grant of nobility.

Having disposed of the claim of “a hundred thousand,” Zuo turned to responsibility for impeaching negligent subordinates, and his language grew still sharper: “As for the statement that the entire enemy force escaped from Hangzhou and no impeachments were heard of, this is particularly incomprehensible. Jinling had long been completely surrounded; Hangzhou had not been surrounded at all. From Jinling came the report, ‘All the rebels have been killed.’ From Hangzhou came the report, ‘The principal rebels have indeed escaped.’” Those two sentences alone had the force of an experienced magistrate’s judgment: Zeng Guoquan had been responsible for preventing escape, and Zeng Guofan had falsely reported the military situation. At the close, however, the pen turned again with calculated subtlety. “In military affairs, truth and substance are of the first importance; I have therefore had no choice but to explain. In all future public business I shall continue to consult and cooperate harmoniously. I would not dare harbor the slightest prejudice and thereby increase my own fault.” These were needles wrapped in silk. Cautious and modest on the surface, the words were sharp beneath; every sentence contained a concealed accusation against Zeng Guofan.

Within half a month of the memorial’s dispatch, a reply came. Issued through Prince Gong, the confidential court dispatch was extraordinarily tactful. Instead of saying that Zuo could not yet be ennobled, it declared: “Since entering Zhejiang, Zuo Zongtang has recovered dozens of cities and fortified positions and cleared the whole province. His achievement is very great. We had intended at once to bestow a substantial reward, but feared that, with the young rebel Hong still undestroyed, the governor-general would insist upon declining it. As soon as the remaining rebels are eliminated, a decree of favor will be issued.” This clearly implied that Zuo’s ennoblement was only a matter of time. There was gentle reassurance concerning his dispute with Zeng as well. “The court does not wish to press meritorious servants over minor details. When the young rebel Hong entered Zhejiang, the governor-general reported the facts; when he left the province, troops were sent in pursuit. Both were proper actions. His declaration that he will continue to consult harmoniously with Zeng Guofan in public affairs, harboring no prejudice, particularly accords with the bearing of a great minister and deserves high praise. The court’s expectations of him are great and far-reaching. Let him exert himself still further and become an eminent minister of his age, fulfilling our hopes.” The edict did not reproach Zeng, but its judgment of right and wrong was clear. The promise that Zuo might be “an eminent minister of his age” was especially rare language in an imperial decree. In this dispute of memorials, submitted to the sovereign’s judgment, Zuo had gained the advantage on every point. Naturally, his enmity with Zeng deepened as well.

On the surface, the quarrel between Zeng and Zuo was a war of words; beneath it lay a fierce struggle for territory. Caught between them, and at the same time bearing the first impact, was Zeng Guoquan. His substantive appointment was Governor of Zhejiang. In wartime, to encourage commanders, appointments had not followed ordinary administrative boundaries. A senior official of one province leading troops in another was nothing unusual. Once a phase of the war ended, however, the situation changed. By ordinary reasoning, whatever faults Zeng Guoquan might have committed after taking Jiangning, his distinction in a hundred battles was undeniable. The proper course was to let him assume his governorship. But Zuo, Governor-General of Fujian and Zhejiang and acting Governor of Zhejiang, had no intention of giving way. Zeng Guofan requested sick leave for his brother partly out of anxiety over slander and criticism, seeking prudent retirement at the height of success. More fundamentally, he had perceived the hidden uncertainty of his brother’s position. Judging that Guoquan could never take up the post, he thought it better to recognize the situation and withdraw voluntarily.

The court was in a dilemma. On one hand, military operations in the southeast had culminated in the recovery of Huzhou and the pacification of Zhejiang, so Zuo’s dignity had to be considered. On the other, to let a newly created earl lay down his arms and retire to the countryside seemed an improper way to treat a meritorious servant. The request for Zeng Guoquan’s retirement on grounds of illness was therefore left unresolved. The court hoped that Zeng and Zuo might put aside their resentment and be reconciled, with Zuo himself requesting permission to relinquish the acting governorship and an order for Guoquan to take office. That hope was impossible to fulfill. At last the court saw that indefinite delay would not do and agreed to Guofan’s wish, allowing Guoquan to resign. Who should fill the vacancy, however, required careful thought. The court knew what Zuo wanted: Jiang Yili promoted from provincial treasurer to governor, with the treasury vacancy filled on Zuo’s recommendation. But Jiang lacked sufficient seniority and standing; such an arrangement would also humiliate Zeng Guofan. In balancing its commanders, the court would not lightly give the impression of favoring one man. Zuo’s wishes were therefore ruled out.

Three considerations remained. First, the new Governor of Zhejiang must be honest, experienced, and capable. Of all the provinces ravaged by Hong Xiuquan and Yang Xiuqing, Zhejiang had suffered most, and its reconstruction would be the hardest. Nothing less than integrity and practical ability would suffice. Second, he should have neither obligations nor grievances toward Zuo, while enjoying the support of Zeng Guofan and even Li Hongzhang, so that Zhejiang could obtain its neighbors’ help. Third, with the great rebellion suppressed, arms were to give way to civil government. This would be the first provincial governorship filled since the defeat of the Taipings, marking the restoration of civil rule. The appointee should preferably have risen through the examinations. Military achievements as well would make him ideal. An exceptionally suitable man was eventually chosen: Ma Xinyi, courtesy name Gushan. His ancestors were Hui Muslims who had helped the founding Ming emperor win the empire. Appointed military officers in a Shandong garrison, they settled in Heze County, Caozhou Prefecture. More than four hundred years had passed. Apart from his adherence to Islam, Ma was thoroughly a native son of Shandong.
