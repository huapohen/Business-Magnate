HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 9 — Part 5 of 7

They next turned to the business Jiang Yili had entrusted to Hu: Zhejiang’s monthly contribution to the army in Fujian. “Zhejiang formerly depended on Fujian for more than three million taels,” Hu began. “Now that Zhejiang troops are going to its aid, Zhejiang should naturally support them. What arrangement do you propose, my lord? We should know early enough to prepare.” “I have settled it with Xiangquan: Zhejiang will send me two hundred thousand a month.” “Two hundred thousand is not much. The trouble is that Zhejiang’s strength has been so badly drained.” Hu deliberately paused, then asked without warning, “Once you reach Fujian, do you intend to request the transfer of Jiang and Yang to assist you?” Zuo was baffled. “I have no such intention. Both have worked their way up to senior provincial office. For one thing, Fujian has no suitable positions; for another, the court might not approve. Besides, why should I clear the road for Ma Gushan, vacating two important posts so that he can bring in his own men?” Hu had expected this answer, particularly the last point. Keeping Jiang as provincial treasurer and recommending Yang Changjun as judicial commissioner was a calculated move to restrain Ma and protect Zuo’s revenue in Zhejiang. Why would Zuo surrender that advantage? Hu had asked precisely to lead the conversation toward his real purpose. “Exactly. If Jiang and Yang remain secure in their posts, it will be almost as though you still held the acting governorship. But I fear I must offer some unwelcome advice.” “Go ahead. There is nothing you and I cannot discuss. Besides, I know it will be for my good.”

“Yes, I am thinking of your interests: a small but steady stream, a firm and lasting arrangement,” Hu replied calmly. “Zhejiang’s income is not merely limited; it cannot yet be estimated with certainty. The land-tax payments have been remitted by imperial order. Salt revenue will not improve before late spring or early summer next year. The rice levy depends upon neighboring provinces’ willingness to help. Only likin can be relied upon. As trade revives, receipts should increase, though even that depends upon the honesty of the collectors. On the expenditure side, reconstruction comes first, and the sea dikes second; both require great sums. Government offices and civil and military salaries must also be provided for, and the province must maintain some troops of its own. Suppose two hundred thousand taels is scraped together each month and sent first to your supply bureau, leaving the provincial treasury bare and all other work at a standstill. How could Jiang Xiangquan remain treasurer?” “Surely—” Zuo was silent for a while. “Surely things cannot be as difficult as you suggest?” “Of course. I have overstated them a little. But consider Governor Ma too. Having just risen to provincial authority, he naturally wishes to accomplish something. If he is hampered on every side, what can he do but press Jiang? And to press Jiang is to press you.” Hu paused. “Governor Shen of Jiangxi owed his advancement entirely to Grand Secretary Zeng. Yet he withheld his province’s likin revenues when he chose, and the court did not blame him for delaying the Zeng brothers’ eastern campaign. Governor Ma may not be as fierce as Shen, but neither is he content merely to drift.” The example of Shen Baozhen’s rupture with Zeng put Zuo on his guard. No one paid closer attention to practical interests. Considered calmly, Ma’s position was secure and offered no weakness to attack. If a dispute arose, Zuo might not prevail. Jiang would bear the first blow; once removed from Zhejiang, how could he provide two hundred thousand a month? With this thought, Zuo asked in a more even temper, “Then what do you advise, Xueyan?”

“The amount must be reduced,” Hu answered directly. “By how much?” “I dare not fix a figure. You must ask the officers and men to bear some hardship for the present. Once matters improve, they can be compensated.” “Very well.” Zuo nodded. “I too cannot bear to overburden Zhejiang. As you suggest, I shall have the supply bureau recalculate and reduce the sum as far as possible. But you know my position. I have always been isolated and unsupported. I must open a way forward.” “Yes,” Hu answered without expression. “You must help me greatly. How do you think I should find that way?” “Have you not already decided, my lord?” He meant the plan to gain control of Guangdong. Zuo frowned slightly. “Removing Guo is not difficult. The question is who can replace him. Xiangquan still lacks the standing for a governorship. If my recommendation is rejected, I shall find it hard to speak again. The consequences are serious. I cannot act rashly before I am sure. Yet I cannot tell Xiangquan this.” Hu listened closely, searching beneath the words. If Jiang wanted Guangdong, he would need another source of support. In other words, if influential patrons at court could ensure that the recommendation would succeed, Zuo would gladly submit it.

Hu then asked himself whether he should help Jiang and whether he could. The first question required little thought: Jiang’s promotion to Guangdong would bring great public and private advantages. As for whether help was possible, it was too early to say. Much depended upon effort; if he did his utmost, there was hope. “You are a pillar of the court, my lord, and have always enjoyed great imperial favor,” he said. “In Shanghai I heard from people newly arrived from Beijing that Prince Gong values you highly, and Prince Chun admires you still more. Grand Secretary Zeng was allowed to recommend governors for provinces within his military command. Why should you not have the same right? As for Xiangquan’s standing, promotion from Treasurer of Zhejiang to Governor of Guangdong would be no extraordinary leap. Governor Ma is an example ready to hand. Guangdong is different because its maritime customs revenues are closely connected with the Imperial Household Department. Still, if someone in Beijing looks after the matter, there is hope.” “Exactly—someone in Beijing must look after it. Xiangquan is at a disadvantage there.” “It is not too late to begin tending those neglected fires.” Zuo looked at him intently, pondered for some time, then finally said, “You might discuss it with Xiangquan.” “Yes.”

“He must rely on you,” Zuo continued. “And I can do without you even less. You command no troops for me and run no supply bureau, yet your work is more important than either. Once I leave, you must set out promptly and remain in Shanghai. If the supply bureau falls short—whether of pay, grain, or military equipment—you alone will be my resource.” The responsibility weighed heavily upon Hu, but he could show no hesitation. He braced himself. “Yes, my lord. You may be at ease.” “With that assurance, I truly can.” Zuo breathed out, then asked, “What would you like me to do for you? I intend to leave at the end of the month; there is still half a month. If you want anything, tell me early.” Hu had already considered this. After Zuo left, Jiang would hold the acting governor’s seal and could still be consulted; yet a request to Zuo would be more direct and effective. During their half-year together, Hu had asked no favor. He must not miss the opportunity now, especially when Zuo himself had invited him to speak. Further silence would seem affected and false, and would hardly be wise.

He decided to speak freely, first retreating a little in order to advance. “There are many things I wish to ask, but I fear you may find them tiresome.” “Not at all!” Zuo waved both hands. “I am always asking you for help and owe you many favors. Speak.” “First, the former Governor Wang died so tragically. You upheld justice, investigated what had happened, and reported the facts to the throne. But the case is not finished. I should like you to see it through.” “Oh?” More than two years had passed, and Zuo’s recollection was vague. “In what way is it unfinished?”

“I mean your memorial in the fourth month of the first Tongzhi year, reporting the inquiry into Wang Lüqian’s obstruction and failures—” “Ah!” Reminded, Zuo said, “Wait a moment.” He lifted his riding jacket and took a key from his belt. Keys signified authority. In institutions as great as the Shenji Camp or the Imperial Household Department, to be designated keeper of the keys meant being entrusted with leadership. In a wealthy household, a steward—or Wang Xifeng in Dream of the Red Chamber—likewise displayed practical power by holding the keys. A key was small, however, insufficiently imposing to proclaim such authority. Hence the additions: usually quantity made the impression. Useless keys were tied together, sometimes threaded on a large iron ring that clattered in the hand. The intention resembled a jailer’s: shake the bunch, and every prisoner would submit. Yet the truly weighty key was often a single one. Zuo had only one, threaded on a silk cord at his belt. Pulling it out, he leaned toward a book chest, unlocked it, and removed a thick stack of red booklets. From a distance Hu could read the large characters on their covers: “Copies of Memorials.” Zuo found the booklet for the fourth month of the first Tongzhi year, put on his smoky-crystal reading spectacles, and studied it before asking, “What remains unsettled, Xueyan?”

“If you look at the reply received then, my lord, you will see.” The reply could not readily be found. “You must know what it said. Tell me.” “Yes.” Hu now felt awkward.

When Wang Youling had held Hangzhou through the siege, his principal source of funds had been Shaoxing. Wang Lüqian, the militia commissioner residing there, had been uncooperative. Unscrupulous local gentry, skilled with their pens, surrounded him and portrayed the governor’s demands for military funds as callous extortion for private gain. Officials and people had almost imperceptibly become enemies. This made the position of Prefect Liao Zongyuan extremely difficult. When the rebels advanced from Xiaoshan toward Shaoxing, imperial gunboats actually clashed with the local militia. Badly outnumbered, Liao’s bodyguards lost twelve men, and he himself suffered a head wound. Wang Lüqian should have intervened and restored order, but stood aside. Soon Shaoxing fell and Liao died at his post. Wang Lüqian had already fled to Ningbo and escaped by sea to Fujian. The needless loss of Shaoxing, together with Wang’s constant obstruction and disregard of the larger cause, filled Governor Wang with bitter hatred. In his blood-written letter from the doomed city, he said that he could not “close his eyes in death.” Hu too had never forgotten and wished to avenge him.

Even without Hu’s intervention, of course, the court would have investigated responsibility for Hangzhou’s fall and would not let Wang Lüqian escape punishment. In the spring of the following year, the first Tongzhi year, Qingrui, Governor-General of Fujian and Zhejiang, was ordered to arrest Wang and send him to Quzhou for interrogation by the new Zhejiang governor, Zuo. Zuo recommended penal military exile to Xinjiang; the throne approved, giving some satisfaction to Governor Wang’s memory. But the chief offender in the affair was Zhang Cunhao, a wealthy Shaoxing gentleman. He had led the false accusations that Liao’s gunboats were in league with the rebels, the killing of the guards, and the assault on the prefect. Zuo’s memorial stated that Zhang and the others, enraged by Liao’s urgent demands for contributions, had acted from private resentment and committed unforgivable offenses. Once Shaoxing was recovered, they should be arrested and punished to the full extent of the law. Now not merely Shaoxing but all Zhejiang had been cleared. Yet no arrest had followed. That was what Hu meant by the unfinished case.

His present difficulty arose from a sudden reluctance to be harsh. Amid the disorders of the age, who had failed whom, and who owed what to whom? Such questions were never simple. Years had passed, and everyone had survived a great catastrophe. Perhaps they ought now to be calmer and more forgiving. Zuo could not guess this change of heart. “We have many matters to discuss,” he urged. “Take them one by one, quickly. We must not waste time.” Hu had to tell the truth. As he spoke, another thought occurred to him. After explaining his original intention, he added, “You might show mercy and spare Zhang Cunhao, but he should not get off too easily. Let him make amends through some useful service. Could he be required to contribute to a public undertaking?” “Certainly. What are his means?” “He was wealthy before, and I hear he is still comfortably off.” “Then I shall tell Xiangquan to instruct the Shaoxing prefect to summon him and require a contribution proportionate to his means for some local work of merit.” “That will satisfy both public justice and private feeling. As for those loyal men who died during the two disasters, the Reconstruction Bureau is collecting their histories and reporting them in turn. I hope you will memorialize promptly, to comfort the dead.” “Naturally. I shall attend to that before leaving. Now tell me the second matter.”

The second was important in both public and private affairs. Since Ma’s appointment had begun to change Zhejiang’s political arrangements, Hu had wished to stop acting as banker to the provincial treasury. Jiang would not release him. At the slightest mention, he clasped his hands repeatedly and begged Hu to “continue helping.” Hu valued personal feeling too much to refuse and had reluctantly carried on. “Things are different now,” he said, after explaining. “You have ordered me to remain in Shanghai. If grain, money, or arms are urgently needed, I alone must answer. The responsibility is too great for me to continue serving Zhejiang’s treasury as well.” By “serving,” he meant finding advances whenever receipts failed to cover immediate needs. Zuo understood, but saw it differently. “Zhejiang’s treasury is to remit a hundred and forty thousand taels to me each month through your bank. If you remain its banker, it will be merely a transfer from your left hand to your right. It costs no trouble. Why refuse so firmly? In my view, your continuing in that role can only benefit me. Whenever funds arrive, they can be remitted. If the front urgently needs money, you can arrange it more easily.” “No,” Hu replied. “First, once I have been transferred at your request for service in Fujian, it is improper for me to continue acting for Zhejiang’s treasury. Second, precisely because I am managing your supplies, I must keep my dealings with Zhejiang clearly separate. If someone raises objections later, your reputation will not be involved.” “I am deeply obliged. You think of my interests at every turn. Since you insist, I shall tell Xiangquan.”

The promise lifted a weight from Hu. He alone saw the entire situation clearly. Although the contribution for Fujian had been reduced by sixty thousand taels, the remaining sum was still an immense burden. The new governor would naturally have plans of his own. Even if he could not restore everything at once, the sea dikes alone required a large expenditure. Frost’s Descent had already passed, the season when river officials reported that the waters were safely under control. At the beginning of the new year, however, work must start immediately. From the spring “peach-blossom flood” onward, rising waters at the turn of spring and summer might bring catastrophe. Would it be easy to act as banker to the treasury then? Hu could plead inability to advance the cost of the dikes. But if Ma said, “Then let Intendant Hu’s bank advance the contribution to Fujian,” could he refuse, on either public or private grounds? In truth, even after relinquishing the treasury business he would still have to advance funds for Zuo. The difference lay in how the transaction was understood. As the treasury’s banker, he would be expected by Zhejiang to find the money. Zuo, meanwhile, would regard it as an advance for Zhejiang and owe him no gratitude. Hu would please neither side. If instead Zhejiang, unable to remit, privately asked him for an advance, his action would be a public-spirited favor: Ma would be grateful, and Zuo would acknowledge his friendship. Most importantly, a formal loan from his bank to the provincial treasury would carry security and interest, leaving him no risk to bear. “What else is there?” Zuo asked. “You might as well tell me everything now.”
