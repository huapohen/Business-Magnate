HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Red-Topped Merchant

Chapter 10 — Part 1 of 3

CHAPTER TEN

At the end of the tenth month of the third year of Tongzhi, Zuo Zongtang relinquished his concurrent duties as acting governor of Zhejiang. Amid cannon salutes and the respectful farewell of Hangzhou’s civil and military officials, he set out for Fujian to direct the campaign. The relief armies had already departed in three columns. The western column’s main force comprised eight thousand newly raised troops under Liu Dian, Zhejiang’s judicial commissioner and assistant director of military affairs in Fujian. With twenty-five hundred men under Wang Debang, who held the designation of judicial commissioner pending appointment, they entered Tingzhou from Jianchang in Jiangxi. In the centre, Huang Shaochun, designated for appointment as provincial military commander, and Deputy Brigade Commander Liu Mingdeng led forty-six hundred men from Quzhou through Pucheng and Jianning towards Yanping. In the east, Acting Zhejiang Military Commander Gao Liansheng and Wei Guangbing, a prefect awaiting appointment, led forty-five hundred men across the Qiantang River to Ningbo, where they boarded steamships for Fuzhou. All three columns were directed towards southern Fujian. Li Shixian occupied Zhangzhou, west of Xiamen; Ding Taiyang was at Wuping, where Fujian, Guangdong, and Jiangxi met; Wang Haiyang ranged between the eastern and western districts of southern Fujian. Zuo was determined to prevent them from putting to sea. He would tighten the pressure from north, west, and east, forcing them south. South of Fujian lay Guangdong. Mao Hongbin, governor-general of Liangguang, and Guo Songtao, governor of Guangdong, saw the danger. If Zuo drove the rebels into their province, the relieving army would follow, leaving Guangdong squeezed between rebels and “friendly troops.” They were deeply alarmed. Yet the Guangdong forces under the two deputy brigade commanders Fang Yao and Zhuo Xing amounted to only eight thousand. Kang Guoqi, intendant of the Yanping-Jianning-Shaowu circuit in Fujian, was a Guangdong man and commanded a newly formed force largely recruited there, but it could not be counted as a Guangdong army: he served under Zuo and took no orders from Guangdong’s senior officials. Mao and Guo had little choice. They sent Fang and Zhuo into Fujian to join the campaign, openly blocking the rebels and covertly checking Zuo. Meanwhile, hoping to strengthen their own position, they planned to ask the court to appoint Feng Zicai, the celebrated defender of Zhenjiang, to direct military affairs along the East River.

Before the relieving armies arrived, Lin Wencha, Fujian’s land-force military commander, had already fought Li Shixian. A native of Changhua in Taiwan, Lin had entered military office as a youji commander in the eighth year of Xianfeng, after contributing funds and helping suppress bandits in Tamsui. His Taiwanese volunteers were fierce, adept with firearms, and effective in battle. Their success helped him rise to brigade commander and gain the title Baturu. While Wang Youling was besieged in Hangzhou, Lin had been ordered to assist Zhejiang but was held up at Hengzhou. Later placed under Zuo’s command, he earned further distinction and received substantive appointment as commander of the Funing Brigade in Fujian. Soon promoted to the province’s land-force military command, he returned to Taiwan with troops to suppress disorder in his native district. The rebel leader was Dai Chaochun, whose ancestral home was Longxi in Zhangzhou. He belonged to the surviving adherents of the White Lotus sect, that old source of rebellion which never seemed to disappear from Chinese history. Nominally he organized militia in Changhua; in reality he was in contact with the Taipings. At the transition from the Xianfeng to the Tongzhi reign, Zhejiang fell and many government troops in Fujian were transferred to northern Fujian and southern Zhejiang. Dai judged the moment favourable. In the third month his follower Lin Gangsheng rose at Dadun. Five days later Changhua was occupied and Kong Zhaoci, Taiwan’s military intendant, was killed. Dai called himself the Eastern King and Lin the Southern King; there were also Western and Northern Kings, with a Grand Preceptor, chief ministers of the left and right, and ministers of the six boards beneath them. This rough imitation of the Taiping administration, a collection of pretenders playing ministers and generals as though on a stage, survived for a time because the southeastern war had reached a critical point. In response to Grain Intendant Ding Rijian’s urgent requests, the court had sent only six hundred men against it. Not until the autumn of the second year of Tongzhi, when Zuo was confident of recovering Zhejiang, did he send Lin back to Taiwan to rally his former troops. Fujian’s governor, Xu Zonggan, also sent Ding, who had served in Taiwan for many years, with reinforcements. Ding was appointed Taiwan’s military intendant and given charge of military affairs throughout the island.

Early in the eleventh month, Changhua was recovered, followed by Douliu. By the end of the year Dai had been captured at Zhangcuozhuang and Lin Gangsheng defeated and killed at Sikuai Cuo. The situation could be considered stable. Clearing out the remaining rebels was nevertheless arduous. In particular, after Li Shixian occupied Zhangzhou, Dai’s surviving followers prepared to join forces with him and rise again. This was precisely why Zuo feared that Li and Wang Haiyang might put to sea. Lin Wencha faced a dilemma. On one hand, he could not relax his efforts to prevent the embers of rebellion from flaring up. On the other, as Fujian’s highest military officer on land, he had an inescapable responsibility for recovering Zhangzhou, Tingzhou, and the other occupied districts. After careful thought, he decided that he must return to Fujian. If Li could be destroyed, the rebels in Changhua would lose both support and hope and collapse without a fight. He crossed hurriedly, taking only two hundred personal guards in his ship. Having fought Li more than once, he did not underestimate him. His original intention was to make arrangements after reaching Fujian and only then advance. But Li was already prepared, with an ambush at Wansong Pass awaiting his arrival.

Lin then changed his plan. Feeling his force too small, he gathered many stragglers along the road into an improvised army. Given ample pay and provisions, sufficient time, and capable assistants, such men could gradually be trained into a strong force. Without those advantages, the only course was to exploit their urgent desire for a livelihood or their thirst for revenge, sharpen their fighting spirit, and stake everything on one desperate effort. An experienced commander, Lin studied the circumstances and concluded that he must seek a rapid decision. Delay would merely consume supplies while discipline became impossible, and the army would collapse without fighting. Zuo’s original order had been to hold Quanzhou and Xiamen firmly. That was a difficult task. The country from Zhangzhou eastwards to the two coastal cities was flat, easy to attack and hard to defend, while the opposing forces were grossly unequal in strength. Strategically, attack should serve as defence. In weighing the consequences, although attacking a larger force was arduous, defeat while attacking was preferable to defeat while merely holding a position. Early in the tenth month, therefore, Lin advanced from Quanzhou and Xiamen and camped at Wansong Pass. Also called Wansong Ridge, it stood on Phoenix Mountain, twenty-five li east of Zhangzhou, commanding the route into the city after crossing the river from the coastal towns. His camp was scarcely established when Li sent a force to test its strength. Deputy Brigade Commander Huishou proved incapable, and the rebels actually overran the camp. Lin had to withdraw to a place called Yuzhou. Two days later, he attacked and returned with a minor victory.

At this point another order arrived from Zuo: dig deep trenches and raise high ramparts; avoid rash fighting in pursuit of victory; wait for the Zhejiang troops, then cooperate in recovering Zhangzhou. Lin now had to obey. He encamped on Wansong Ridge and waited. Naval Brigade Commander Zeng Yuming established a river camp at Haikou near Haicheng County on the Jiulong River, so that the two positions could support one another. They held on until the end of the tenth month. Zuo had not yet entered Fujian, but the Zhejiang forces sent ahead were nearing positions from which the three columns could close in. Li had originally laid his ambush west of Wansong Pass, expecting Lin to enter it. Seeing him remain inactive while the Zhejiang troops approached, Li urgently needed to open a way out. On the third day of the eleventh month, he launched a surprise attack by land and water. The rebels striking the river camp blackened their faces with soot, both to appear terrifying and to recognize one another. Zeng’s naval force was caught unprepared. Apart from resistance with the small guns aboard the gunboats, the camps collapsed. To the west, Lin’s force at Wansong Pass consisted of stragglers whose condition had steadily deteriorated with delay. Hearing that their rear had been attacked, they fell into disorder before fighting began. Huishou and Youji Commander Xu Zhongbiao could not hold the ranks and escaped as best they could. Lin refused to flee and was shot dead. The routed troops were barely collected on the east bank of the Jiulong River, forming what protection they could for the approach to Quanzhou.

Three months later, Zuo reached Pucheng and formally entered Fujian, intending to establish his travelling headquarters there. The first battle report received at those headquarters announced Lin’s defeat and death in action. This was no omen of immediate success, and Zuo was greatly displeased. In his view, Lin had blunted the Zhejiang army’s spirit and damaged his own prestige. Although the man had not deserved death, his failure was unforgivable. Zuo therefore would not devote a separate memorial to it. Instead, under the heading “Arrival at Pucheng to Direct the Campaign, and Current Arrangements for Suppression,” he reproached Lin for disregarding orders and thereby suffering defeat. Fortunately, Gao Liansheng’s troops had reached southern Fujian from Fuzhou, so Quanzhou and Xiamen could be secured. As for Lin’s posthumous honours, a separate request would be submitted; it was easy to infer that the provision would not be generous. The situation soon stabilized. Zuo’s greatest concern was that Li might break southeastwards to the sea. If Gao’s force could move south from Fuzhou in time to block him and hold him briefly, the arrival by sea of the Jiangsu armies under Guo Songlin and Yang Dingxun would make the clearing of the interior virtually certain.

Zuo accordingly adopted a strategy of holding in the east, attacking from the north, and applying pressure from the west. First he recovered Longyan, in the northern part of southern Fujian. Then, in cooperation with Fang Yao’s Guangdong troops, he recovered Yongding on the provincial border. These two victories greatly raised morale and gave him more freedom of command. He began to drive the rebels into Guangdong, first pushing Wang Haiyang’s men south from Tingzhou and Liancheng, near Jiangxi, towards Wuping and Shanghang on the Guangdong border. The Jiangsu reinforcements were arriving in succession. They divided the defensive sectors with Gao’s and Huang Shaochun’s Zhejiang forces, taking Zhangzhou as their common objective. The Jiangsu armies held the south of the city, the Zhejiang armies the north, completely cutting off Li’s route to the sea. In the middle of the fourth month they attacked simultaneously from north and south. On the twenty-first, Zhangzhou was recovered. Li, however, opened the western gate and escaped to join Wang, leaving the two like cornered beasts. The strongest forces now lay along the southeastern coast around Quanzhou, Xiamen, and Zhangzhou. More than seven thousand guarded Yongding to the northwest; a gap in the northeast had also been closed in time. Only the west remained weak, with almost no dispositions made by Zuo. To the west lay Dabu and Raoping in Guangdong. Fang Yao’s troops defended them, but anyone could see they were no match for Li and Wang. What, then, did Zuo intend? A discerning observer could draw the conclusion.

That observer was Xu Gengshen, chief secretary of the Grand Council, far away in the capital. On the twelfth day of the fifth month, a memorial passed down to him caused considerable surprise. Submitted by Li Hongzhang to record his troops’ achievements, it was headed, “Jiangsu Reinforcements in Fujian Join the Zhejiang Forces in Converging Attacks and Recover the Prefectural City of Zhangzhou on the Twenty-first Day of the Fourth Month.” In describing the attack, it stated that “the rebel Shi King, Li Shixian, secretly opened the western gate and fled.” This did not agree with Zuo’s report received at the same time. Zuo’s heading was “Great Victory in the Advance West of Zhangzhou, and Current Arrangements.” It mentioned neither the recovery of the city nor Li’s escape through its western gate. It said only that Li, repeatedly defeated and increasingly desperate, had tried to take a minor route north of Zhangzhou around to Anxi and strike the government troops’ rear. Failing in that, he had attempted to stir up internal disorder in collusion with bandits in Tong’an. Guo Songlin had then hurried to Tong’an with two battalions and, together with Intendant Zeng Xiande, cleared the bandit strongholds at Xitang, Shangzhai, and Hujing. The memorial was dated the twenty-sixth day of the fourth month and submitted from the provincial capital. Fuzhou was only two or three days from Zhangzhou. If the city had been recovered on the twenty-first, Zuo could hardly have remained ignorant on the twenty-fifth. If he knew, why did he not announce the victory in his memorial on the twenty-sixth? This was a major puzzle, but a little consideration made it plain. Zuo withheld the victory report because Li had escaped. By first describing an intended movement towards Anxi, then collusion with Tong’an’s bandits, and finally Guo’s actions, he meant quietly to shift responsibility for Li’s escape onto Guo Songlin.

The reason for Zuo’s wish to deal with Guo could also be inferred. After Lin Wencha’s death, Guo, commander of the Fushan Brigade, had been appointed acting land-force military commander of Fujian. Acting or not, it was a promotion. Had Zuo not requested the Jiangsu reinforcements, Guo might never have obtained this highest provincial military office. Zuo reasoned that, since the promotion arose from service in Fujian and the post itself belonged to Fujian, both gratitude and the proper relationship between title and duty required Guo to enter his command. Yet despite repeated urgings from Governor Xu Zonggan, Guo refused to assume the appointment. He fought in Fujian while holding a Fujian military office, but continued to regard himself as a visiting commander. Zuo found that hard to tolerate. Before Guo and Yang sailed south, however, Li had expressly stated that Guo would not take up the post and that he would not urge him to do so. Zuo had agreed and could not now conveniently go back on his word. His resentment remained. He therefore planted a suggestion for future use: on one hand, his vague phrasing implied that pursuing Li Shixian was Guo’s responsibility; on the other, he could wait to see what happened. If Tong’an’s bandits proved difficult to suppress, he could formally request that Guo remain in Fujian. A province’s own military commander suppressing its own bandits was an entirely proper arrangement. The court could scarcely refuse, Li would have to release him, and Guo would have to stay.

Once Zuo’s concealed manoeuvring was understood, Li’s memorial on the relief forces’ victory and the joint recovery of Zhangzhou became clear. Besides claiming distinction and rewards, it left the Jiangsu armies a means of disclaiming responsibility in advance. Its account of the campaign was particularly precise about the sectors and dispositions of Guo’s and Yang’s forces. First, it stated that Dongshan, ten li south of Zhangzhou on the main road to Zhangpu, was held by Guo with eight battalions. Another ten li farther lay Zhenmen, centrally placed between Dongshan, Haicheng, and Shima, where Yang held the ground with five battalions. Haicheng County lay behind both armies, with mountain paths leading to Zhangpu; three more battalions had therefore been deployed inside and outside the county town to prevent a surprise attack on their rear. Second, it stated that Brigade Commander Liu Lianjie and Officer Wang Kaibang were in the northwest, while Military Commander Gao Liansheng, Huang Shaochun, and their forces were on the eastern route. Once the Jiangsu troops occupied Dongshan, the southern route was cut. Third, it recorded that the defeated rebels fled towards Nanjing County; the various battalions pursued for several li, then joined Gao, Huang, and the others in turning back southeast to clear all the rebel positions at Fangzi Bridge beyond the eastern gate, the Dongyue Temple, and the New Bridge near the southern gate. Every detail showed that, whether defending or attacking, Guo and Yang were chiefly responsible for Zhangzhou’s southern side, with its eastern side secondary. Who, then, was responsible for Li’s escape through the western gate? The answer was obvious.

After repeated examination, Xu concluded that Zuo was practising a dangerous form of political manipulation. Since the war began, provincial commanders had habitually tried to drive rebels beyond their own borders. Zuo, however, seemed deliberately to be making a neighbour bear the calamity, with some further malicious purpose concealed behind it. The matter must be corrected early. Xu explained the consequences to Prince Gong, Wenxiang, and the others. With the two empress dowagers informed, a court dispatch was drafted. It first noted Li’s report that Zhangzhou had been recovered and the rebel Shi King had secretly escaped through its western gate. It continued that, although the city was recovered, the chief rebel remained alive and would certainly join Wang Haiyang in further plans. The southeastern routes were stable and Liu Mingdeng and others held the northeast in coordination, but Zhangpu, Yunxiao, Zhao’an, and Pinghe in the west remained in rebel hands. There could be no doubt that the fugitives would try to escape that way. Fang Yao’s forces guarded Raoping and Dabu in Guangdong, but might be too few to cover the area. Zuo and his colleagues were therefore to detach strong forces, make a circuit to the western route, join the Guangdong troops, and intercept the rebels, preventing them from crossing the border. At this point, Zuo knew the moment had come to drive Guo Songtao from office. He had already laid groundwork for Jiang Yili, using Li Hongzhang as a favourable comparison. Early in the ninth month, when Ruilin and Guo fell out, Zuo had added a remark to his memorial requesting withdrawal of the order placing the armies of three provinces under his control: fighting in Liangguang might continue indefinitely, but if it had commanders such as Li Hongzhang and Jiang Yili, perhaps the disorder could be brought to an end. The implication was that Li should become governor-general there and Jiang governor of Guangdong. This was a tentative hint, and the court had given no explicit response. Yet the impression that Jiang could govern a province had been planted in the minds of the Western Empress Dowager and the grand councillors.

Zuo still could not openly recommend Jiang for promotion and transfer to Guangdong. Instead, he attacked Guo and supported Jiang in an attached memorandum on Guangdong’s military affairs and finances. It began by declaring that the province’s military condition would not bear examination, and its finances scarcely bore inquiry. The war had lasted a long time, it said; in some provinces military strength had grown from weakness, whereas Guangdong, once fierce, was now feeble. In some provinces funds had gradually recovered from scarcity, whereas Guangdong, once rich, was now exhausted. Those two sentences alone attacked the governors and governors-general of the preceding two years. Naturally, Guo bore more responsibility than Ruilin, since he had been in office longer.
