TOWERS OF LIGHT — SYNOPSIS

Hu Guangyong (1823–1885), courtesy name Xueyan, was a native of Jixi in Anhui. Towers of Light is the first instalment of a new sequence of novels by the celebrated Taiwan-based author Gao Yang, continuing Hu Xueyan and The Red-Topped Merchant. Here the author resumes the account of Hu's extraordinary life.

Hu had helped Zuo Zongtang suppress the Nian and Muslim uprisings, raising military funds, purchasing modern Western firearms and sending medicines to the armies at the front. When Zuo's campaigns were concluded, he recommended Hu to the throne for reward. In recognition of his “exceptional services,” the court granted special honours beyond the usual rules: permission to wear a yellow riding jacket and a coral button on his official hat. He had become a conspicuous “red-topped merchant.” With official influence supporting his commercial power, his career reached its summit. Drawing on his substantial capital and the high official standing acquired through contributions and “exceptional services,” Hu moved between commerce and government and cultivated powerful acquaintances. He made use of the complex rivalries and mutual manipulation within officialdom to borrow several million taels in a single transaction from British and German merchants for Zuo's enormous military expenditure, thereby gaining still more of Zuo's protection. His skilful transactions often brought immense profits overnight, earning him the name “God of Wealth.” He undertook lavish building projects, laid out gardens on a vast scale and took many concubines, living in unspeakable corruption and indulgence. Yet a tall tree catches the wind. Criticism had already gathered, and at the very height of his commercial success, Hu faced a crisis.

In The Desolate Foreign Settlement, which follows Towers of Light as its second volume and is also published by our company, Gao Yang describes the dangers surrounding Hu after his rise to the summit, his precarious position as though walking on thin ice, and the beginning of his decline.

FOREWORD

The remarkable circumstances that brought Hu Guangyong, known during the Tongzhi and Guangxu reigns as the “God of Wealth,” together with Wang Youling and Zuo Zongtang form the principal subject of my novels Hu Xueyan and The Red-Topped Merchant. Towers of Light continues The Red-Topped Merchant and tells the story of Hu's end. The summit of Hu's career coincided with the conclusion of Zuo's western campaigns, the pacification of Xinjiang and Zuo's elevation to a marquisate of the second class—the height of his lifetime's achievements. This was in the spring of the fourth year of Guangxu's reign.

One must remember the source when drinking the water. Without Hu's fundraising and logistical support, Zuo's western campaigns could not have achieved such brilliant results. On the fourteenth day of the fourth lunar month that year, Zuo and Tan Zhonglin, governor of Shaanxi, submitted a joint memorial requesting “exceptional rewards for Circuit Intendant Hu Guangyong.” They listed no fewer than nine instances of his “services.” The first five concerned donations Hu had made at his mother's instruction over the years to relieve disasters by land and water in various provinces. These earned his mother an honorary title of the senior first rank, allowing Hu to erect an imposing gatehouse at his residence on Yuanbao Street in Hangzhou. Even the governor of Zhejiang had to leave his sedan chair outside the main gate when visiting, since his own rank was only senior second.

The remaining four were Hu's actual “achievements.” First, he had founded Hu Qingyu Hall in Hangzhou, a large pharmacy whose reputation rivalled Beijing's Tongren Hall. Over the years, he had donated and dispatched the prepared medicines needed by the western armies—Zhuge Marching Powder, Biwen Pills, Shenqu and Liushen Pills—as well as plasters for bruises and injuries, wound medicines and other medicinal supplies for military use. Second, at Zuo's direction, he had established a purchasing and transport bureau in Shanghai, forwarding supplies without delay. Third, he had procured foreign firearms of good quality at reasonable prices. Whenever Western countries produced new weapons, he purchased them and sent them to the front. Zuo held that “the swift pacification of Xinjiang owed not only to the excellence of the troops but also to the effectiveness of their weapons.”

The final service was the most important: raising funds for Zuo. Besides arranging foreign and merchant loans totalling more than sixteen million taels, Hu alone managed the supporting remittances from the provinces. When these had not arrived but the front-line troops had to be paid, he often advanced the money himself. Both the Hunan and Anhui armies had repeatedly suffered mutinies over pay; only the western expeditionary forces never “made trouble over their pay.”

Zuo considered this contribution to military morale “truly no different from that of the commanders at the front.” Beforehand, he had asked Hu what reward he hoped to receive. “I should like a yellow riding jacket to wear,” Hu answered. The memorial accordingly requested that he receive “exceptional honours and permission to wear a yellow riding jacket,” and the emperor approved it. Hu was a circuit intendant who had entered official service through the contribution system. His military services brought the additional honorary title of provincial treasurer. A civil official of the junior second rank wore a coral hat button. There had been salt merchants with red hat buttons in the Qianlong reign, but a red hat button together with a yellow riding jacket belonged to Hu Xueyan alone.

In the eleventh lunar month of Guangxu's sixth year, Zuo was summoned to an imperial audience. His posts as imperial commissioner supervising military affairs in Xinjiang and governor-general of Shaanxi and Gansu were taken over, respectively, by his generals Liu Jintang and Yang Changjun. He reached the capital at the end of the first lunar month of the following year. As Grand Secretary of the Eastern Pavilion, he was ordered to administer the Ministry of War, appointed to the Grand Council and assigned duties in the Zongli Yamen, the office for foreign affairs.
