Hu Xueyan: Towers of Light

CHAPTER ONE: FROM GENERAL TO MINISTER

On the seventh day of the third lunar month in the seventh year of Guangxu, Hu Xueyan at last fulfilled his promise and arrived in Beijing. Two foreigners accompanied him: Focke, a German who had traded in China for many years and managed the Tailai Trading House, and Cameron, a representative of the British-owned Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation. The weather had been exceptionally cold that year, and the harbour at Tianjin was still frozen, so Hu had travelled overland. His imposing procession of more than ten carriages entered You’an Gate and made straight for Fukangfu Bank in Caochang Shitiao Hutong outside Qianmen. Wang Weixian, the head manager, had begun preparing for his employer’s arrival ten days earlier. As soon as the carriages stopped, Hu and his guests, together with Gu Yingchun and Secretary Yang, who handled his correspondence, were ushered into the reception room. Four bright apprentices, specially chosen for the occasion, hurried about bringing water for washing and pouring tea. Wang attended to Hu himself, seeing to his needs while asking about the journey.

When the bustle had subsided and they were seated, Baofu, one of Hu’s personal attendants, brought his gold water pipe and filled it for him. “Madam Zhang has already sent her maid to ask you to come in,” he murmured. “How can I possibly find time now?” Hu’s tone suggested that Madam Zhang ought to know better. Baofu said nothing. He merely waved a hand at a maid of sixteen or seventeen standing behind the screen, indicating that Hu could not come in yet. From south to north, in the great cities where Fukang had branches, Hu also maintained residences, most of them with a woman in charge. Madam Zhang, the concubine who occupied the rear quarters of Fukangfu, was not a particular favourite; hence his tone.

“Mr Hu,” Wang Weixian asked, “would you prefer Chinese food or Western food?” He promptly added a word about his own efforts. “I was afraid our two foreign guests might not take to Chinese dishes, so I specially borrowed a Western cook from His Excellency Wen. Everything is ready.” His Excellency Wen was Wenyu, the Minister of Punishments, a Manchu of the Plain Blue Banner who had become Fuzhou Garrison General in the seventh year of Tongzhi. After the Qing forces entered the passes, garrison generals had been stationed at strategic points throughout the country. The Fuzhou post was famously lucrative because its holder also supervised the Fujian Maritime Customs. Wenyu had held it for ten years and accumulated a very substantial official purse; he had over a million deposited with Fukang. Wang knew that he and Hu were old friends from Fuzhou and on excellent terms. He had therefore felt free to borrow the cook Wenyu had brought from Fuzhou, who could prepare Western meals, to entertain Focke and Cameron. Since everything was ready, Western food it was. Hu had wanted to ask Wang several questions, but as they were both acting as hosts, Western custom placed them at opposite ends of the long dining table, making conversation difficult. Only after dinner, when English-speaking clerks from Fukangfu took the two foreigners to see Dashilan, could Hu discuss business with Wang.

The most important matter was the purpose of Hu’s journey: to discuss with Zuo Zongtang a loan of three or four million taels of silver. Hu was eager to learn how Zuo had fared since entering the court. Did he still enjoy the same great favour from the empress dowagers behind the curtain? How did he stand with the two princes, Prince Gong, who headed the Grand Council, and Prince Chun, the Guangxu Emperor’s father? What position did he hold within the Council itself? Hu needed to understand all this thoroughly before deciding what he could say and what he had better leave unmentioned. “I don’t reckon His Excellency Zuo will stop long in the capital,” Wang said. Wang too was from Hangzhou and spoke to his employer in their native dialect, using its local word for “stay long”. The remark gave Hu a sharp shock. “Why won’t he stop long?” “His old temper has come out again: no amount of stewing will soften him, no amount of boiling will cook him.” This was another Hangzhou expression, meaning obstinately determined to have one’s own way. To support his opinion, Wang went on to describe Zuo’s conduct during Grand Council discussions of the Russian treaty and coastal defence.

When the Muslim uprising had begun in Xinjiang, Russia had sent troops to occupy Ili under the pretext of protecting its subjects. It announced that the occupation was temporary and that Ili would be returned to China as soon as the uprising had been suppressed. In his western campaign, Zuo Zongtang had recovered such important towns as Urumqi and Turfan, restoring order in succession on both sides of the Tianshan Mountains. He then raised the question of recovering Ili and called on Russia to honour its promise. Russia made one excuse after another, gradually revealing its true intention: to keep what it had supposedly borrowed. Zuo brought military pressure to bear, but neither side would yield. In the autumn of the fourth year of Guangxu, the court decided to seek a settlement through formal diplomacy. Chonghou, the Left Censor-in-Chief, was appointed imperial envoy to Russia and given the honorary title of Minister of the Inner Court. As plenipotentiary for the treaty negotiations, he was authorized to act at his own discretion. In the last lunar month of that year, Chonghou travelled through France and Germany to St Petersburg and immediately began negotiations with Giers, the Russian foreign minister. After six months they reached an agreement. Exercising his discretionary powers as plenipotentiary, Chonghou signed the Treaty of Livadia near the Black Sea. It ceded the territory west and south of Ili to Russia, provided five million roubles in compensation for military expenses, opened several additional trading ports, and allowed Russian trade at Xi’an, Hanzhong and Hankou, as well as unrestricted trade along the Songhua River as far as Boduna.

When the news reached China, public opinion erupted, denouncing Chonghou for surrendering sovereign rights and humiliating the country. He had dared sign the treaty because two powerful men stood behind him. One was Grand Councillor Shen Guifen, leader of the Southern faction at court, who could hold his own against Li Hongzao, leader of the Northern faction, and enjoyed the deep confidence of both empress dowagers. The other was Li Hongzhang, Governor-General of Zhili and Beiyang Minister, who presented himself as the heir to Zeng Guofan’s mantle. His influence in military and foreign affairs was firmly entrenched and difficult to shake. Throughout the six months of negotiations, Chonghou had regularly sought their advice by letter. Only after receiving Shen’s and Li’s approval had he felt safe in signing. He had then set off home without first requesting an imperial command, leaving his counsellor, Shao Youlian, to serve as acting envoy. Though Shen and Li both supported the Ili treaty, their motives differed. Shen believed that a continuing deadlock would oblige the court to maintain large forces on the frontier and Zuo to keep borrowing abroad. In time, the enormous military expenditure would impoverish the people and exhaust the treasury. His intention could be described as accepting a compromise to preserve the greater good. Li’s position was different and partly self-interested. First, if relations with Russia broke down and war followed, the Russian navy would surely attack Tianjin. As Beiyang Minister, he did not know what he could use to repel it. Second, Zuo’s continual foreign borrowing to extend his power was hardly something Li welcomed. Once the Ili affair was settled and Zuo brought his army home, it would be virtually the same as laying down his arms and retiring.

Unfortunately, Chonghou had conducted the negotiations so badly that both empress dowagers were furious and the scholarly world condemned him bitterly. Even the ministers serving in the Zongli Yamen, the office for foreign affairs headed by Prince Gong and Shen Guifen, thought the concessions excessive and considered revision necessary. The court accordingly removed Chonghou from his post as envoy to Russia for setting out home without awaiting an imperial order and referred him for severe disciplinary review. The Russian treaty was submitted to the Six Boards, the Nine Ministers, the Hanlin and Heir Apparent’s establishments, and the supervising secretaries and censors, for careful deliberation and a joint report. This was what the Ming dynasty had called a court deliberation. Its conclusion was that not a single provision of Chonghou’s treaty could be accepted. The two empress dowagers therefore convened an imperial conference. Cixi already intended to punish him severely; now Baolian and Huang Tifang, two of the Four Remonstrants of the Hanlin, submitted fierce attacks on him that also touched on Li Hongzhang and Prince Gong. Chonghou could no longer escape dismissal and arrest, followed by examination of his offences at the Ministry of Punishments. Though he was not thrown into prison in chains, he was confined in the heated room of the Ministry’s Prison Office. It was easy to imagine how each day there must have seemed a year.

This treatment conflicted with international law, and the foreign envoys protested as one, but the court remained unmoved. It appointed Zeng Jize, Zeng Guofan’s eldest son and currently imperial envoy to Britain and France, to serve concurrently as envoy to Russia and seek a revised treaty. At the same time, Chonghou was sentenced to beheading, to await review in prison. Yet the court had not abandoned hope of a peaceful settlement. Alongside its preparations for war, Li Hongzhang induced the British, French and German envoys to mediate. Chonghou’s death sentence was remitted, though he remained confined. Only then, in the sixth lunar month of the sixth year of Guangxu, did Zeng leave London for Russia to revise Chonghou’s treaty. The negotiations were bound to be difficult at first. Fortunately, Zeng proved worthy of his distinguished father. Drawing on his knowledge of international law, his diplomatic experience and his connections, he enlisted the help of the British and French envoys in Russia. Two days before Zuo Zongtang reached Beijing, he and Giers agreed on a revised draft: Ili would be recovered; trade through Jiayu Pass would be allowed without specifying how far inland it might extend; navigation on the Songhua River would be cancelled. The compensation for military expenses, however, was increased by four million roubles, making nine million in all.

During the tension with Russia, Li Hongzhang had advocated coastal defence in direct opposition to Zuo Zongtang’s emphasis on land defence. When Zuo arrived in Beijing and entered the Grand Council, the Russian treaty was discussed first. Zeng Jize had recovered more rights and interests than either the court or the public had expected, so Zuo too expressed satisfaction, and there was little dispute. But when discussion turned to Li’s coastal-defence plans, he had much more to say. From coastal defence he moved to land defence, then to conditions on the western frontier and the story of his campaigns in Xinjiang. He talked endlessly, as though nobody else mattered. In the Grand Council, only his voice and laughter could be heard. Prince Gong, whose rank placed him above all the officials, sat silently for two to four hours at a stretch without being able to get in a word. “Just think, Mr Hu,” Wang said. “Never mind Prince Gong; nobody could stand it. The prince endured it again and again, until at last he simply told the Council secretaries to put the original memorial away. They would discuss it no further.” “No further?” Hu was astonished. “Li of Hefei has enormous plans for coastal defence! There’s the Beiyang Fleet and the Telegraph Bureau to be set up. So many people are waiting for a bite of that rich meat. How can it all end with a few words and no more discussion?” “There,” Wang lowered his voice, “is the trouble. They have to discuss it, but if they do, they’re afraid our His Excellency Zuo will monopolize the conversation with empty talk. The only way is to lure the tiger away from the mountain before they resume.” Hu’s hopes sank halfway at these words. With a powerful patron like Zuo on the Grand Council, he had assumed that Zuo would inevitably take charge of future foreign loans, bringing an endless stream of profit. Now Zuo seemed likely to be sent out to a provincial post again, and Hu’s expectations would come to nothing. Worse still, Prince Gong appeared to dislike him. That did not bode well. But how did Prince Chun treat him?

“Prince Chun treats him well. You know he has a taste for military affairs, Mr Hu. With His Excellency Zuo’s military achievements, he regards him as a hero. As they say, men of spirit appreciate one another. They visit often and are very close. The prince has even invited him to watch the drills at Shenji Camp.” “What did you say?” Hu asked. “Prince Chun has invited His Excellency Zuo to watch the drills at Shenji Camp?” “Yes.” “Who told you that?” The question carried a note of disbelief. It was also clear that Hu considered the matter important. Unable to fathom his reaction, Wang could only answer truthfully. “Mr Xu, the ‘junior grand councillor’, told me. His Excellency Zuo arrived at the end of the first month. Early in the second, Prince Chun invited him to dinner and to see the newly completed garden at Taiping Lake. Sometime in the teens of that month, he invited him again and personally asked him to watch the drills. His Excellency Zuo promised that he would certainly come, but they didn’t fix a day. Now that you are here, Mr Hu, I expect they will.” Hu was more puzzled than ever, but he did not ask immediately. Why could no date be fixed for Prince Chun’s invitation until he himself arrived? Only when he had thought it through did he ask, “Who told you that? Mr Xu?” “Who else?” Mr Xu, the so-called junior grand councillor, was Xu Yongyi, a secretary of the Grand Council. Hu reflected that he had always been close to Zuo, who consistently entrusted him with arrangements for his dealings with officials in Beijing. Anything he said about Zuo could be relied upon.

Then another thought struck Hu: why trouble two hosts when one would do? He too had many matters that could be entrusted to Xu. He immediately told Secretary Yang to write an invitation asking Xu to join him for a modest drink, with a special request that he come at once so Hu might benefit from his advice. He also selected four kinds of Hangzhou speciality: two Fang Yuhe hams, ten Shulianji sandalwood fans, four jars of Jingyangguan pickled vegetables, and Hu Qingyu Hall’s own products, including Biwen Pills and tiger-bone-and-papaya liquor. These were packed into a basket and sent with the invitation to Xu’s house. Xu arrived at sunset, still wearing his official robes. His substantive appointment was as a secretary in the Ministry of Punishments, whereas Hu wore a coral button on his official hat. Their ranks were far apart, so Xu observed the formalities of a subordinate calling on a superior. “When did Your Excellency arrive in Beijing?” he asked, hurrying forward and gathering up the hem of his robe, ready to make his formal obeisance. Xu’s courtesy name was Xiaoyun. Hu, who had met him once before and addressed him as Mr Xiaoyun, quickly caught him with both hands. “Mr Xiaoyun, Mr Xiaoyun! You positively insult me with these formalities. Please change your clothes at once, and then we can talk.” Xu’s attendant was already waiting in the corridor with a bundle of clothes. At these words he came in to help his master change into informal dress: a lined gown of royal-blue Nanjing silk and a rose-purple tribute-satin vest with a curved, pipa-shaped front fastening. This had become the customary, distinctive attire of Grand Council secretaries and made quite a show in social gatherings. They exchanged bows and sat on the kang. Xu now changed his form of address. “On what day did you arrive, Mr Hu?”

“Just now. You are my first guest, Mr Xiaoyun.” Looking almost overwhelmed by the honour, Xu clasped his hands and replied in polished literary phrases. “Your gracious regard, and the many precious gifts you have so kindly bestowed, leave me at once grateful and ashamed.” “Mere trifles, mere trifles! Not worth mentioning.” Hu asked, “Do you see His Excellency Zuo often?” “Every day. When I’m on duty, twice a day: in the morning at the Grand Council, and in the afternoon at his residence in Xianliang Temple.” “Is the old gentleman keeping well?” “Very well, very well. Only…” Xu frowned slightly. “Rather too well, which is not altogether a good thing.” “Because he talks so much, I suppose?” “Not only does he talk a great deal; he has powerful lungs. When he holds forth in the north room, those of us in the south room can hear everything.”

Hu nodded and put Zuo aside for the moment. “Mr Xiaoyun, I am completely in the dark here in Beijing. I must depend on you to look after me.” Xu knew this was only courtesy. With silver for his lantern, Hu could see perfectly clearly. “You are too kind. If there is anything I can do, please give me your instructions without hesitation.” “You put it too strongly,” Hu said. “I truly do want to ask your help. Would you draw up a list for me? Whom must I attend to? Whom must I visit myself? And how should I go about it in each case? I should like your guidance on all these points. I have one further, unreasonable request: please draw up the list now.” This was an important responsibility, and Xu naturally agreed. After a moment’s thought he said, “First, the senior officials from your home province, especially those whose office is to remonstrate with the throne. They should receive rather more.” “Exactly. Please tell me what to do. Whatever amounts you say, I shall accept.” Such confidence from a man he scarcely knew made Xu feel that he must repay one who appreciated him. “May I venture to ask, Mr Hu, whether you have come to Beijing to discuss a foreign loan?” “Yes.” “Anything else?” “I also want to find out what view the capital takes of silk reeling by Western methods.” “That is easy. I know the answer myself; we can discuss it later. But if you are here about a foreign loan, the secretaries of the Zongli Yamen and the departmental officials of the Ministry of Revenue must receive attention. I’ll draw up a list.” Brush and inkstone were brought, and Xu wrote at the tea table. After careful consideration he put a figure beneath each name, ranging from one hundred to five hundred: the amounts, of course, to be given in banknotes.

“There is one man whose gift will require careful thought,” Xu said, laying down his brush. “Grand Secretary Bao now oversees the Ministry of Revenue, and he also serves in the Zongli Yamen.” Under the Qing system, grand secretaries were assigned oversight of ministries. Zuo Zongtang, with his outstanding military achievements, had been summoned to serve at court as Grand Secretary of the Eastern Pavilion and naturally supervised the Ministry of War. Bao Yun, Grand Secretary of the Hall of Military Glory, had succeeded the late Wenxiang in supervising the Ministry of Revenue, giving him actual control over the national finances. He had often criticized Zuo’s high-interest foreign loans, as Hu was well aware. Xu’s mention of Bao touched the very point troubling him. He leaned closer and lowered his voice. “I have never had dealings with Grand Secretary Bao. Can you advise me, Mr Xiaoyun? Is there a way to approach him?” “There is one. I have only heard about it, but we could try.” “What is it?” “This is how it works…” A secret was not to be passed to a third pair of ears: Xu spoke so softly that only Hu could hear. Later, while refreshments were being served to Xu, Hu found an opportunity to call Gu Yingchun over and give him some instructions.

“You know antiques, calligraphy and paintings. I should like you to make a trip to Liulichang.” Gu was puzzled. Hu had only just arrived in Beijing and had not yet attended to a single item of business; why had he suddenly found the leisure to hunt for works of art? Seeing his doubt, Hu added, “I want to buy something as a gift.” So that was it. “For whom?” Gu asked. Hu took his hand and traced the character Bao on his palm. “Understand?” “I do.” “Good. There is a shop in Liulichang called Haiyue Shanfang: Hai as in Shanghai, Yue as in Lord Yue. Go in and ask for a clerk surnamed Zhu, a man from Shaoxing. Ask him what a certain person likes. If he says calligraphy and paintings, ask for those; if he says antiques, ask for antiques. And make it clear: the pieces must be good, whatever the price.” Gu considered these instructions carefully, then nodded emphatically to show that he understood. “I’ll go at once.” By the time he returned, host and guest were already at table. Hu introduced him to Xu. “Come, come, have a few more cups with Mr Xiaoyun.” Then he asked, “Well?” “I’m to see the goods tomorrow.”

Knowing that contact had been established, Hu asked no more. He turned to Xu. “You said just now that there are four shortcuts to official advancement these days, Mr Xiaoyun. What are they?” “Four sorts of people: imperial tutors, princely aides, envoys to devils, and men on divine commissions. Men like Li Lansun and Weng Buping made their way by teaching the emperor: those are the imperial tutors. Grand Secretary Bao is a devoted partisan of Prince Gong, as Grand Secretary Wen used to be. Those are the princely aides.” “His Excellency Wen?” Hu exclaimed. “Has he become a grand secretary?” Xu paused in surprise, then understood. He meant the late Wenxiang, Grand Secretary of the Tiren Pavilion, whereas Hu thought Wenyu had been promoted to assistant grand secretary. “As a rule, a minister has to be transferred to the Ministry of Personnel before he can become assistant grand secretary. He is still Minister of Punishments. It will be some time yet.” “Ah, yes,” Hu said, understanding in turn. “You mean the late Wen Wenzhong.” Wenzhong was Wenxiang’s posthumous name. “Exactly.” “Mr Xiaoyun,” Gu put in, “‘envoys to devils’ explains itself: men sent abroad to deal with foreign devils. But what are these ‘divine commissions’?” “Once explained, it is quite simple.” Xu gestured towards Hu. “Mr Hu and I were just discussing Shenji Camp; shen in its name means divine. A ‘divine commission’ means a posting there. Because of Prince Chun, men serving in the camp receive exceptionally favourable recommendations for promotion. But Han Chinese have no share in it. Even the occasional exception is a military officer. There are no Han civil officials serving there.”

“Yingchun,” Hu said, “Mr Xiaoyun and I were just talking about Prince Chun’s invitation to His Excellency Zuo to watch the drills at Shenji Camp. His Excellency is waiting for me to arrive before fixing the day. Do you know why? Because he must reward the troops when he goes, and he is waiting for me to prepare the rewards. Work out a plan for me.” Gu reflected that rewarding troops ordinarily meant paying cash. With Fukangfu Bank here, Zuo need only send someone over to say that he wanted silver. Since he had not done so, he must consider cash unsuitable and want something else. “We don’t know what they would like,” Gu suggested. “Why not simply ask His Excellency Rong to go to Prince Chun and inquire frankly? We can prepare the rewards according to the prince’s instructions.” “Rong Zhonghua stopped calling at Prince Chun’s house long ago,” Xu said. Rong Zhonghua was Ronglu, whom everyone knew to have been raised to prominence by the prince himself. That he should no longer visit his own patron was extraordinary. Even Hu wanted to know why. “It is a long story, involving a secret that nobody would believe.” Xu lowered his voice. “Have you heard in the south what illness the Western Empress Dowager is suffering from?” “I heard it was dry-blood wasting disease,” Hu replied. “How could she have developed such an illness?” “It is…” Xu broke off abruptly. “Better that I should not speak of it, and better that you should not hear it. If you heard and accidentally passed it on, it could bring terrible trouble. I should have harmed you both. Let us talk of something else.” Hu was disappointed to have Xu become mysterious just at the critical point. Then he reflected that such caution made Xu a man to be trusted, and his disappointment dissolved.

They drank and chatted a little longer. Since this was his first time at Hu’s table, Xu would not drink much. He asked for a bowl of rice gruel, finished it and prepared to leave. “Weixian,” Hu called, “is everything ready?” “It is.” Wang himself brought a tray containing more than ten red envelopes and a list of names. These were the “small tokens of respect” Xu was to deliver on Hu’s behalf. “I am most obliged,” Hu said, clasping his hands. “I shall deal with the others just as you advised, either calling myself or sending someone with the gift. Everything will be done tomorrow.” “Good, good. When will you see His Excellency Zuo tomorrow, Mr Hu?” “I shall go early and wait for him.” “Then we will meet at Xianliang Temple and talk further there.” “Yes, indeed.” As he spoke, Hu held out a hand to Wang, took a red envelope, and drew out the banknote to check it. It had been made out for four hundred taels, exactly as he had instructed. He quietly slipped it into Xu’s hand and closed his own hand over it so that Xu could not refuse. “No, no! There is no reason for this.” “A mere trifle. If you will not accept it, you do not regard me as a friend.” “I truly feel ashamed to accept it. Thank you, thank you.”

After Xu had left, Hu asked about Haiyue Shanfang. Gu said that he had met the clerk surnamed Zhu and asked what Bao Yun liked. Zhu had replied that he liked everything. Gu had then repeated Hu’s instructions: a high price did not matter, provided the pieces were good. They had arranged for him to inspect the goods the following morning. “Go early. As soon as you have seen them, bring the foreigners to Xianliang Temple,” Hu said. “As for His Excellency Zuo’s rewards for Shenji Camp, I have thought of a plan myself. Whether it can be carried out or not, we will leave that until tomorrow afternoon.” He yawned. The clerk at Haiyue Shanfang was nicknamed Zhu Tiekou, or Iron Mouth Zhu, a name that sounded like a fortune-teller’s professional title. He had earned it through his unfailing judgment of antiques, paintings, calligraphy and book editions: what he declared genuine was genuine; what he called false was false. Although only an employee, he was respectfully addressed as Mr Zhu by the proprietors of Liulichang’s antique shops and Nanhai shops. Gu, always careful in his dealings, had learned of his abilities and wanted to cultivate his friendship. Putting aside the superior standing of a customer, he too called him Mr Zhu. Zhu naturally protested that he did not deserve such respect and suggested, “Just call me Old Zhu.” “Better to obey than insist on courtesy,” Gu replied. “Old Zhu, what have you to show me?” That single “Mr Zhu” had altered Zhu’s usual manner of receiving customers. “A great many things.” He picked up an inkstone. “Look at this, Mr Gu.”

The inkstone was seven cun long, five wide and three high, and the colour of pig’s liver. A line of seal-script characters ran down either side of its upper surface. On the right was “A loyal heart shines through the sun”; on the left, “Inscribed by Pengju of Tangyin.” “So it was used by Yue Fei, Lord Wumu.” “Not only by Lord Wumu. The founding emperor of the Ming used it too,” Zhu said with a smile. Looking more closely, Gu saw four lines in regular script carved on the right side: “The inkstone of Yue, the Junior Guardian, formerly served the imperial presence. His Majesty has now graciously bestowed it upon his servant Da. It is the precious inkstone of a loyal minister of old. How can I be worthy of it? I solemnly pledge my utmost loyalty and devotion, that I may bring no dishonour upon this inkstone. Respectfully recorded by the servant Xu Da on the first day of the first month in the second year of Hongwu.” “Xu Da ranked first among the founders of the Ming, and his children married into the founding emperor’s family,” Gu said. “With such a history, this inkstone must surely appear in some Ming scholar’s collected notes. Wouldn’t you say so, Old Zhu?” Zhu laughed. “One can tell from that remark that you know your subject, Mr Gu. No false words before a true master. Whether Xu Da, Prince of Zhongshan, ever owned it is something we needn’t discuss.” He put the inkstone back. “Please come inside and sit down.”

“Inside” meant a tiny room behind the accounts counter. Once the door was shut, its only light came through a skylight in the roof. A slanting ray of sunshine fell neatly across the square table against the wall. When Gu had seated himself opposite, Zhu leaned forward and spoke in a low voice, his manner solemn and mysterious. “Who introduced you to us, Mr Gu?” “My employer told me to come. There was no introduction.” “And who is your employer?” Gu hesitated, uncertain whether he could reveal Hu’s name, and did not answer for some time. “What instructions did your employer give you, Mr Gu?” “Only to find you, Old Zhu, and ask what Grand Secretary Bao likes. The pieces must be good; the price doesn’t matter.” “Then it’s no wonder you won’t tell me everything. Your employer didn’t make himself clear. Naturally, he has some purpose in buying antiques and paintings for Grand Secretary Bao. What exactly is it? How much does he intend to give? Tell me frankly and I’ll work it out for you. I guarantee that not a penny will be wasted; every bit will go where it counts.” Gu could hear that there was much beneath these words. Zhu seemed sincere, so he answered honestly. “You are quite right. My employer did not explain clearly. Could you first tell me how it all works, Old Zhu? Then I can judge whether I may act on his behalf.” “Of course. We have dealings with the various princely households and several grand secretaries’ residences. To put it plainly…”

To put it plainly, this was the sale of offices and honours. It was inconvenient for those receiving the payments to take bribes openly, so intermediaries were needed to conceal the transaction. “Suppose you wanted an appointment as an examiner, Mr Gu, or your son was about to sit the provincial examination. Afraid that literary merit might count for nothing in the examination hall, you might want to buy a private arrangement. Then you would go to Damochang, to the shops selling collections of examination essays, run by men from Jinxi in Jiangxi. They would quote you a price. But if you wanted a post or a commission, you would come to us. We would make inquiries and tell you what you must give. Naturally, you would buy it here…” “Wait,” Gu interrupted. “Do you mean it has to be bought from you?” “Yes.” “At a price you set?” “Of course.” “Can one bargain?” “Certainly. Why not? You have been good enough to think well of me, Mr Gu, and I couldn’t bear to make a dishonest profit from you. That is why I ask what post your employer wants, so that I can tell you the true going rate.” “I see.” Gu thought it over, but some points were still unclear. “Give me an example.”

“Suppose you wanted to be Shanghai Circuit Intendant. I would inquire and tell you that all you needed was to give Grand Secretary Bao a copy of the Jade-Pillow Lanting calligraphy album. The album would cost a hundred and twenty thousand taels. You would buy it and send it in. Once the grand secretary knew the money was in hand, you would get your wish. The actual price of the Shanghai post is a hundred thousand. We add twenty per cent on top and deduct twenty per cent as commission, making forty thousand on the transaction. A shrewd customer who haggles and haggles can at most knock off the twenty per cent added on top. For someone to whom I want to show friendship, such as yourself, I would state it frankly: a hundred thousand, and not a single coin less.” “Ah, so that is how it works. What if someone didn’t know this route through your shop and approached someone else to arrange it?” “They too would tell him to give a copy of the Jade-Pillow Lanting, and tell him where to buy it.” Zhu continued, “Heshen invented this method in the Qianlong reign. Whatever item he told a man to give was actually from his own collection. When we went to ask the price, we simply brought the object back with us.”

“Thank you, thank you. I have learned a secret. But there is one more point. Suppose I didn’t want to bargain at all, but simply wanted to give someone a certain sum. How would that be done?” “We have rules for that too. First we ask whom you want to give it to. There are things for Prince Gong and things for Grand Secretary Bao. Say you wanted to give something to Prince Gong. I would tell you: this inkstone of Yue, the Junior Guardian, is two thousand; that Ge Tie calligraphy album is three thousand; a Song edition of Du Fu’s poems is five thousand. If you wanted to give ten thousand taels, they would add up exactly.” “Would there be a markup in those prices?” “Genuine goods at fair prices. No markup.” Zhu explained that this differed from selling posts and appointments. If the Shanghai post was worth a hundred thousand taels, then once that amount was received, all that was due had been paid. Any additional markup came at the buyer’s expense. But if a man intended to give eighty thousand and the goods were actually worth only seventy thousand at the agreed base prices, would that not be taking money owed to the seller? Once trust was lost, the seller would use another intermediary. To lose such excellent business for a small immediate gain would be exceedingly foolish.

“Old Zhu, since you have told me everything, I must not deceive you in the least. My employer is not seeking a post or an appointment; he has another purpose. I don’t yet know how much he intends to give, but I suspect this is no mere matter of thirty or fifty thousand taels. Let me go back and ask him clearly, and then we can discuss it further.” Gu added with emphasis, “Rest assured: unless he decides to give nothing, the transaction will certainly go through you. Even if my employer wanted to use another shop, I wouldn’t agree.” Hearing his sincerity and observing his appearance and dress, Zhu knew he had found a wealthy customer. If the business succeeded, the proprietor would have to give him at least several thousand taels. He could easily set up for himself. The thought delighted him. “I cannot thank you enough for your patronage, Mr Gu.” He rose and made an obeisance. “You must have a considerable collection yourself. What do you like? Let me see whether I can be of service.” Gu thought that if he wanted to make a friend, he ought not to leave empty-handed. Unable to think immediately of anything he wanted, he asked casually, “Have you anything unusual?” “Indeed I have. Please come out and look.”

After some searching, Zhu produced four curios, respectively long, round, square and flat. The long object was a xiao flute that appeared to be made of yellow jade. The round one was a cricket jar from the Xuande reign of the Ming. The square one was an iron certificate of immunity from execution, bestowed by the emperor on Xu Huizu, Duke of Wei, a founding worthy of the Ming. The flat one was a moulded-gourd object from the Kangxi reign: a box for seal paste. “Which would you say is worth most, Mr Gu?” “I should think this jade flute.” “Jade? Look carefully. Is it jade?” Gu picked up the flute and tapped it twice with a finger. It rang clearly. “What is it, if not jade?” “Look again.” On closer inspection he found an inscription: “Not lustrous without, not dry within; it receives the breath whole. Its tone is neither stifled nor floating, and its quality surpasses the finest bamboo.” The writing was in ink. How could ink adhere to jade? That was strange. “It is a paper flute, made in Fujian,” Zhu said. “It dates from the Ming. The method of making it has now been lost.”

Greatly surprised, Gu set the flute aside to show that he liked it and would buy it. “Which is the rarest piece, Old Zhu?” Rarity made for value, so the rarest should naturally be the most expensive. Zhu carefully lifted the cricket jar and tapped it with a finger. It gave a deep, resonant ring like metal or stone. “Genuine. Five hundred years old,” he said with satisfaction. His manner aroused Gu’s curiosity. Taking the earthenware jar, Gu examined it closely. Figures were carved around its sides. In the centre of the base were the words “Made in the Xuande Reign of the Great Ming,” with a smaller line beside them: “Respectfully made by Zou Daxiu of Lumu, Suzhou.” The workmanship was certainly delicate, but it was only a cricket jar. Having survived four or five hundred years did not in itself make it a valuable antique. Gu felt awkward about saying so directly. “What is so good about it, Old Zhu?” “Its age, and the quality of its clay. All the fierceness of the firing has left it, so it won’t harm the insect. You have fought crickets, surely?” Crickets were called ququ in the north, and sometimes in the south as well. Though Gu did not pursue the pastime, he knew that enormous sums changed hands in wagers on cricket fights. “A single contest can involve thousands or tens of thousands of taels. To put it bluntly, they treat a good cricket like an honoured ancestor. What is a hundred-odd taels for a Xuande jar to such a man?” Gu marvelled inwardly. “A hundred taels for an earthenware pot?” “Yes. But for you, of course, I would make a special concession. Let us say two hundred taels for all four pieces.”

That could hardly be considered expensive. Without a word, Gu took a Western-style leather wallet from his clothes, drew out a sheaf of banknotes, counted out two hundred taels and put the wallet away. Watching closely, Zhu saw that every note had been issued by Fukangfu. A sudden realization made him exclaim in delighted surprise, “So your employer is Hu, the God of Wealth!” Hu had been known by that name for several years. Gu could hardly deny it. He merely lowered his voice. “It is enough that you know, Old Zhu. Keep it to yourself. If the news gets about, this transaction will be impossible.” “I understand, I understand. How could anyone spread word of such a thing?” Gu nodded and told Zhu to deliver the four curios to Fukangfu. He himself hurried into the city by carriage, making for Xianliang Temple in Bingzhan Hutong to act as interpreter.
