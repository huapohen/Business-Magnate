HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang
English Translation

INSTALLMENT 121
Towers of Light — Chapter Four, Part Two

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

Yueru straightened the bedding and washed her hands, then opened the door to the stairs and called the maid to bring up the pot of duck congee. She sent the maid away again, filled a bowl for Hu, and took half a bowl herself to keep him company. “Master,” she asked casually, “is it true that you mean to move all twenty-three managers to different shops?” “Yes. That is what Old Tang has gone to Deqing to discuss.” “Where do you mean to send him?” “I do not know yet.” “How can you not know?” “Heaven will decide. How could I know?” “What do you mean, Heaven will decide?” “We shall draw lots. The twenty-three pawnshops will be divided into three classes—large, medium, and small—and the managers will draw within each class. Suppose there are eight in the largest class: their eight managers draw together, and each goes wherever his lot sends him.” “Then if Old Tang draws Suzhou he goes to Suzhou, and if he draws Zhenjiang he goes to Zhenjiang?” “Exactly.”

At this, Yueru put down her chopsticks, covered her face, and stumbled back into the bedroom. Startled, Hu followed. She lay face down on the bed, her shoulders shaking with sobs. “Yueru! Yueru!” Though he kept nudging her, she paid no attention. The sound of her crying did seem to stop. “What is the matter? Why are you suddenly so heartbroken?” Facing the inner side of the bed, she complained, “How could I not be? You heartless creature! You have tricked me into your arms, had your taste of something new, and immediately thought of this scheme. You will have Old Tang take me into exile in another county, so you need never set eyes on me again!” Hu could not help laughing in astonishment. “Where did that come from? I never dreamed you would connect two things that have nothing to do with each other.” “Nothing to do with each other? If Old Tang is sent away, I must go with him. You do not seem to care in the least. You have had your pleasure, and that is the end of it.” Her accusation was not wholly unreasonable. After considering it, Hu said, “You need not necessarily go with him. I could buy you another house.” “To be your secret household?” “It would not exactly be that…” He was at a loss, but Yueru pressed on. “What else would it be? Even if we said Old Tang had bought it, how should I explain living alone in Hangzhou? People would ask. And someone would be sure to discover your visits. If your attendants said nothing, somebody else would tell Madam Luosi. One day she would come with a crowd to attack me. Then I should have no choice but to throw myself into a river or a well.” Unable to refute her, Hu stood speechless for some time. “You know the transfer plan came first. Even yesterday I had no idea we would share a bed today. The two things were entirely separate; now they seem tied together. Tell me, then: what can we do?”

Yueru deliberately paused as though thinking it over. “There is a way. But first I must ask: did you only want to take advantage of me today, or do you still want me?” “I still want you.” “Then there is only one thing to do. Leave everything as it is.” “What do you mean?” “Move the other managers wherever you please, but leave Old Tang at Gongji. He is the head of all your pawnshop managers, different from the others. There is good reason to leave him where he is.” “How could that be justified? Once I make an exception, everyone will object.” “Then move none of them. I know nothing about business, but surely it depends on knowing people. Arrive suddenly in a strange place and you are completely in the dark. By the time you can see your way, other firms have taken the business.” Hu turned the matter over anxiously, unable to find a solution that served both purposes. At last he sighed. “We shall have to leave everyone where he is.”

The servants in Yuanbao Street soon knew of Tang Zishao’s beauty trap. Hu’s mother, however, ruled the household strictly. She treated the saying “Those who bring you gossip are troublemakers themselves” as an inviolable law, so no one dared carry this secret into the Twelve Chambers. It remained unspoken nearby but traveled far away. Gu related it gloomily to the Seventh Lady, who refused to believe him. “Little Uncle is not that sort of man. If a woman could make him overturn a settled business plan, how could he have built up what he has today? He would have ruined himself long ago.” “I cannot be bothered to argue. He will soon be here to meet His Excellency Zuo. Ask him yourself.” “Of course I shall.” She continued defending Hu. “He must have his reasons for leaving the twenty-three managers in place. His judgment will not be wrong.” Hu arrived the next day and stayed, as before, at Gu’s house. He and Gu returned from their engagements after eleven that night to find the Seventh Lady waiting with the customary late supper. Over wine and conversation, she asked casually, “Little Uncle, I hear you have abandoned your plan to transfer the twenty-three managers. Why?” “Ah, Seventh Sister, please do not ask.” Her expression changed at once. “Why should I not ask?” “Pleasant things are worth talking about. Unpleasant things will only upset you as well. Why bother?” “Then it is true. Tang really did lay a trap for you to walk into. Little Uncle, how could you do anything so foolish?”

As she said “foolish,” her mouth twisted, her eyes turned askew, and her face became fiery red. “Something is wrong!” Gu cried, rushing to support her. She could no longer sit upright and fell headlong from her stool. Fortunately, the thick Persian wool carpet kept her head from being cut open. “A stroke!” Hu jumped up and shouted for servants. While some lifted her, others were sent for doctors. Hu ordered them to fetch Doctor Cao, known as an imperial physician because he had been summoned to take pulses in the palace during the Xianfeng reign. Gu trusted Western medicine and sent separately for a doctor he knew, Ailituo. It was past midnight, and knocking up doctors from their beds naturally took time. Gu remained comparatively composed; Hu was extraordinarily distressed. He paced and wrung his hands, watching the Seventh Lady on the couch, her eyes closed, a rattling sound of phlegm in her throat. He knew that anger at his disgraceful conduct had brought this on. If she died, it would be like the saying, “I did not kill Boren, but Boren died because of me.” Lifelong remorse would be his. How could he go on living with it?

At last Ailituo arrived. Seeing her lying flat, he told Gu in English that a stroke patient should not be left in that position. Gu immediately had two serving women sit her up in an armchair, supporting her on either side. Western doctors observed none of the rules against physical contact between the sexes. Ailituo opened his bag, put the stethoscope to his ears, and had Gu unfasten her clothes so that he could listen to her heart. After examining her, he pried open her clenched teeth and contrived to get her to swallow a pill with warm water. If she regained consciousness after six hours, he told Gu, her life could be saved. He would return after daybreak. As he was speaking, Doctor Cao arrived. Ailituo looked displeased and protested that someone receiving Western treatment could not also consult a Chinese physician. Gu was embarrassed and asked Hu what to do. “You trust Western medicine, so naturally you must decide. Let Doctor Cao examine her and write his prescription as usual. Simply do not give her his medicine.” “Quite right. That is the way.” Gu followed the suggestion.

Ailituo was a capable doctor. By daybreak the Seventh Lady had actually opened her eyes, though Hu was now too exhausted to keep his own open. “Little Uncle, go and sleep. You still have to meet His Excellency Zuo this afternoon. Rest without worrying; I shall wake you in time.” “If only I could put my mind at ease enough to sleep.” “Life and death are ordained. Besides, she seems better. You need not worry.” But how could Hu be easy? His eyes were sore and heavy, yet his sleep was broken by frequent starts. He rose before noon. “Ailituo has been again,” Gu told him. “He says the danger has largely passed, though paralysis is probably unavoidable. Many people live another ten years or more with such an illness. Attend to your business, Little Uncle. I am sorry I cannot accompany you. Please explain to His Excellency when you see him.” “Of course. I shall.”

Zuo had waited until after Cixi’s birthday before leaving Beijing, with permission to visit his ancestral graves. He reached Changsha, the Hunan provincial capital, on the twenty-fifth day of the eleventh lunar month. His first act was to call on Guo Songtao. Their relations were tangled with old favors and grievances. In the eighth year of Xianfeng, when Zuo served on Governor Luo Bingzhang’s staff in Hunan, he decided everything for himself. One day Luo was reading in his office when a gun sounded at the outer gate. As it was not time for the regular noon gun, he asked what had happened. “Adviser Zuo is ceremonially dispatching a memorial,” a servant replied. Luo had not even known that a memorial was being sent. Zuo was virtually the governor of Hunan himself and acquired the nickname “Left Censor-in-Chief.” Governors normally carried the concurrent title of Right Vice Censor-in-Chief: the nickname implied that Zuo exercised still greater power.

At that time Fan Xie, brigade commander of Yongzhou in Hunan, was a man of poor reputation from Enshi in Hubei. On one visit, Zuo questioned him about Yongzhou’s defenses. Fan could answer nothing and was less than respectful. Furious, Zuo slapped him hard across the face and immediately drafted a memorial denouncing him as “corrupt, unrestrained, lawless, and notorious.” Among its judgments was “unable to recognize a single written character.” He sent it without informing Luo. Whether Fan was corrupt and lawless remained to be investigated, but how could a wholly illiterate man command a brigade? He was dismissed first and investigated afterward. Those words burned more deeply into him than a branding iron. Once retired, he used the considerable military funds he had withheld to engage distinguished teachers for his only son. Beside the study’s tablet honoring Heaven, Earth, the Sovereign, Parents, and Teachers, he pasted red paper bearing the charge of illiteracy. “Zuo is only a provincial graduate, yet he puts on such airs. Unless you become a jinshi, you are no son of mine.” The son justified his hopes: he not only became a jinshi but entered the Hanlin Academy, winning literary distinction early. He was Fan Zengxiang. While educating his son, Fan also sought revenge. Through influence he appealed to Luo’s superior, Guanwen, described as Governor-General of Liangguang, and sent men to present his grievance at the Censorate in Beijing. Guanwen’s memorial used the formidable phrase “one office, two seals,” meaning that two men were acting as governor of Hunan. Official authority could not be lent to private hands, least of all the authority of a great provincial governor. The matter was serious.

Guo Songtao was then a Hanlin scholar serving in the Southern Study. His child had married a child of Zuo’s elder brother Zuo Zongzhi, so he knew Zuo well and understood his exceptional ability. He approached Pan Zuyin, another Southern Study scholar. “Without Zuo Jigao, Hunan cannot possibly hold out. There would then be no hope for the situation in the southeast. I am his fellow provincial and a relative by marriage; it would be improper for me to speak. Why not submit a memorial yourself?” Pan did so. After recounting Zuo’s achievements, he concluded, “The state cannot do without Hunan for a single day, and Hunan cannot do without Zuo Zongtang for a single day.” Moved, the Xianfeng Emperor asked Zeng Guofan whether Zuo should remain in Hunan or be transferred to his headquarters, where his abilities could be fully used. Zeng replied that Zuo was “firm, clear-sighted, able to endure hardship, and thoroughly versed in military affairs.” An edict assigned him to assist Zeng. Misfortune had become good fortune, thanks largely to Pan and Guo, but Zuo treated the two men very differently. For Pan, he sent generous gifts of money on the customary three festivals and two birthdays. Knowing Pan’s interest in ancient inscriptions and stone monuments, he also sent the first rubbings of newly unearthed Guanzhong steles by special messenger while serving as Governor-General of Shaanxi and Gansu. Sometimes he sent the original stones themselves.

After the Taiping defeat, Guo had been appointed Governor of Guangdong. Ruilin, Governor-General of Liangguang, also resided in Guangzhou. A governor and governor-general in the same city often failed to agree. Ruilin was greedy and incompetent, but his Imperial Household background gave him direct access to both empress dowagers and powerful protection. Guo suffered badly from his obstruction and could do little about it. Difficult though his position already was, he never expected Zuo, an old friend whom he had once helped, to turn against him as well. Over supplementary military contributions, Zuo sent reproachful letters and submitted four memorials criticizing Guo’s administration. Various stories explained their estrangement. The most widespread was that an auspicious lingzhi fungus had appeared in the Confucian temple at Xiangyin when Guo was appointed to Guangdong. His younger brother Guo Kuntao wrote that this foretold good fortune for his new governorship. Zuo supposedly took offense: “If lingzhi in the temple is an omen, it must refer to my ennoblement. What has it to do with the Guo family?” In fact, lingzhi appeared there often, and Zuo was not petty enough to quarrel over such a thing. The real reason lay in military finance. Since the Taiping wars, commanders had raised their own funds: possession of territory meant possession of revenue. Li Hongzhang understood this best and never loosened his grip on Jiangsu, especially Shanghai. Zuo had only Zhejiang, whose resources frequently fell short. Seeing Guangdong’s possibilities, he hardened his heart against friendship and repeatedly attacked Guo. At last his own general, Jiang Yili, replaced him. Jiang’s tenure as Governor of Guangdong, however, was brief; he too was soon transferred.

Guo remained depressed by his frustrated career. At the beginning of the Guangxu reign, senior officials living in retirement were recalled, and he and Zeng Guoquan were summoned to Beijing. Zeng was appointed Governor of Shaanxi, but, unwilling to serve with Zuo, was transferred to the governor-generalship of the Hedong River Conservancy. Guo became Judicial Commissioner of Fujian. For a former governor, this was humiliating, but he accepted it. Soon afterward he was released from the post, placed on the expectant list for a vice-ministership, and appointed imperial envoy to Britain. At that time the British minister’s interpreter, Margary, had crossed the Yunnan–Burma frontier to meet a British armed surveying party and been killed by Chinese troops and civilians. A serious diplomatic dispute followed. The British minister, Wade, said Guo could leave at once if his credentials acknowledged China’s fault; otherwise he must wait until the Yunnan case was settled. The Zongli Yamen ministers refused such an admission, so Guo could not sail. He was made acting Vice-Minister of War and assigned to the Yamen.

Guo believed foreign affairs required fairness and reason, without which foreigners could not be persuaded. In his view, Governor Cen Yuying of Yunnan could not disclaim responsibility. After the killing, Cen had sought to protect those involved, failing to establish the circumstances and report them truthfully, and simply blaming remote mountain tribes. Officials at court defended him because the man killed by the troops was a foreigner, making the British feel more wronged and increasingly intransigent. Guo attributed the deadlock entirely to this absence of fairness and reason. On entering the Yamen, he submitted a memorial under his sole name, requesting a strict disciplinary review by the ministry of Cen’s part in provoking the affair, “as a warning to those who rely on empty arrogance instead of calmly examining principles and circumstances, and thereby burden the state.” Conservative defenders of orthodoxy already resented Guo’s views. To impeach a man who had killed foreigners now seemed clear proof of collusion with foreign powers. His action provoked general indignation. Even his close friends and former pupils could not understand him; many Hunan men would no longer acknowledge him as a fellow provincial. In Beijing, someone wrote a mocking couplet: “Outstanding among his kind, surpassing all his fellows—yet unwelcome in an age of Yao and Shun. Unable even to serve men, how can he serve devils? Why must he leave his parents’ land?” At the end of the seventh lunar month of the following year, the Chefoo Convention settled the Yunnan dispute. Guo could finally sail for Britain, but another unhappy incident occurred before his departure.

Liu Xihong, a Cantonese formerly serving as an assistant departmental director in the Ministry of Punishments, was an old Guangdong acquaintance with whom Guo had enjoyed discussing foreign affairs. He wanted to accompany him. Talking was one thing, however, and conducting diplomacy another. Guo thought Liu too rigid and impulsive for work requiring patient, delicate effort. Liu persisted, enlisting Guo’s friend Zhu Sunzhi to intercede. Zhu said he had repeated Guo’s criticisms to Liu, who recognized his faults and promised not to meddle: Guo should simply take him as a friend with whom to talk and relieve the loneliness of a foreign country. Moved by Liu’s poverty and lack of prospects, Guo recommended him as a counselor. A mere sixth-rank departmental official, Liu qualified for no higher diplomatic appointment. Yet the edict released him from the Ministry of Punishments, made him an expectant fifth-rank metropolitan court official, added an honorary third-rank title, and appointed him deputy envoy to Britain. Such a precedent was rare. Behind it lay Grand Councillor Li Hongzao’s mistrust of Guo. Fearing that Guo would always take Britain’s side, he had raised Liu to deputy rank to restrain the chief envoy. Liu misunderstood his mission as that of an enforcer. After thanking the throne, he visited Guo and demanded to know why Guo had proposed him only as counselor, rather than deputy envoy. He accused him of failing a friend and added much abusive language.

Guo was furious, but could only blame fate for continually bringing him people who repaid kindness with hostility. Liu indeed obstructed him at every turn and made disgraceful scenes. In a letter to Li Hongzhang, Guo described their service together as “ghosts howling in the rooms, foxes screeching among the rafters.” The unrest throughout the legation was easy to imagine. By then Liu had become minister to Germany and could submit memorials independently. They impeached one another, and Liu’s backing from Li Hongzao gave him the advantage. Zhang Peilun, one of the celebrated Four Hanlin Remonstrants and a follower of Li Hongzao, called for the envoy to Britain to be recalled. Discouraged, Guo repeatedly asked to leave. In the seventh lunar month of Guangxu’s fifth year, Zeng Guofan’s eldest son, Zeng Jize, was appointed to replace him. Liu fell at the same time, however, and Li Fengbao, whom Guo admired, succeeded him in Germany. This was the result of Li Hongzhang’s vigorous intervention.

Guo had won an excellent reputation in Britain, where his departure was widely regretted. His first wife had long been dead, and his second had been put aside. He had taken only a concubine surnamed Liang to Britain. Her status should have barred an audience with Queen Victoria, yet an exception had been made for her. However well he had been treated there, Guo knew that Li Hongzao’s party would not leave him in peace at home. Already sixty-two, he resolved to retire. On reaching Shanghai he pleaded illness rather than returning to Beijing to report, and requested release from office. His wish was eventually granted, and he settled in Changsha. Retirement did not mean indifference to public affairs. He corresponded frequently with Li Hongzhang and Zeng Guoquan, discussing current policy, especially foreign affairs, in detail. His days were fairly tranquil. In the seventh year of Guangxu, two events, one early and one late, gave him particular satisfaction. In the second lunar month, Liu Xihong, recalled to serve as a councillor in the Office of Transmission, denounced Li Hongzhang as overbearing, disloyal, and practically assuming imperial powers. He resented Li for having broken his diplomatic rice bowl. Li was then at the height of favor, and Liu’s ill-judged attack merely invited humiliation. An edict condemned his “reckless slander” and referred him for disciplinary review. The result was dismissal.

The other satisfaction was Zuo’s visit. His procession was magnificent: horses before and behind, lofty boards displaying his titles, and crowds of attendants around a great green-covered chair. Within sat the Grand Secretary of the Eastern Pavilion and Marquis Kejing, wearing a jeweled hat button, a double-eyed peacock feather, a four-slit robe, and a yellow riding jacket, with large dark crystal spectacles on his nose. The prefect and county magistrate of Changsha had already cleared the streets near Guo’s house and stood waiting. Hunan’s two provincial commissioners, expectant intendants, and other officials also attended. Yet Guo’s double doors remained firmly shut. Zuo had to alight outside and send a red-buttoned military aide with his visiting card. Guo’s doorkeeper came out, made a formal obeisance, and said, “We cannot presume to receive such an honor. Please have Your Excellency’s chair turn back.” Zuo had expected this and showed no anger. “Tell your master that I have come to see a friend of fifty years. Informal clothes would not show sufficient respect, so I have worn my official robes.” The servant went in, and no answer came. Seeing the marquis-grand secretary left in this embarrassing position, the county magistrate forced his way inside and pleaded with Guo: unless he admitted Zuo, all the civil and military officials in the city would remain stranded outside. He begged consideration for their predicament. At last Guo agreed to open the main gate, though he himself waited in the hall. “My old friend,” Zuo said on meeting him, “I behaved disgracefully. I have come expressly to ask your pardon.” He brushed back his horseshoe cuffs, gathered the skirt of his robe, and knelt. “I dare not accept this!” Guo had no choice but to kneel in return. The accompanying officials helped both men up. Zuo reproached himself for his former conduct, making no attempt to explain it by military funding or the need for territory and revenue. He merely repeated, “I deserved to die. I behaved outrageously.”

Zuo was always talkative. He discussed the western campaign, frontier defenses, and news from Beijing, then turned from Zeng Guofan to old times. Noon arrived with no sign of his leaving. Guo could hardly dismiss him by lifting a teacup, as governors did with visitors, and had to invite him to lunch. The county magistrate provided for the attendants, ordering a banquet from Yuloudong, Changsha’s foremost restaurant, to be served at a nearby temple of Guan Di. Zuo, however, must be fed from Guo’s own kitchen if hospitality were to be properly observed. Fortunately, senior men from the Hunan Army, apart from Hu Linyi, were undemanding about food. Zuo liked dog meat, which he called “earth mutton.” A dish of that, some cured meats, and a plate of boned Dong’an chicken made a feast for him. The meal lasted until about three in the afternoon. Only then did his enthusiasm ebb enough for him to leave. At the door he gestured, and a military aide handed him a red envelope. Offering it in both hands, he said, “A modest token to help you through the year’s end. I earnestly beg you to accept it.” Guo refused; Zuo insisted. Before so many Hunan officials, Guo considered a dispute undignified and accepted, having already decided what to do. Inside was a Fukang banknote for ten thousand taels. He immediately wrote “Canceled” across it, put it in another envelope, and sent it to Zuo’s temporary headquarters. Courtesy required a return visit, but Guo dispensed with that convention too. In the end, Zuo had still met with a rebuff.

On the second day of the twelfth lunar month, Zuo reached Xiangyin. That evening a special messenger from the governor’s office delivered a confidential dispatch from the Grand Council. It reported an impeachment of Li Hanzhang, Governor-General of Huguang, for employing favorites, indulging corrupt officials, maintaining defense units below their authorized strength while wasting public funds, harming the people, and amassing wealth without restraint. Popular resentment was deepening. Four charges were particularly serious. First, Hubei’s likin revenue amounted to three or four million taels annually, but only forty thousand was reported to the ministry. Second, bamboo and timber duties yielded a million a year, while only thirty thousand was reported. The governor-general’s office spent seven hundred and fifty taels a day from this source, consuming over two hundred and seventy thousand annually. Third, public steamers carried private merchandise for open sale. Fourth, Li owned pawnshops in both Yangzhou and Wuhu. Qing procedure varied according to the gravity of an impeachment against a governor or governor-general. A serious case usually brought a special investigator from Beijing, at least a minister and senior in standing to the accused. To prevent the destruction of evidence, the public edict named another province as his destination. An assignment to Sichuan, for example, required passage through Hubei. On reaching Wuchang, the investigator would proclaim the actual order, and his staff would immediately seal the treasury and inspect the books, taking the accused by surprise. In a minor case, or one the court intended to treat lightly, a nearby official of superior rank and seniority would be appointed to investigate formally or simply ascertain the facts and report. Zuo’s order read, “Establish the truth of each allegation and report the facts.” This was an inquiry for a report, not a formal disciplinary investigation. Zuo disregarded the distinction.

By the time he reached Wuchang on the thirteenth, Li Hanzhang had been warned by Li Hongzhang that Zuo was coming to investigate. He had prompted Yang Zonglian, an expectant circuit intendant with the honorary title of provincial treasurer, to take leave and return home. In the closing years of Xianfeng, Yang had organized militia in his native Jintan, Jiangsu, while holding rank as an assistant departmental director in the Ministry of Revenue. In Tongzhi’s first year, when Jiangsu gentry raised a hundred and eighty thousand taels to hire British steamers to bring the Anhui Army from Anqing to fight the Taipings at Shanghai, Yang had gone back and forth making the arrangements. This gave him a close connection with Li Hongzhang. During Li’s two years fighting the Nian, Yang had managed his army administration; afterward he remained a circuit intendant in Hubei. As the Li brothers successively governed the province, he passed from the service of “Second Master Li” to that of “Eldest Master Li,” managing Hankou’s New Customs. A customs assignment was always lucrative. Hankou was the Yangtze’s foremost commercial port, and bamboo and timber duties formed its principal customs revenue. Timber from the southwestern mountains was assembled into rafts at Chenzhou in western Hunan, floated down the Ruan River into Dongting Lake, then through Yueyang to the Yangtze for sale at Hankou. Zuo had long heard western Hunan raft merchants describe abuses at the New Customs. On reaching Wuchang, he immediately sought Yang.

Acting under an imperial commission, Zuo avoided private dealings with Li Hanzhang. From his temporary headquarters he sent the governor-general’s office a formal request that Yang be ordered to attend for questioning. The reply stated that Yang had already taken leave and returned home, and could not be summoned. Zuo was furious. He issued directives to the Han–Huang–De circuit intendant and the prefect of Wuchang, requiring them to have Yang proceed promptly to Jiangning for questioning. He also memorialized: “On my previous journey home through the New Customs, Yang Zonglian avoided meeting me. On this occasion he again obtained leave in advance and returned home. Whether this was deliberate evasion cannot yet be known. Inquiries into his ordinary reputation, however, agree that it is undistinguished and his disposition unstable. No one speaks in his defense.” Whether there had been fraud in the bamboo and timber duties must await examination of the receipt counterfoils and original ledgers, together with Yang’s personal interrogation. Zuo explained that, as he had to assume office in Liangjiang, Yang was being summoned to Jiangning. Describing him as “greedy, mean, and deceitful,” he requested Yang’s immediate dismissal pending investigation. He Weijian, the circuit intendant, and Li Qian, an expectant prefect, were also Li Hanzhang’s clients. Zuo spared neither. Calling He “weak and incompetent,” he requested removal from his post and presentation through the ministry for an imperial audience—in other words, examination by Cixi herself. Li Qian was “adept at accommodating himself to others, unlikely to show vigor.” Zuo asked that Governor Peng Zuxian of Hubei keep him under observation.

The memorial also lectured Li Hanzhang. The Li family, Zuo wrote, had enjoyed an enlightened reign and risen to great distinction. Many relatives and associates could support themselves, but many others lived through dependence. Those who had earned wealth and rank in the brothers’ service had their own friends and relations, creating successive chains of patronage. In time such people abused their influence. Officials found them difficult to punish and tolerated them; local people endured their bullying, angry but afraid to speak. “The worthy will not correct them, while the timid fear their resentment. Slanders multiply and troubles arise one after another. This can scarcely benefit the family. They should lead by example, refrain from contending with their fellow provincials for power and profit, and exercise restraint, lest they become a focus of resentment. Where Li Hongzhang and Li Hanzhang cannot say everything necessary, we who have the honor to hold provincial office should earnestly instruct these people, so they may understand that righteousness is their true advantage. Preserving the family across generations is the foundation of service to the state. Such conduct would benefit both the Li brothers’ relatives and friends and the family itself.” The language was far from gentle, yet Zuo believed he had largely exonerated Li and done him a great service. After ceremonially dispatching the memorial on the nineteenth day of the twelfth month, he took a Yangtze steamer east to Jiangning to receive the seal and assume office. Thus Hu’s journey to meet him came to nothing. Zuo’s original plan had been to visit the ancestral graves, then travel south through Guangdong to Fujian, take a specially dispatched Nanyang warship from Xiamen to Shanghai, and proceed to Jiangning. Besides revisiting the ground of his old campaigns, he intended a coastal inspection in his capacity as Nanyang Minister. The imperial inquiry received at Xiangyin delayed him eleven days, forcing him to take the direct route if he was to assume office before the new year.

“In that case, Little Uncle, go home to Hangzhou for the new year,” Gu said. “Afterward I shall accompany you on a special visit to Nanjing.” “There seems no other choice. But I cannot stop worrying about Seventh Sister.” “She will be all right. She has regained consciousness; with patient nursing she will gradually improve. The doctor says a stroke depends above all on aftercare. She will have to live on with the illness.” Hu and the Seventh Lady were as close as brother and sister. Though awake, she still could not speak. She recognized him, and tears streamed down her face as her lips moved, full of things she could not say. Hu could not hold back his own tears. “Little Uncle, please do not,” Gu urged. “That only makes her feel worse.” Hu nodded, wiped his eyes, and forced a cheerful expression. Sitting beside the bed, he said, “Seventh Sister, there is so much business at year’s end that I must leave. Take time to recover. I remember your horoscope said you would meet a crisis at forty-four: fierce in its onset, but not truly dangerous. Once past it, you would live to seventy-eight. This year the Beginning of Spring falls before the lunar year ends, so the reckoning is already the renwu year, when you turn forty-four. You have passed the crisis. Next autumn Mother will be waiting for you at her birthday feast.” Unable to speak but understanding him, she moved her head on the pillow to acknowledge his words. “One more thing, Seventh Sister. That foolish conduct was a single lapse. It will never happen again.” Anger at his behavior had brought on her illness, and this was the best comfort he could offer. She even smiled through her tears.

Leaving her bedside, Hu prepared to return home. Gu gave him a farewell dinner that evening. With the Seventh Lady ill, they stayed at home, where the cook prepared a few delicate dishes. No other guests were invited; the two men drank alone. During dinner a clerk from the Purchasing and Transport Bureau brought a letter from Zuo, written at Xiangyin. It explained that the imperial order to investigate at Wuchang had canceled his original itinerary. When that business was finished, he would go straight to Jiangning, and wished to meet Hu there after the Lantern Festival. He also requested a discreet inquiry: “The treasury, customs, likin and other bureaus in Jiangsu, together with the salt-sales supervision bureaus in Hubei, Hunan, Anhui and the province abbreviated as Xi, all make earmarked monthly military remittances. What are the exact amounts? Pray inquire confidentially and let me know.” Hu considered the letter for some time. “I believe His Excellency means to move against Li of Hefei.” “You see a sign of it, Little Uncle? How will he act?” “It is too early to say, and action will depend on opportunity. But he already has the intention.”

Several units of Li’s Anhui Army were stationed in Jiangsu. Like the Hunan Army, it consisted of men recruited through personal and local ties. A commander was appointed first and then raised his men; the resulting camps took his name. Wu Changqing’s forces were the Qing camps, one of which was stationed in Jiangsu. Although “Sixth Pockmarked Liu,” Liu Mingchuan, had already carried home his official fortune and built the Daqian Mountain Retreat at Hefei, where he drank, wrote poetry, and thoroughly enjoyed playing the scholarly general, the Ming camps retained their designation. Li had divided them. One part, under Liu Shengxiu, a registered candidate for provincial military command, guarded important canal points around Zhangqiu in Shandong. Another, under Tang Dingkui, the provincial military commander of Fujian, was stationed in two counties named in the text as Jiangsu and Jingjiang. A mounted vanguard camp of the Ming forces stood at Suqian in Jiangsu, chiefly to rush to the aid of places along the canal at the first alarm. The Anhui Army also included a former Hunan Army commander, Guo Songlin. His old forces were known as the Wuyi Army. Ten camps formed river-defense units in Jiangyin and Jingjiang, while five served coastal defense in Shanghai and Baoshan. Jiangsu paid all these troops. The expression “treasury, customs, likin and bureaus” meant the provincial treasury, customs offices, likin administration, contribution and tax bureaus, and the Huai Salt Sales Supervision Bureau. Salt from Lianghuai was an important source of revenue for Liangjiang. Anhui and Jiangsu could not consume the entire output, so designated sales areas, known as salt-certificate districts, extended into Hubei, Hunan, Jiangxi, and Anhui. Here Xi meant Jiangxi, not Shanxi. Each of the four provinces had a Huai Salt Sales Supervision Bureau, whose revenue also belonged to Liangjiang.

“There is no need to investigate in Hangzhou or set the Purchasing and Transport Bureau to work,” Hu said. “I have a much easier way. Let Old Mi write to the various places and ask. That should tell us nearly everything.” Old Mi was Mi Benchang of Ningbo, manager of the Shanghai establishment of Fufeng Xueji Bank. Shanghai was Fufeng’s headquarters. If he wrote to its affiliated banks in the various places, asking how much the treasury, customs, and other offices had remitted in recent months to the Anhui Army’s Rear Commissariat, the approximate monthly burden could readily be calculated. It was indeed convenient. “But if His Excellency intends to attack Li, the inquiry must be secret,” Gu said. “Might word of it get out?” “What word? Suppose you managed Fufeng at Nanchang. If I asked how much the Jiangxi Huai Salt Sales Supervision Bureau sent each month to the Rear Commissariat at Jiangning, why would you imagine that Zuo wanted the figures for a particular purpose?” “Quite right. I worry only because I know what lies behind it. Otherwise I should never dream of it. But if all the money goes to Jiangning, why take so much trouble? Have the Fufeng bank there look it up. Would that not give us the total account?” “Ah!” Hu slapped his forehead. “My brain is failing me. Taking off my trousers to fart—quite unnecessary!” Before boarding the boat next morning, he had set the matter in motion with just two letters. One informed Zuo that there seemed to be no simpler way of remitting the Jiangsu offices’ military contributions than through Fufeng. Hu had therefore asked its Jiangning manager to prepare a statement and send it directly to Zuo’s headquarters; any omissions could be investigated and reported afterward. He also confirmed that he would come after the Lantern Festival. The second letter instructed the Jiangning manager to carry this out.

“When will you come back after the new year, Little Uncle?” Gu asked. “Around the time the lanterns go up.” “Good. I shall go to Nanjing with you then.” “Nothing would please me better, but it depends on Seventh Sister’s condition.” “She is sure to be well enough by then. And when you go home, do not mention A’qi’s illness. With the new year coming, why make your mother worry?” Hu was silent for some time. At last he sighed. “I truly never imagined Seventh Sister would care so deeply about what happened to me.” Gu hesitated, then decided to speak. “Since you have seen it, Little Uncle, I may as well say so. A’qi has been worrying about you for a long time. She often says a tall tree catches the wind. You must have made many enemies without intending to. That is the lesser concern. In recent years, the people you employ have been much poorer than before: some lack ability, others character. She cannot understand why your judgment has grown less sharp. Is your business too extensive and complicated? Have you less energy to oversee it? Or is there some other reason?” Hu flushed. He knew “some other reason” was Gu’s discreet way of putting it. In the end, the trouble came back to the dogskin plasters. “I know Seventh Sister means well. But she does worry rather more than she need. When she is a little better, tell her I shall think very carefully about every fault she has pointed out and make a thorough effort to correct it.” “That will put A’qi’s mind much more at ease,” Gu replied with pleasure.
