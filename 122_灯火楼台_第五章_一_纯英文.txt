CHAPTER FIVE: MADAM LUOSI

Hu Xueyan did not enter the New Year in quite the spirits of former years. The Seventh Lady’s stroke had left him with a sense of guilt he could not shake off. Nothing of this showed on the surface, however. The Hu household celebrated with its customary splendor and bustle, and no one was busier than Madam Luosi. The name was of long standing. Her surname was Luo, and she was the fourth in her family. Before her marriage she had been an exceptionally capable young woman of modest background. Everyone who knew her, old or young, called her Luo Sijie—Sister Four Luo—as a mark of respect. She had recognized Hu’s promise while he was still down on his luck and had helped him through his difficulties. Unfortunately, he already had a wife. Though they were fond of each other, they could not marry. Before long the Taiping uprising sent them fleeing in different directions. They lost all news of each other and did not meet again for nine years. Hu remembered their reunion clearly. It was the sixth year of Tongzhi. He had already been appointed to direct the Purchasing and Transport Bureau for the western campaign and spent most of his time in Shanghai. Shortly after Qingming, his old associate Fat Zhang died. Buddhist services were held at Jing’an Temple, and Hu went with Gu Yingchun and the Seventh Lady to pay his respects. There he saw a young married woman, lightly made up, offering incense. Her face was extraordinarily familiar, yet he could not remember where he had seen her. When she had finished, she left with a maid of thirteen or fourteen. Unwilling to let the matter rest, Hu quietly followed, wondering all the way who she could be.

Jing’an was Shanghai’s foremost ancient Buddhist temple, founded in the tenth year of Chiwu under the Wu emperor. Its grounds were extensive. Once celebrated for its Eight Views, it now retained only one, the Gushing Spring, also called the Boiling Well. The water churned throughout the year as though boiling, and Shanghai people called it an opening into the sea. A first-time visitor could hardly fail to go and look. The woman was no exception. Hu followed closely, then stopped nearby, pretending to be a traveler examining the well while he watched what she did. “A’hua, be careful! If you fall in, you’ll lose your life!” The maid had gone too close to the edge and was leaning forward to peer down. The danger made her mistress call out loudly. Her Hangzhou accent unlocked Hu’s memory. Could this be Luo Sijie? He was astonished and delighted. At first he thought of calling her name to make sure before approaching. But there were many visitors about. Shanghai might be comparatively liberal, yet it had not reached the Western custom of allowing men and women to greet each other openly in a crowd. After a moment’s thought, he turned to his page Guisheng and told him to fetch one of the Seventh Lady’s maids as quickly as possible. Guisheng ran off. Without first consulting the Seventh Lady, he found Caifeng, the more friendly toward him of the two maids she had brought. “Come with me. Something important. Quick!” Thinking he had got himself into trouble, she hurried after him. Only when Hu came into sight did Guisheng stop.

“It’s our master who wants you.” Hu pointed the woman out discreetly. “Caifeng, go and ask whether she is Sister Luo from Hangzhou. If she says yes, tell her that your mistress is a relative of Mr Hu’s and would like her to come over and meet her.” Caifeng was quick-witted. She considered this and asked, “What if she refuses?” “Just turn and look at me. Then she will certainly agree.” Events followed his prediction. When Caifeng approached the woman, there seemed at first to be some difficulty in making herself understood. Then she looked back at Hu. The woman followed her gaze, searching until she found him in the distance, and appeared deeply shaken. Hu knew that his plan had worked. He immediately turned and hurried to a monastic room set aside for the women guests. Finding the Seventh Lady’s other maid, he asked her to bring her mistress out for a word. “Seventh Sister, do you remember the Sister Luo I told you about?” After a moment she nodded. “I do.” “She is here today. I have had Caifeng issue a false imperial decree in your name: she has told her we are relatives and you want to meet her. She’ll be here any moment. Invite her home, Seventh Sister, and ask her carefully about her circumstances. She seems to be living as a widow.” “Of course, of course! And what about you, Little Uncle?” “I must go to the bank. As soon as I have dealt with something important, I’ll come straight over.”

Hu had been gone some time before Caifeng appeared with an attractive young woman in plain mourning clothes. Resting a hand on her maid’s shoulder, she advanced slowly with delicate steps. The impatient Seventh Lady strode forward on her unbound feet to meet her. “Are you Sister Luo?” “You are too kind. My name is Luo. May I ask yours?” “My husband’s name is Gu, my maiden name You, and I am the seventh in my family. Our Little Uncle calls me Seventh Sister. Please call me that too.” Frank as ever, she poured everything out at once. To Luo, it did not quite make sense. Why should someone she called Little Uncle call her Seventh Sister? That puzzle could wait. One more pressing question had to be settled first. “May I ask, Mrs Gu, who is this Little Uncle of yours?” “Who else but your old acquaintance Hu Xueyan, owner of the famous Fukang Bank?” Luo was both surprised and delighted. She had heard that Fukang’s proprietor was the Hu Xueyan who had once worked for Fat Zhang. She had long wanted to inquire but had found no opportunity. So it really was true. “You can tell from my calling him Little Uncle that we are close, Sister Luo. You must come and sit with me at home. He has told me how kind you were to him in the old days. He will be coming over shortly.” Hu had not forgotten her kindness after all! The thought set Luo’s heart in a turmoil. “Will you do me the honor, Sister Luo?” the Seventh Lady urged. “Oh, Mrs Gu, you are far too courteous. I really don’t deserve it.”

After taking leave of the bereaved family, the Seventh Lady brought Luo and her maid home. The style of the household, together with her hostess’s warmth and generosity, convinced Luo that she wanted to cultivate this friendship. She adopted the more intimate form of address and began to speak freely of her life. She had fled the fighting with her parents, traveling a thousand li in their wanderings, and both had died on the road. Remaining entirely alone was no solution; she had to choose someone to marry. They had been traveling in a party of three families. Two of the other families had unmarried sons in their twenties, both continually looking for opportunities to show her attentions. One family was rich and the other poor. Luo chose the poor family’s son, an only child named Cheng. “Seventh Sister, I chose him because, poor as he was, he wanted to make something of himself. So long as he had that ambition, I was confident I could help him rise. Besides, he had only an old mother. The other man had both parents, three brothers, and two sisters. Marrying into that household would have been like performing a play with a stone mortar on my head—all effort and no thanks.” “I would have chosen as you did, Sister Luo.” The Seventh Lady had already noticed the chrysanthemum made from white hair cord at her temple, but deliberately asked, “And where is Brother-in-Law Cheng? When will you bring him to meet us?” “He is gone,” Luo answered sadly. “He died at this time the year before last.” “Poor thing. Poor thing.” The Seventh Lady clasped her hand, offering the comfort for which there were no words.

“In a way, it was my fault,” Luo said. “He had learned the woodblock cutter’s trade. Once he told me that, two years before the Longhairs reached Hangzhou, he and his master went into the examination compound to cut the blocks for the provincial examination papers. He worked through the night until dawn, thinking: ‘I have studied too. If I must stay up all night, why shouldn’t I be sitting the examination for a juren degree, instead of working here as a lowly block cutter? If a candidate writes a wrong character, at worst there is a notice on blue paper. If I cut one wrong character, they beat my palms. I get a thrashing!’ So I told him, ‘If you really mean it, take down your shop sign and devote yourself to your books. Leave all seven daily household necessities to me. You needn’t worry about them.’ He took me at my word and studied with his head down, burning his lamp in the third watch and rising with the cock in the fifth…” “Sister Luo,” the Seventh Lady interrupted, “how did you provide those household necessities?” “I embroidered. And not only that: I collected work for embroidery shops. Later I became a small contractor, taking on a whole batch and distributing it among other women. We lived very comfortably. Shanghai is a prosperous place, Seventh Sister. There is silver lying everywhere, if only you are willing to take the trouble to pick it up. To tell you the truth, I don’t believe anyone need starve. People do starve, of course, but because there is no rice to be bought, even with money—not because they have no cash to buy it. That is a different matter. Wouldn’t you say so?” “Certainly!” The Seventh Lady smiled. “You sound very much like Little Uncle. What happened then?”

“After Hangzhou was recovered, he told me he would have to go back there to take the examination for a xiucai degree, and later the provincial examination as well. If we moved, all I had built up here would be lost. It would be better to purchase Imperial Academy student status and go straight to Beijing next time to sit for the juren degree. If he passed in the autumn, he could take the jinshi examination the following spring. Even if he won his juren in Zhejiang, he would still have to go to Beijing for the jinshi. Doing in two stages what could be done in one would be less economical. I thought he was right and scraped together two hundred taels to purchase the qualification for him. Then he studied harder than ever. Ah…” She sighed and could not continue. “Did the studying damage his health?” asked the Seventh Lady, who understood the ways of the world. “First he began coughing blood,” Luo said, quietly but calmly. “He hid it from me. He coughed into his handkerchief and washed it himself. His face grew paler and paler, yet in the afternoons his cheekbones looked as though he had put rouge on them. I was foolish and thought nothing of it. One day he came home from an engagement. I helped him take off his robe and happened to put my hand into a pocket. There was a bloodstained handkerchief. Only then did I realize he had consumption.” “Consumption?” The Seventh Lady looked alarmed. “Did he still go to the examinations?” “No. How could he, in that condition?” “What happened afterward?” Afterward, naturally, he tried to recover. People called consumption the craving sickness: the sufferer longed for one thing after another. Luo always procured whatever he wanted, but once it arrived, he took only a taste. What remained was not merely uneaten food, but his guilt. “I had heard that with a good appetite, consumption was not too serious. But he had terrible cravings and no appetite at all. He grew thinner every day. In only three months… Ah!” She sighed again.

There was no need to ask more about her husband. The Seventh Lady felt it was Luo herself who now needed concern. “Where are you living?” “In the southern city, behind the Catholic church.” “Life must be very difficult?” “I manage,” Luo answered without emphasis. “Any children?” “No.” Her answer was brisk, though inwardly she could not help feeling apologetic. “With no children, and still a long way from being old…” The Seventh Lady stopped short. This was their first meeting, after all; she could hardly go so far. As the conversation faltered, Luo rose. “Seventh Sister, I must leave. I’ll come to see you again tomorrow.” “No, no!” Her hostess stopped her at once. “Why wait until tomorrow? We have taken to each other like old friends. Don’t stand on ceremony. Stay for dinner, and I’ll send you home in the carriage.” Luo had only been saying something to fill the silence; she had no real wish to leave. With so warm an invitation, she was happy to comply. “You are quite right, Seventh Sister.” She sat down again. “I feel we have known each other forever. Perhaps we were connected in a former life.” “If you speak of a bond from a former life, I’m still less willing to let you go.” The Seventh Lady’s heart warmed further. “You cannot go on like this. Remaining a widow looks easy, but it is very hard. My advice is…” She did not finish, but there was no need to explain what she meant. Luo answered frankly, “I have no ambition to have a chastity arch raised in my honor. But this time I must choose very carefully.”

While they were talking, Hu arrived. “It really is Sister Luo!” He gazed at her, overwhelmed by conflicting feelings. Countless things he wished to say crowded his throat, and he could not decide where to begin. Luo seemed the more composed of the two. Rising, she said, “I used to call you by your name. Now I don’t know what to call you.” “Go on calling me Xueyan.” “That would hardly be proper. You are a great proprietor now. To keep calling you by your name would show no sense of what is fitting.” The Seventh Lady intervened. “Everyone calls him Mr Hu, or simply Sir. You may do the same, Sister Luo.” “Very well. That is a respectful form. Sir, it must be nine years since we last met?” Hu calculated silently. “Nine years! Yet it feels like another lifetime. After Hangzhou was recovered, Governor-General Zuo put me in charge of relief and restoration. I had people inquire for you everywhere, without the slightest news. Where were you then?” “Already in Shanghai.” “How did you come to Shanghai?” “That is a long story.” The Seventh Lady thought the account would take some time. Having heard it already, she need not sit there idly. She stood up. “Sister Luo, Little Uncle, you are both staying for dinner. I’ll see to it. Take your time talking.” All she needed to do was give a few instructions: order dishes from a restaurant and send word to Gu that there was a guest, Hu was there too, and he should cancel any dinner engagement if he could and come home to help entertain them. Then she sat in a small room adjoining the sitting room, with the door open, resting her eyes and listening to the old friends reminisce.

“Sister Luo,” she heard Hu say, “you helped me so much in the old days. Now I can at last stand on my own feet. If there is anything I can do for you, please say so.” “Thank you. I can still get along. When I can no longer manage, I shall ask for your help, Sir.” “But living alone like this is no lasting solution.” The Seventh Lady’s interest stirred. She rose quietly and looked in from a distance. Hu and Luo were gazing into each other’s eyes. Their old affection had been rekindled, she thought. She was right. Hu felt that Luo had changed during their nine years apart. In the old days she had been a young woman of modest means whose long braid swung to and fro, whose waist swayed briskly as she walked, and whose eyes were always glancing about. Strangers never made her blush. Now she was much quieter, and her complexion had grown fairer and clearer. The sharply contrasted black and white of her eyes, set in an unpainted oval face, no longer moved about as restlessly as before. Yet an occasional glance at him seemed charged with countless things she wished to tell. Most touching of all was the chrysanthemum of white cord beside her loose, drooping hair knot. Hu had a particular taste for young widows, and this white flower of mourning stirred his imagination more than anything. “What do you think of this?” he asked. “Let me help you open an embroidery shop in Hangzhou.” “No. I don’t want to return to Hangzhou.” “Why?” “I am used to living in Shanghai.” “Then open it in Shanghai?” “Thank you,” she said. “Let me think it over.”

The Seventh Lady would have liked to listen longer, but Gu had returned. She had to intercept him, explain briefly who their new guest was, and then bring him into the sitting room. “Ah, Brother-in-Law Gu.” Luo gathered her jacket neatly and made her greeting with easy grace. The intimate form of address soon dispelled Gu’s feeling of unfamiliarity, and he talked to her as he would to an old acquaintance. Before long the restaurant dishes arrived and they went in to dine. Everyone urged Luo to take the place of honor, but she would not hear of it. In the end Hu occupied it as usual. The four of them sat one on each side of a square table. “Sister Luo can drink,” Hu told the Seventh Lady. “And she has an excellent capacity.” “Then grape wine will be too light. What do you prefer, Sister Luo? Shall we warm some Huadiao?” “Thank you. I no longer drink.” “Why give it up? Living by yourself, you need a little wine. One good bout of drinking dissolves a thousand sorrows.” “Listen to yourself!” Gu reproached her. “You sound drunk before you have touched a drop. Sister Luo is managing perfectly well. Why should she drown her sorrows?” “Very well, I spoke out of turn.” The Seventh Lady yielded, then urged her guest again. “Break your rule for me. I’ll have a couple of cups with you.” “You are too kind. If you insist, I will.” “Then that is settled. What shall we have?” “Whatever you have.” “I only drink for amusement. I’m afraid you may not like it.”

The Seventh Lady fetched a bottle of mint liqueur from a cabinet. Its gourd-shaped bottle and emerald-green contents were so attractive that Luo’s appetite for a drink revived. “I’ll have some too,” Hu said, joining in the spirit of the occasion. With his glass in hand, he looked at the Seventh Lady. “I have been urging Sister Luo to open an embroidery shop. What do you think?” “Sir, I have considered it,” Luo interposed. “Thank you for your kindness, but it is beyond my strength. Even if you supply the capital, there must be people to do the work. I could not oversee it all alone.” “Why worry about that? Ask Seventh Sister to help, and let Yingchun look after outside business. I’ll send you two reliable, experienced assistants as well. You need only be the proprietor of a business ready-made for you.” “There is not much satisfaction in having everything handed to me.” The conversation was beginning to go awry. Gu thought it better to leave the matter for the time being and changed the subject. As host, he naturally chose something familiar or interesting to the guest, and so they came to Gu embroidery. Chinese embroidery fell into three schools: Hunan’s Xiang embroidery, Suzhou’s Su embroidery, and the Shanghai style uniquely called Gu embroidery. The last had a long history and a considerable tradition behind it. Luo proved surprisingly well able to explain it. “Everyone knows that it was handed down by a concubine of the Gu family at Luxiang Garden. They tell me the place where I now live stands on the garden’s old grounds…”

Luxiang Garden stood in the northwestern corner of the walled city. Gu Mingru, a Ming prefect of Daozhou, had originally built it under the name Retreat of Ten Thousand Bamboos. His younger brother Gu Mingshi became a jinshi in the thirty-eighth year of Jiajing and rose to Assistant Director of the Imperial Seals Office. Retiring home with a substantial fortune from his official career, he saw that much ground remained vacant east of the retreat and enlarged it, digging a pond. The site proved to have held a pond before: an unearthed stone inscription supplied the evidence. It bore the words “Dew-Fragrance Pond,” in Zhao Zi’ang’s own calligraphy. Gu Mingshi therefore renamed the retreat Luxiang Garden, the Garden of Dew Fragrance, and naturally restored the pond’s old name. Among his many concubines was one surnamed Ying. While in Beijing she had learned embroidery, including techniques passed down from the palace. Gifted in the craft, she improved on them until her work became still more exquisite. With fine needles and close stitches, her silks of five colors blended seamlessly from light to dark; the beauty of her color combinations went without saying. Above all, Gu embroidery used the needle for a brush and silk thread for pigment, taking famous paintings as models. Landscapes, figures, flowers, and birds all possessed a living spirit and unrivaled delicacy. It was then known as “painted embroidery.” Mistress Ying once embroidered a version of Zhao Zi’ang’s Eight Steeds. Dong Qichang declared that even Zhao himself, working with a brush, might not surpass her. She also made a Pausing the Needle, extraordinarily expressive and beautiful, in which painting and embroidery were indistinguishable. A Yangzhou salt merchant later obtained it in exchange for a set of linked jade rings from the Han dynasty and a painting of a beauty by the celebrated Southern Tang artist Zhou Fang. Under Mistress Ying’s instruction, all the women of Luxiang Garden, down to the maids, became highly accomplished embroiderers. Their fame overshadowed Gu Mingshi’s own name, until one day, after drinking, he complained that his reputation hung between their ten fingers.

The name “Gu embroidery,” however, came into use only after the beginning of the Qing. Gu Mingshi had a granddaughter who married a man named Zhang. Widowed at twenty-four with a year-old son, she supported the orphan and maintained her widowhood entirely through the work of her slender fingers. Her embroidery equaled Mistress Ying’s. Besides pictures, she embroidered ordinary designs, and business flourished. “Gu embroidery” replaced “painted embroidery” as the name of the craft and became known throughout the country. It was now one of Shanghai’s celebrated products, practiced in household after household; even men learned to embroider. Luo told the story with such command that Hu and the Seventh Lady listened with keen enjoyment. Gu was somewhat distracted, however. Hu had six or seven engagements at changsan houses that evening. At half an hour in each, plus travel, they would take at least four hours. As soon as Luo reached a pause, he reminded Hu, “We ought to go.” Hu frowned. “Which can we miss?” “Best to go to all of them. If you absolutely cannot, there are two you must attend.” “Very well. We’ll go to those two. What about Sister Luo? Someone ought to see her home.” “There is no need,” the Seventh Lady said. “It is such a long way into the city, and at night too.” Clearly, she meant to keep her guest overnight and would accept no argument. Luo wanted to stay, but only an old manservant was at home to watch the house. If she did not return, he would worry, and she could not bear to cause him that anxiety.

“That is easily settled,” Gu said. “Give me your address, Sister Luo, and I’ll send someone to let him know.” The two men left the table first. When the women had finished, the Seventh Lady took Luo to a room reserved for female guests, checked the bedding and other necessities, and invited her to remove her makeup and settle comfortably over tea and conversation. They talked until midnight. A late supper was a daily custom in the Gu household, and while they were eating congee, Gu returned—with Hu beside him. “You didn’t go home, Little Uncle?” the Seventh Lady remarked casually. “I felt like some congee,” Hu answered just as casually. Everyone understood that he had come expressly to see Luo. With her makeup removed, she wore her hair in a loose, thick braid, and had put on wide-legged trousers and a little lined jacket. By convention this was bedroom dress, unsuitable for receiving male visitors. But since they had come upon her in it, she could only behave naturally, as though nothing were amiss. “Which two houses did you visit?” the Seventh Lady asked. “Yajun’s Fifth Miss in Huile Lane, and Qiuyuelou’s Fourth Miss in Zhoujin Lane.” “Hadn’t Fourth Miss left the profession to marry? Has she merely ‘taken a bath’ and come back?” “It wasn’t that she meant it to be just a bath,” Hu replied. “The Qiu family’s senior wife drove her out.” “Is she still so thin?” “She has filled out a little.” “That’s good. She needs a little more flesh to look her best.”

As they talked, Luo kept glancing from one to another in surprise. The Seventh Lady noticed. “Have you ever visited a courtesan house, Sister Luo?” “Never. I have never even heard of such a thing.” “Among women, only this wife of mine goes visiting them,” Gu said, a little embarrassed. “Would you like her to take you and broaden your experience?” “Thank you, thank you!” Luo laughed and made a hesitant movement with her hand. “I wouldn’t dare.” “What is there to fear?” the Seventh Lady encouraged her. “You learn something from every experience. Once you have visited those houses, you will understand why men in their fifties and sixties enter a ruinous turn in their fortunes. And once you understand, your own husband won’t suffer that fate.” “How does that follow?” “Because you will know how a woman can hold a man’s heart. Don’t force him to do things he dislikes. Say less of what he doesn’t want to hear. Whatever he cares about, take it to heart as though it concerned you yourself. Once you can do that, let him visit courtesans and drink with them as much as he pleases. His heart will still belong to you.” “No wonder Brother-in-Law Gu is so obedient!” Luo said with a smile. “Obedient may not be the word,” Gu answered, trying to save face. “But I probably won’t suffer that turn of fortune.” “There you are!” the Seventh Lady urged. “We’ll go for tea and conversation, and if we feel like it, order a whole banquet. You’ll learn something. We won’t be going with the men, so what is there to fear?” “I have no need to learn about that. I am alone in the world. Such knowledge would be no use to me.” Raising her head as she spoke, she met Hu’s eyes squarely. She looked away at once, only to encounter the Seventh Lady’s half-smiling gaze. For no reason she could explain, she felt guilty and blushed, hardly knowing what to do with herself.

“Seventh Sister! Yingchun!” Hu began talking about something quite unrelated, drawing their attention away to rescue Luo from her embarrassment. Finding an opening in the conversation, she touched her faintly burning cheeks and said, “I mustn’t drink any more. Another glass and I shall be drunk.” “Not at all. One can tell at a glance whether someone can hold her drink. Perhaps the wine is not to your taste.” “Probably,” Gu agreed. “Mint liqueur is sweet. People who drink well generally dislike sweet flavors. Would you care for a couple of glasses of brandy, Sister Luo?” “Mixing two kinds will make me drunk.” “No, no!” the Seventh Lady put in. “Foreigners drink several kinds at one meal, some before it and some afterward. They put all those different drinks into their stomachs, and I’ve never seen anything wrong with them.” “Really?” Since Luo did not seem firmly opposed, Gu fetched a bottle of three-star brandy. As he worked at the cork with a corkscrew, she spoke again. “I have heard that if you rub the crescent-shaped label at the top with a damp cloth, three blue marks appear. Without those marks, the brandy is counterfeit.” “That is new to us. Let us try.” A damp handkerchief was brought, and they rubbed and rubbed, without the least result. Luo said calmly, “That shows how little one can trust hearsay. There could hardly be counterfeit wine in your house.”

“That is not certain either. We must taste it to decide.” The Seventh Lady fetched two crystal glasses and said to her husband, “You will have to keep Sister Luo company.” “What about you, Mr Hu?” Luo asked. “I have little capacity. Please go ahead.” The Seventh Lady returned to the proposed visit. “Well, Sister Luo, what day shall it be?” Hu interposed with a joking air. “Seventh Sister, how about helping me arrange a tea visit with a little ‘border’ added?” “Who wants you adding a border? We want none of your ‘border’—and we mean to snip your border away as well!” Watching them joke so freely, without the slightest affectation or awkwardness, Luo understood how close they were. The Seventh Lady was not merely warm and frank; Hu evidently listened to her. If Luo intended anything with Hu, she thought, she would first have to win the Seventh Lady over. Her tone changed accordingly. She had regarded courtesan houses as disreputable places and refused even to discuss them. Now she asked of her own accord, “Seventh Sister, what does ‘snip your border away’ mean?” “Steal someone’s sweetheart.” Leaning toward her with playful curiosity, the Seventh Lady lowered her voice a little. “I’ll take you to see Little Uncle’s favorite. A true Suzhou woman! Once you sit down and hear her speak, you won’t want to leave.” “A true Suzhou woman?” Luo was puzzled. “Can there be false ones?” “Of course. Ask them, and they all say they come from Mudu in Suzhou. In fact, they have merely learned a courtesan-house version of the Suzhou dialect.” “Suzhou speech is Suzhou speech. What do you mean by a courtesan-house version?” “I can’t imitate it. Come and listen, and you’ll understand.” “Very well!” Having resisted so firmly, Luo used this opening to change her position. “I’ll go with you one day and broaden my experience.”
