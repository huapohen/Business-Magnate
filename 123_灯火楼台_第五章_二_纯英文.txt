INSTALLMENT 123
Towers of Light — Chapter Five, Part Two

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

“You may go,” Gu warned, half in jest, “but take care that your adventures do not appear in the Shen Bao.” “Ah!” Hu suddenly raised his voice. “That mention of the Shen Bao reminds me of something. Since the Tianjin–Shanghai telegraph opened last winter, I have noticed that some of its news arrives by wire. With Sheng Xingsun directing the telegraph administration, he is exceptionally well informed. In certain kinds of business, if our news comes later than his—even one step later—we can suffer a serious loss. I have been wondering, Yingchun, whether we could obtain help from the newspaper's reporters.” “To telegraph market quotations?” “Yes.” “Then why not station someone of our own in Tianjin and have him send a daily report in cipher?” “That would be of limited use. There are things only reporters can discover. Besides, I do not mean merely conditions in the market. I mean conditions at court too. Take the impeachment of the elder Mr Li last winter…”

The elder Mr Li was Li Hanzhang. For all her outward bluntness, the Seventh Lady was inwardly perceptive. Once the conversation turned to the political fortunes of senior statesmen, she knew that many consequential remarks ought not to be overheard by people unconnected with the matter. If repeated, they might cause trouble for both Hu and Gu. She quietly drew Luo Sijie aside, signalling with her eyes that they should leave the table. “Once they begin, they will talk for ever. Let us go over there and have our own conversation.” Luo immediately understood and cooperated. “I have things to say that I would rather not discuss before them. Seventh Sister, I feel rather unsettled.” “Why?” Instead of answering, Luo led her to a long red-velvet easy chair, where they sat side by side. With one hand she held the Seventh Lady's; with the other she kept touching her wine-flushed cheeks. “Are you unwell?” the Seventh Lady asked anxiously. “Why should you suddenly feel unsettled?” “It is not that.” Luo seemed to find the words difficult. “I never dreamed that a day like this would come: meeting Xueyan again, and getting to know you. I feel like a family that buys its firewood a handful at a time and suddenly wins a prize in the White Pigeon lottery. I hardly know what to do.” Though a native of Songjiang, the Seventh Lady understood Hangzhou expressions through her acquaintance with Hu. Luo meant that an ordinary poor person had suddenly won a fortune. The comparison pleased her. She had liked Luo already and now felt still closer to her. Finding a suitable reply was difficult, however, so she merely smiled and said, “Surely not! Surely not!”

“Why not? When has a widow like me ever had a day of talking, laughing, and drinking like this? He offers to help me open an embroidery shop, and you offer to take me to a courtesan house. I could never have imagined such things in this life—or even a previous one.” Her delight was unmistakable. Taking one of Luo's hands between her own warm, plump palms, the Seventh Lady quietly asked, “Helping you open a shop would take no more than a word from him. What do you really think of it, Sister Luo?” Luo lowered her eyes without replying, as though troubled by something she could not express. “Say something. If you want it, you want it; if you do not, you do not. Nobody can force you.” “How could I not want it? But, Seventh Sister…” She suddenly looked up. “What would that make me?” “The proprietor.” “The person who supplies the capital is the proprietor. The capital would not be mine.” At first the Seventh Lady was puzzled. Becoming the owner of a ready-made business was a wonderful opportunity; why hesitate? Then understanding dawned. “The proprietor's wife, then?” Luo lowered her head again. “That is exactly what I fear people would think.” By speaking of other people rather than herself, she conveyed a subtle implication. At such moments the Seventh Lady was far from indiscreet. She would not venture a delicate remark until she had judged its precise effect.

“You are still young, Sister Luo,” she finally said, testing the ground. “And with no children, what is the point of remaining a widow?” Before supper, Luo had spoken of her past, vaguely implying that she had no children. Hearing this repeated, she felt she should correct it at once to demonstrate her honesty. “I have a daughter,” she said. “She lives with her maternal grandmother.” “Where is her grandmother?” “Hangzhou.” “A daughter is different from a son. In the end, she belongs to another family. Depending on a son-in-law may be tolerable if the young couple get along well, but otherwise that ready-provided meal can be hard to swallow. Especially if his mother is still living: the looks a daughter's mother receives from her fellow mother-in-law can be very unpleasant.” “I shall never depend on a son-in-law,” Luo replied. Her voice was calm, each word clear and confident. “Whom will you depend on, then?” “Myself.”

“Then you need something dependable all the more.” The Seventh Lady meant that Luo should accept Hu's financial assistance. But during supper, the various thoughts drifting through Luo's mind had gradually crystallized into a resolution: if she accepted anything from Hu, it must be more than an embroidery shop. Otherwise, she would accept nothing. Understanding perfectly, she pretended not to. The Seventh Lady did not believe she had missed the meaning; silence must conceal some further calculation. “Am I not right? It is admirable to rely on yourself, but you must have something secure in your hands. Embroidery depends on youth, clear eyesight, and accurate fingers. In a few years, you will no longer be able to rely on them.” The dependable alternative was plainly a shop. Luo could not continue pretending incomprehension. After a pause, she said, “As for opening an embroidery shop, if I work hard for another two or three years and bring in one or two women friends as partners, I can manage it myself.” Did she consider Hu's offer insufficient, or was she too proud and upright to accept favours? The Seventh Lady could not yet tell and fell silent. Luo suddenly asked whether Hu's mother was still alive. “Very much alive and vigorous. Have you met her?” “Yes.” “And Mrs Hu? Have you met her too?” “Yes.” Luo gave a soft sigh. Now the Seventh Lady understood completely. Neither Hu nor Luo had forgotten their former affection. There would be no obstacle on his side; if Luo were willing to accept a less exalted position, the arrangement might prove a happy one. Affection required willingness on both sides. She decided to give them opportunities to draw closer on their own. If their feelings truly accorded, there would still be time to act as matchmaker once matters had developed.

“A'qi!” Gu called. “Little Uncle is leaving.” Turning, the Seventh Lady saw the maid helping Hu into his riding jacket. “Little Uncle, today does not count. Tomorrow evening I shall formally entertain Sister Luo. Are you free?” Before he could answer, Luo protested politely. “Seventh Sister, you are much too kind.” “It is not excessive kindness. It is only proper. And even if it is a formality, it will be the only time.” Luo fell silent. Hu smiled at her. “That is one of Seventh Sister's talents. A casual sentence, and she closes your mouth, leaving you nothing to say.” “I still have something to say: obedience is better than excessive courtesy.” “Now that,” the Seventh Lady replied, “really is too polite.” “Then here is something less polite: just this once, and no precedent.” “Very well. No precedent.” Hu and Gu exchanged a glance of shared understanding. Luo was a woman of mettle too, matching the Seventh Lady's wit point for point.

“Enough talk,” the Seventh Lady said. “Are you free tomorrow evening or not, Little Uncle?” “Even if I am not, I shall make time.” “See what consideration he shows you, Sister Luo!” “I merely benefit from your influence.” “Where shall we dine?” Hu asked. The Seventh Lady consulted her husband. “At home, I think?” He agreed. “Then it is settled. But I must make one thing clear beforehand. Sister Luo is staying here tonight. Tomorrow morning I shall take her home and fetch her again in the afternoon. Taking her home tomorrow night, Little Uncle, will be your responsibility.” This tested Luo's feelings. If she had no interest in Hu, she would certainly object. A man escorting an unattached woman home late at night would provoke much comment among the neighbours. If she raised that objection, the Seventh Lady could not insist. Refusal would also be easy: she could simply say that it would be inconvenient to return so late and that she wished to stay at the Gu house again. Instead, she said, “Mr Hu has so many engagements. We ought not to take up more of his time.” “None! None!” Hu answered quickly. “I have no engagement tomorrow evening.” The Seventh Lady smiled at Luo, who grew a little embarrassed and looked away.

The next morning the Seventh Lady took Luo home. She lived in Nanshi, in a two-storey stone-gated house on a lane inhabited by comfortably situated families. The living quarters were upstairs and the reception room below. The latter was already crowded, most of the visitors carrying flat parcels. A middle-aged woman approached first, sounding reproachful. “Where were you all yesterday, Sister Luo? My son needs a doctor. I am waiting to deliver my work and get the money.” “Oh!” Luo replied apologetically. “I stayed at my sister's last night.” Nobody had heard that she had a sister, and everyone looked curiously at the Seventh Lady. Her prosperous appearance and fine clothes were impressive enough; the pair of jade bracelets at her wrists and the dazzling diamond ring, its stone as large as a soybean, inspired still greater respect. Yet she was entirely unassuming and cheerful. “Do not attend to me. Everyone is waiting. Get on with your work, and I shall help.” “Nothing could be better! Old Ma! Old Ma!” Old Ma was her assistant, a man in his fifties who kept the accounts, answered the door, and occasionally did odd jobs. Honest but slow in speech and movement, he had been unable to cope with so many people delivering and collecting work and had simply hidden in a side room. Only when called did he emerge. Normally Luo and he handled all the receiving and issuing themselves. With another pair of hands, things went more smoothly, but it was still noon before they finished. “I am so sorry to have kept you working all morning,” Luo said. She told Old Ma to order food from a restaurant, intending to keep her guest for lunch.

“There is no need. I came to learn where you live and shall return to fetch you later. There are things to arrange at home. I shall not even go upstairs now; I can see your room this afternoon.” Luo welcomed this, for her bedroom was bound to contain some untidiness unsuitable for a visitor. “Then I shall not detain you. Just send a carriage this afternoon; you need not trouble to come yourself. Tomorrow evening I should like you and Brother-in-Law Gu to have a simple meal here. I wonder whether he is free.” “We can discuss that later.” Once her guest had gone, Luo could relax. After lunch she took her customary nap. On waking, she remembered that Hu would come that evening and summoned the maid and Old Ma to help scrub the floor, wipe the tables, and clean the windows. She changed the bedding, brought out a fine porcelain tea service seldom used, and prepared four dishes of fruit and nuts. Then she dressed. Since she normally wore no makeup, she needed only to arrange her hair with particular care before changing her clothes and sitting down to wait. At five, voices sounded below, and the maid hurried upstairs. “Mr Hu has come.” Luo had not expected him to fetch her himself. Fortunately everything was ready, so she could invite him upstairs. At the stairhead she called, “How kind of you to come, Mr Hu. Would you like to sit upstairs for a moment?” “Gladly!” His figure followed his voice, and she quickly stepped aside. Jiangsu and Zhejiang observed many restrictions between men and women. On stairs, a man must ascend first and a woman follow; descending, the order was reversed. A woman's skirt must not rise above a man's head, or it would bring him bad luck. That was why she moved aside. By the time he reached the top, she was holding the door curtain for him herself.

Entering, Hu looked around and nodded. “How neatly everything is kept. Plenty of sunlight too. An auspicious place.” “A widow's household, without even a son—how prosperous can it become?” He had not expected so direct and suggestive an opening and could not decide how to respond. He merely smiled. The maid brought tea. Following Hangzhou hospitality, Luo took a little of everything from the stemmed dishes—dried longans, lychees, melon seeds, and pine-nut sweets—and placed it before him. She modestly declared the refreshments unworthy, while he repeatedly thanked her. “I have a small token for you, Sister Luo. You must do me the honour of accepting it. The man who came with me is carrying a presentation box. Please ask the maid to bring it up.” The box was ebony inlaid with silver wire, with a small silver lock and its key tied to the clasp. Inside lay three folded account books and a little ivory case.

Hu picked up two of the books and handed them over. “One is for Yuanli; the other for Wang Taihe.” These were two of Shanghai's celebrated large firms, one dealing in imported and Cantonese general merchandise, the other in produce from north and south. Luo opened the books. Both accounts were in Fukang Bank's name, and the first page bore a stamped instruction: goods could be obtained against the book, with settlement at the three annual festivals. Whatever she needed to eat, wear, or use, she could collect from either firm; Fukang would pay. This was generous enough. But the third book made her heart leap: it recorded a fixed-term deposit of ten thousand taels at Fukang under the name Weiji. “I had intended to call it Luoji, but that name was already taken. Splitting the written surname Luo into its components suggested Siweiji, but that account name existed too. So I dropped Si and kept Weiji. Here…” (An editorial note in the supplied text refers to the traditional form of Luo, but the character it is meant to display is missing.) He picked up the little ivory case. “A seal to go with it.” Luo took the book and ivory seal and set them on the table. Neither declining nor explicitly accepting, she merely said, “You really have become wealthy, Mr Hu. Ten thousand taels, and you call it a small token.” “If I did not, how could I persuade you to take it?” “If I refuse, you will argue, and we shall waste our energy. Fortunately, whether to give is your decision, and whether to use it is mine. I shall keep the three books and the seal here.” Her words and actions always followed a considered design. Hu felt no further persuasion was needed. “Then put them away safely, and let us go.”

“How are we going?” “You will see when you go downstairs.” Hu had arrived by sedan chair and provided a particularly handsome one for Luo. Thoughtfully, he had also brought a smaller chair for her woman servant or young maid. Three attendants accompanied him in new blue lined robes, black satin waistcoats, and light, thin-soled boots. Since they rode horses, the skirts of their robes were tucked into their belts. Each looked alert and carried himself with impeccable courtesy. Luo cared about appearances too. She could not help thinking how imposing an employer would look with such attendants when going out. Just as she was about to enter the chair, she remembered something and returned inside. She ought to bring gifts to the Gu household, and embroidery was ready to hand. For the Seventh Lady she selected two coverlets, a pair of pillows, a set of chair covers, and material for two skirts. Valuable as these were, they were surpassed by the gift for Gu: a vertical embroidered panel copied from Emperor Huizong's painting of a peacock, a genuine example of Gu embroidery. When the gifts were displayed at the Gu house, the Seventh Lady was delighted. “These are substantial presents, but I shall not stand on ceremony. First, we have many years ahead of us, and opportunities to return one another's kindness. Second, I truly like them.” She immediately had the chair covers arranged. Peonies embroidered on pink satin gave the room a rich splendour.

“Seventh Sister, hold these skirt lengths against yourself. I embroidered them.” One was red, covered with a hundred butterflies in dazzling colours. “It is beautiful, but on me it would be an ugly woman making a spectacle of herself,” the Seventh Lady said. “A bride of about twenty, returning to visit her parents, would look splendid in it. I shall keep it for my daughter.” Hu sprang from his chair. “Yingchun! Will you be inviting me to eat red eggs?” The couple had only one son, but the Seventh Lady had often spoken of wanting a daughter. Hu had noticed her thickened waist, and her words now made him suspect that she was expecting. Gu smiled without answering, tacitly confirming it. Luo took the Seventh Lady's hand. “Congratulations! How many months?” “Four,” she answered softly. “Four months! Quickly, sit down. It would be dreadful to disturb the baby.” “It is all right. The foreign doctor says I should walk about regularly to make the birth easier.” “How broad-minded you are, consulting a foreign doctor even about pregnancy!” With no outsiders present, and knowing her companion's frankness, Luo teasingly asked whether she had even allowed the doctor to feel her belly. “Of course. How else could the doctor tell whether the baby was lying properly?” Luo had meant it as a joke; discovering it was true left her speechless. The Seventh Lady was quite unconcerned. “There is nothing strange or shameful about it.” Luo shook her head repeatedly. “I could never do that, even if I died.” Gu laughed. “Do not let her deceive you, Sister Luo. She is teasing. The doctor is indeed foreign—but a woman.” “I thought as much!” Luo breathed with relief. “Would you not be afraid of a foreign man's hand, covered in black hair like a bear's paw, touching your belly?”

The Seventh Lady merely smiled and took up the other skirt length. It was pale blue satin, embroidered along the lower edge with waves and, above them, two birds she could not identify. The design was both novel and graceful. “I like this one. I shall have it made tomorrow. When the skirt moves, it will look just like a flowing tide. How did you think of it?” “One of my customers, the Zhang family's second young mistress, is very learned. We get along so well that whenever I visit, we talk for half a day. Once, somehow, she mentioned an old line: ‘Her six-panelled skirt trails the waters of the Xiang.’ It gave me an idea, and I designed this when I went home. Pale blue satin soils easily. I shall embroider another for you to alternate with it.” “There is no need. I do not wear skirts very often.” Meanwhile, Hu and Gu were examining the Gu embroidery. Viewed from different angles, the spread-tailed peacock's colours changed. Camellias, bamboo, and rocks completed a composition that rewarded endless attention. “Why not frame it and hang it?” Hu suggested. Gu immediately summoned a servant and ordered a rosewood frame, with a sheet of glass obtained from a foreign trading house, preferably that very day. They next examined the coverlets and pillows. Laughing at herself, the Seventh Lady said it was almost like inspecting a trousseau, which made the servants laugh too.

“I am hungry,” Hu said. “Can we dine soon?” “Everything is ready. We shall begin at once.” The arrangement was much as on the previous evening, but Gu had specially engaged a Cantonese cook. The dishes were excellent and unusual, delighting Luo's palate and broadening her experience. One consisted of two fish, one red-braised and one steamed, on a double-fish-shaped porcelain platter, one side white and the other yellow. Even Hu had never seen anything like it. “Gold and Silver Fish,” Gu explained. “A dish presented as tribute.” “By whom?” Hu asked in surprise. “By the time cooked fish reached the palace, even if it had not spoiled, it would no longer taste good.” “Naturally, it is cooked there and eaten fresh. As for who presents it, you might never guess: the Duke Yansheng of Qufu in Shandong.” “Ah!” Hu remembered. “I have heard that his household serves its most distinguished guests what is called ‘household cuisine.’ Is this an example?” “Exactly. There are a hundred and thirty-six dishes. Their quality is no surprise; what is remarkable is that each requires a specially made plate or bowl. The service includes gold, silver, pewter, and porcelain. If one piece is missing, the whole table setting is unusable. When the duke entertains distinguished guests, a dependable old family servant has sole charge of the tableware.” “And for imperial tribute, surely the service is gold?” “Certainly. At great palace celebrations, such as the Tongzhi emperor's wedding or Cixi's fortieth birthday, the duke goes to Beijing to offer congratulations, taking his cooks, tableware, and rare ingredients. Permission must first be sought for a day on which the court will enjoy his household cuisine. The food is prepared and delivered at the appointed time, though some dishes are cooked inside the palace itself. That requires advance arrangements with the chief eunuch—and naturally a gratuity for gaining access. Fortunately, the duke's estates are extensive, so he need not worry about the expense.”

Hu was much attracted by this account. “Did today's cook come from the duke's household?” “No, he is Cantonese. But his grandfather did. There is an interesting story behind it.” Gu slowly raised his glass and said no more. “If it is interesting, tell us!” his impatient wife urged. “Do not keep us waiting.” “I only heard it a couple of days ago and cannot quite remember. Let me think.” “Take your time.” Luo offered him a piece of fish. “A story is best when it has a proper beginning.” “Very well. To begin: late in Qianlong's reign…” Bi Qiufan was then governor of Shandong. Ruan Yuan had enjoyed early success and been in the Hanlin Academy only a few years when a major examination of Hanlin and Household of the Heir Apparent officials was held. Qianlong himself set the questions. The prescribed subject for the examination poem was spectacles. It was difficult: spectacles had entered China from the West only late in the Ming, so ancient poetry and prose offered no established allusion to them. Moreover, the required rhyme was ta, an awkward choice that made the task harder still. Every candidate looked distressed. Ruan was originally placed second in the first class, but Qianlong promoted him to first, saying that his rhapsody was excellent. In fact, it was the poem that pleased him, particularly a couplet to the effect that four eyes had no need of these things, and double pupils had no use for them. The emperor had been unusually favoured by Heaven: even after eighty, his hearing and eyesight remained keen, and he wore no spectacles, a fact of which he often boasted to his ministers. Ruan had used the allusions to Shun's four eyes and double pupils to flatter him, implying that he perceived people and affairs so clearly that spectacles were unnecessary.

First place in this examination traditionally brought promotion through three grades. Ruan rose at once from Hanlin Compiler to junior supervisor in the Household of the Heir Apparent, and soon became educational commissioner of Shandong. Still under thirty, he had lost his wife and had not remarried. His father had come to live with him there. Bi Qiufan told the elder Ruan, “My daughter could marry the Duke Yansheng; I should like you to arrange it. The duke's elder sister could marry your son; I shall arrange that match.” Thus Ruan became a son-in-law of the Kong family. The duke's household paid particular attention to food, following the tradition of Confucius, who did not consider food too finely prepared. Four cooks therefore accompanied Miss Kong as part of her marriage establishment. One was surnamed He; his grandson was the cook Gu had engaged that day. “How, then, did he become Cantonese?” Hu asked. “Ruan later became Governor-General of Liangguang, a famously lucrative post, and entertained frequently. Although the food did not equal the duke's household cuisine, it far surpassed anything available commercially. Since the household name could not be used, some called it the Manchu–Han Grand Banquet. The Governor-General's cooks were often borrowed to prepare meals elsewhere. That is how Cook He's grandfather settled in Guangdong, and the family became Cantonese.”

At this moment the shark's fin arrived, and Cook He came before the diners wearing a red-tasselled cap to pay his respects. Such was the custom when the principal dish was served; host and guests were expected to reward him. Hu, generous as ever, casually picked out a banknote for a hundred taels. “How can four people finish such a dish?” Luo asked. “I almost feel the expense on Seventh Sister's behalf.” The fin came on a silver platter two chi and five cun across, enough for twelve people. Four diners could hardly do justice to it. The Seventh Lady had a plan. “We are all friends. There is no need for ceremony. Let us put half aside.” Even half was rather too much. After two small bowls, Hu rubbed his stomach and declared himself full. He then asked why he had never heard of this cook before. “He has only recently arrived from Guangzhou,” Gu replied. “He wants to open a restaurant, but the arrangements have not yet been settled.” “What arrangements?” “Someone is offering the capital. They must agree on terms.” “Ask whether he would be willing to come to me. What I lack now is a good cook.” “Very well. I shall ask him.”

After dinner they sat talking. When the clock struck nine, the Seventh Lady urged Hu to take Luo home. Nine was early in Shanghai, where the city remained awake through the night. In places such as Zhoujin Lane, devoted to song, women, and extravagant pleasure, business was only beginning. But both Hu and Luo understood that the Seventh Lady was deliberately giving them an opportunity to become closer, and neither objected. As they were entering their chairs, she told the bearers to place a large two-tier food box in the chair compartment. “In our household, inviting someone to dinner means inviting them to supper afterwards as well. Tonight I am asking Little Uncle to act as host at your house. There is a porcelain jar of shark's fin in the box. It was set aside beforehand; it is not leftovers.” “Thank you. Let us say that you are inviting Mr Hu, and I shall act as hostess for you.” The Seventh Lady laughed. “As you please. You can settle between yourselves who is host and who is guest.” Luo distributed tips to the servants, and she and Hu returned in the same chairs that had brought them. At home she began bustling about as hostess, but Hu stopped her. “There is no need. You would spend all that effort, and I could not eat another thing. It would only waste your time and make me uncomfortable. Have someone brew good tea, and let us talk.” “Then come upstairs.”

Upstairs the lamps shone brightly, and an atmosphere as warm as spring surrounded them. As their eyes met, affection naturally stirred. Luo suddenly felt a constriction in her chest. She straightened, lifted her face slightly, and drew several deep breaths before feeling better. “How old are you this year?” “A little over forty.” “You look younger than forty.” She sighed softly. “Did you know how I felt about you then?” “How could I not? I thought we were not destined for each other. Yet here we are again. It seems the bond remains.” “Unfortunately, I am no longer the woman I was. An ageing woman is like a yellowed pearl, no longer worth anything.” “Not true at all. To my eyes, you are more beautiful than before. Like a persimmon: once green and hard, now red and soft.” Hu swallowed. “One can imagine how sweet it would taste.” She glanced at him and laughingly called him greedy. “Sister Luo,” he asked, “do you remember a summer when I brought you the savings-club payment, and you were alone at home…?”
