INSTALLMENT 124
Towers of Light — Chapter Five, Part Three

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

Adaptation note: sexualized passages involving a minor have been rendered as non-graphic narrative summaries in both editions. These passages are adapted rather than translated in full detail; their locations are documented in the bilingual edition.

Of course Luo remembered. She had recalled it on the evening of their reunion. It had been the thirtieth day of the seventh month, the birthday of the Bodhisattva Kshitigarbha. After setting out the incense, the family had gone to watch lotus lanterns being floated, leaving her alone to mind the house. Hu had suddenly appeared. “What brings you here?” “Your savings-club payment. Today is the last day of the month. A day's delay would carry it into next month, and credit matters. Where is everyone?” “Watching the lotus lanterns. Actually, it would have been better if you brought it tomorrow. I have to pass it straight on to someone else. In one hand and out of the other—that would be simple. Money kept overnight will not produce little coins, and a thief might come. Your kindness could turn into harm.” “I had not thought of that. If I had, I would have found some way to gather it all and bring it after lunch.” He considered. “Let me take it back, then, so you need not worry. Tell me who is to receive it, and I shall deliver it for you first thing tomorrow.” “That would be wonderful!” she said happily. “You are saving me the journey. I do not know how to thank you.” “First, give me a cup of cool tea.” “Certainly!” They had been talking in the courtyard by the light of candles set in the ground. For tea, she invited him into the main room.

She brought tea, and Hu drank it all. He then behaved inappropriately towards Luo, and she objected. Just then someone outside called loudly, “Sister Luo! Sister Luo!” She immediately moved away from him and answered, “Coming!” At the same time she signalled that Hu should step out of sight. The visitor was a neighbour with a small inquiry, whom she answered in a few words at the door and sent away. Back inside, she kept well away from Hu. When he tried to approach again, she moved to the other side of the square table. “You ought to go. That thoughtless fellow’s sudden shout frightened me nearly out of my senses. Quickly, quickly—please go.”

They returned to the memory of that incident. Luo’s expression showed sadness and loneliness. “It must have been ten years ago?” Hu asked. “Eleven,” Luo replied. “I was sixteen that year.” He tried to pursue the old subject, but she said she did not wish to discuss it, at least not yet. “When, then?” he asked.

“I don’t know,” Luo replied. Then she suddenly asked, “Do you think my abilities fit me for nothing more than an embroidery shop?” The question checked Hu's amorous thoughts. “Let us discuss this seriously. I knew how capable you were when you were still in your teens. Nobody was shrewder at arranging savings clubs or calculating interest. Frankly, if you were a man, I would ask you to manage a bank.” “Compliments cost nothing.” She smiled. “But I am not convinced by your claim that only a man can manage a bank.” “Do not misunderstand. I am not saying that you are less capable. It is inconvenient for a woman—especially one as beautiful as you. The clerks would quarrel over you, and my bank would be ruined.” “How outrageous!” Her feet were not tiny three-inch golden lilies, but the larger sort that had been bound and then released, described as having ginger for sale in front and duck eggs behind. Laughing, she became a little unsteady and reached for the table, only to have Hu catch her hand. “Never mind the clerks. I might be unable to concentrate on business myself, worrying all day that some customer would marry you and take you away.” In Hangzhou, the expression he used meant taking a wife. It touched Luo's old sorrow. “Enough!” She drew back her hand, sat aside, and said softly, “I can only blame my unhappy fate.” “I feel unhappy too,” Hu offered in sympathy. “If only I had married two years later.” She gave a faint, cold laugh. “Fine words come easily to you.” “Whether they are merely fine words, time will show. But to return to business: you say your abilities extend beyond an embroidery shop. What larger enterprise have you in mind? Tell me.”

She lowered her eyes to the tabletop, her lashes moving as though absorbed in calculation. At last she looked up. “Let us talk tomorrow. Will you come for a simple meal?” “Gladly. I shall come early in the afternoon so that we can talk at length.” “No. Come for lunch and leave early in the afternoon. Evenings are inconvenient.” Hu considered his engagements. He had two invitations for lunch the next day, one involving official business that he could not miss. The day after, he also had an engagement, but it could be declined. Luo settled on that day, and he reluctantly agreed. “I shall make a couple of Hangzhou dishes for you. Now let me act as hostess on the Seventh Lady's behalf and offer you supper.” Hu had little appetite, but the thought of not seeing her the next day made him reluctant to leave. Supper would provide an excuse to linger. He nodded, asking her not to go to any trouble. “Everything is ready. Shall we eat downstairs?” Drinking a little together upstairs would have been more agreeable, but would involve extra work and contradict his request. He rose and followed her down. “What would you like to drink?” “Anything. I am no drinker; I shall merely keep you company.” “Then join me in some medicinal wine I have infused myself.” “Ah! That reminds me…” “Wait until I have laid the table.”

Four dishes appeared: ham, crisp eel, vegetarian mock chicken, and dried croaker preserved in wine lees, all sent by the Seventh Lady. Luo also brought a white porcelain jar. The medicinal wine she poured from it had an unappealing colour but an enticing fragrance. “This looks promising. Do you have the recipe?” “Yes. It is called Duke Zhou's Hundred-Year Wine. I can copy it for you.” “The more recipes of this kind, the better. I intend to open a pharmacy and sell medicinal wines.” Surprised, she asked what had suddenly given him that idea. “Several things. One is a request for a very large quantity of prepared medicines. I thought that opening a pharmacy myself would be both convenient and a way to ensure proper quality.” Who had made the request, and why so much medicine? Zuo's soldiers on the western campaign had begun suffering from difficulty adjusting to unfamiliar climates and conditions. Letters to the Shanghai transshipment bureau requested large quantities of pills, powders, plasters, and other remedies. This had prompted Hu's plan for a substantial pharmacy. He had already obtained an official communication from the Governor-General of Shaanxi and Gansu requesting tax exemption, and had sent buyers to Anguo County in Zhili, the largest centre for crude medicinal ingredients, to purchase authentic supplies. Nothing interested him more than this plan, which he regarded as a practical means of relieving suffering and encouraging the troops. Once started, he spoke without pause. Luo listened closely and asked concise questions whenever something was unclear. Seeing that she both understood the project and cared about his work, he grew still more enthusiastic. They talked into the third watch. At last he noticed neighbours looking curiously at the brilliant lights in her house and the chair bearers talking and laughing loudly outside. Ashamed of the disturbance, he could no longer justify staying. “The day after tomorrow at noon, then. If we talk any longer, the neighbours will curse us.”

On the third morning, Hu went as usual to Fukang Bank. He was told that Weiji had withdrawn nine thousand taels in seventeen bank drafts of differing amounts. He remembered the information but asked no questions. Feeling that his previous visit to Luo had been too conspicuous, he took only one attendant this time and took a Hengsimei carriage instead of a sedan chair. Alighting at the entrance to her lane, he sent the carriage away and walked to the house. It was not yet noon, and seven or eight customers were still waiting in her reception room. Luo rose without embarrassment. “Please sit down, Mr Hu. I shall be finished very soon.” “There is no hurry. Attend to your business.” Holding a cup of tea, he watched quietly as she gave clear, brisk explanations and directions. Her customers seemed to accept her authority: what she said was done, without argument. In scarcely the time needed to drink the tea, everyone had departed. “Admirable!” Hu smiled. “You really are capable.” “We do not know that yet. Wait until the land I bought for you rises in value before praising me.” He was bewildered. “What are you talking about?” “I shall explain over lunch. Sit a little longer while I go to the kitchen.”

Most of the meal was ready. Fish head and bean curd simmered on the stove; pieces of pork were in the steamer. Brined shrimps, crucian carp braised with spring onions, and vegetarian mock chicken had been prepared earlier. She needed only to fry crisp bean-curd-skin rolls and stir-fry shepherd's purse with spring bamboo shoots. “Nothing fine to offer you. But you eat shark's fin and sea cucumber every day and must be tired of them. A few simple, refreshing home dishes might persuade you to eat another bowl of rice.” “Exactly.” Hu sat down happily. “I had little appetite before, but now I am hungry.” She smiled, poured medicinal wine, and served him. He ate with relish, much to her pleasure. “I did not understand what you said about land. Please explain.” “I withdrew nine thousand taels yesterday from the account you gave me. Did you know?” “They told me.” “When the British concession renamed its roads two years ago, I noticed how methodically the foreigners proceeded. Wherever they built a road, land prices rose. Unfortunately, I had no spare money to buy any. A couple of months ago someone approached me about a plot on Shandong Road…” “Wait. Where is Shandong Road?” “Temple Street.”

When the British concession's roads were first laid out, they received English names for the foreigners' convenience: Consulate Road where the consulates gathered, and Customs Road where the Jianghaiguan stood. During the Warring States period, Shanghai had belonged to the fief of Lord Chunshen, Huang Xie of Chu. To relieve flooding in the Songjiang region, he had opened a channel to carry water to the sea. It was called the Huangpu after his surname, or Huang Xie Pu. Other names for Shanghai—Chunshen Pu, Chunshen Jiang, and Shenjiang—came from the same association. Later generations had built a shrine to honour his achievement, also known as Lord Chunshen's temple, but its site could no longer be identified. Tradition held that Yanzhen Temple, established in the Ming near Sanmaoge Bridge to worship the Three Mao Immortals, had originally been that shrine. The British therefore named a nearby road Temple Street. Place names in the concession were confused, and the Municipal Council had long wanted a uniform system. East–west roads were to bear the names of major Chinese cities, while north–south roads would take provincial names. Consulate Road became Beijing Road. Nanjing supplied the next great city's name, given to the road extending west from the Bund park. Temple Street ran north–south and became Shandong Road. This had happened two years earlier, but Hu had paid no attention and did not recognize the new name. Temple Street he knew. “Ah! Someone offered you land there?”

“The road is now being extended southwards. Land there is certain to rise, so I withdrew nine thousand taels and bought more than twenty mu. The transaction is complete.” Hu was astonished. Buying property could remain unsettled for months or years, yet she had finished in a single day. Had someone deceived her? Reading his expression, she asked whether he disbelieved her. “Not that. It simply seems very quick. Have you any documents for the land?” “Of course. I have official lease deeds and authority certificates.” He was still more surprised. “You have even completed the minor transfer? You really are capable.” “If you do not believe me, I shall show you.”

She brought three official lease deeds. Following defeat in the Opium War and the Treaty of Nanjing in Daoguang's twenty-second year, five ports had opened to foreign trade and foreigners had begun arriving in numbers. Their settlement, however, posed a problem. All land beneath Heaven belonged to the sovereign; Chinese land could not be sold to foreigners. Some accommodation had to be devised. In Daoguang's twenty-fifth year, the British consul and the Shanghai circuit intendant therefore agreed on land regulations establishing perpetual leases. A foreigner negotiated with an owner, paid annual rent for a plot, and built upon it. In addition, the owner received a sum roughly equal to ten years' rent, called a deposit but effectively the purchase price. Once a lease was agreed, the neighbouring locality had to be notified. The local headman accompanied representatives of the circuit intendant and consulate to measure the property, establish its boundaries, and record them with a plan on the deed. The consul forwarded it to the circuit intendant for examination. If all was correct, the official sealed the lease deed and returned it to the lessee. Such a document was called a daoqi, an official lease deed. It established rights and responsibilities clearly, making disputes easier to decide than under old Chinese deeds, whose vagueness could turn a dispute into years of litigation and bitter regret. As the saying went, “However wealthy you are, never buy property that brings you grief.” People accordingly devised a nominal arrangement: a foreigner would obtain a daoqi on a Chinese owner's behalf. After agreeing terms and receiving his fee, the foreigner issued an authority certificate acknowledging that he held the property for its owner, who could dispose of it freely at any time. This was called nominee registration, and Shanghai had foreign brokers specializing in it. Before buyers and sellers concluded their transaction, they paid the broker to issue a replacement authority certificate. The original daoqi need not be changed; it passed to the buyer with equal validity. This was a minor transfer. Luo's three deeds naturally came with three authority certificates, written in English. Hu had learned a little English through years of dealing with foreigners. When he saw that the named owner was himself, he understood why she had said she had bought the land for him.

“No, no! The land is yours.” Taking the deeds and certificates, Hu said he would have another minor transfer made into her name. “There is no need. Transferring it back and forth merely gives the foreigner more fees. But you ought to show the three authority certificates to Brother-in-Law Gu. He knows English and understands the foreign settlement. If anything is wrong, we can raise it with the foreigner promptly.” “I shall. But I truly do not understand where you learned to buy land and complete every formality in a day. Even a professional might not manage it so quickly.” “Nonsense. Foreigners are straightforward about business. Once buyer and seller agree, they need only register it and sign to collect a fee. Why should they create delays? The middlemen deliberately drag matters out for a couple of days to make their errand fees seem hard-earned.” She explained her own experience. After agreeing to buy the Shandong Road land, she had engaged a specialist to handle the minor transfer for ten taels. He claimed it would take five days. Having heard of this trick, she said she would pay the same fee but employ him only as an interpreter while she dealt with the foreigner herself. The business was indeed completed in a single visit. “Here is something amusing: the foreigner even invited me to a Western meal. He said no Chinese woman had ever come to his office before. He admired my courage and wanted to entertain me.” “Did you accept?” Hu asked, smiling. “No. I said I was brave enough to ask him to do business, but not brave enough to eat his food. When the interpreter translated it, he laughed heartily.” Hu laughed too. “Never mind the foreigner: I admire you myself. Now I understand. You think an embroidery shop too small to display your abilities, do you not?”

“I would not presume to say that. But you have plenty of funds available at your bank. Why do you not buy land?” “I have never considered it.” Hu explained that his view of money differed from other people's. Money ought to flow like spring water. Buying land and waiting for its value to rise was like keeping water in a pond: only Heaven could supply the rain that raised the level. A prolonged drought would dry it up. He disdained business that depended on such chance. “That view is out of date,” Luo said, boldly criticizing him. “Elsewhere, land rises slowly and is difficult to sell. The money does become stagnant water. But in Shanghai the foreigners are building roads day and night. Once a road is complete, the fields along it can carry houses, and prices rise at once. Buyers can also resell easily. With brisk demand and quick sales, land ceases to be immovable property and becomes movable capital. How is that different from your stocking silk and cocoons?” Hu was taken aback. He had not expected such an acute insight and felt genuinely outmatched. “I must be going. Let us have rice.” She brought a shallow bowl. He poured soup over it and quickly finished, then sent his attendant to hail a street carriage at the entrance to the lane. He went to the transshipment bureau to conduct business and receive visitors. After his evening engagements, he called on Gu and his wife at midnight.

“Here is something extraordinary. Sister Luo knows how to deal in land and negotiate directly with foreigners. Look!” Examining the deeds and certificates, Gu asked in surprise whether Hu had commissioned the purchase. Hu said no and explained everything. “Sister Luo is a formidable woman,” the Seventh Lady remarked. “Little Uncle…” She stopped and did not finish. Hu partly understood but did not pursue it. He merely discussed transferring the three plots into Luo's name. “I know this foreign registration broker,” Gu said. “He sometimes plays tricks. We had better spend fifty taels and make a full transfer.” Without asking what a full transfer involved, Hu left the arrangements to him. “What is Sister Luo's name?” Gu asked. “There you have me.” “Sister Luo is Sister Luo,” his wife said. “Surname Luo, given name Sijie. What is wrong with that?” Hu laughed. “Seventh Sister settles a matter with a single stroke. She has judgment.” Gu laughed too, though rather ruefully, and rose with an air of casualness. “I shall write her name in English.”

When Gu had gone into his study, Hu moved closer to the Seventh Lady and lowered his voice. “There is something I want to discuss. Since the two younger women in my household died in an epidemic, and with my wife's health poor, Mother still has to trouble herself with domestic affairs at times. It is hardly right. I am very fond of Sister Luo. I wonder… can anything be arranged?” “I have already considered it. If she would marry you, Little Uncle, you would be a tiger given wings. You would prosper still further. But whether she will accept the position of a concubine is difficult to say.” “Could you sound her out?” “More than that: we must find a way. What about two wives of equal standing?” “That would mean two separate households, leaving Mother with the work as before. If she will accept the lesser formal status, I shall treat her as a principal wife in every other respect.” “Very well. I understand. I shall persuade her. Your wife is kind and sensible too; she certainly would not mistreat her.” “Then…” “Enough, Little Uncle! You need give me no more instructions. I am more impatient than you. I should like to drink the wedding wine tomorrow.”

True to her word, the Seventh Lady visited Luo the next morning, helped her finish with the customers, and ate upstairs with her. During their casual conversation, Luo could not hide her admiration and excitement whenever Hu was mentioned. Seeing a real prospect of success, the Seventh Lady formed a plan. “I know you were born in the year of the Snake. What month?” “The month?” Luo suddenly laughed. “My childhood name was A'he…” “Then you were born in the sixth month, the lotus month.” Noticing an odd smile and an unfinished thought, she asked about the name. “When I was small, the boys teased me by pretending to be man-eating tigers, roaring ‘A-ho! A-ho!’ They said I would grow into a tigress. I refused to answer to that name. If someone wanted my help—another participant for a savings club, for example—calling me A'he ensured they would not get it. That is how everyone came to call me Sister Luo.” The Seventh Lady laughed. “There is a story behind it, then. A tigress is not such a bad nickname. At least, if people know your husband fears you, they will not dare bully you.” “I am not like that. Why should a husband be afraid? Never mind the past. Now there is even less reason to speak of it.” “Not necessarily. Someone will certainly fear you yet.” Luo hesitated, then asked with a slight blush whom she meant. The Seventh Lady nodded thoughtfully. “There will be someone. An ordinary man is not worthy of you. Even if he married you, his fear would be useless.” “Useless?” “He would fear you because you are capable. Anyone can see that you have the fortune to help a husband succeed. But he must have good material in him too. A weak fellow without ambition or ability might receive an excellent idea from you and be too lazy to act, or incapable of carrying it out. Feeling indebted, he would merely fear you. What use is that?” Luo listened carefully and considered. “You are right. There is a reason some men are afraid of their wives.”

“Exactly.” The Seventh Lady returned to her purpose. “Fortune comes from destiny. To help a husband prosper, you must marry a man with a good destiny, and have a good one yourself. Fortune without destiny is like a rootless tree. Nothing will come of it.” “Destiny is unreliable too. When I was little, everyone who read mine said it was excellent. Look at me now. Where is the excellence?” “What did they say?” “I did not understand much of it. Only that a jiazi day and a jiazi hour made an unusually rich and honourable destiny.” “Perhaps the wealth and honour lie ahead.” “What future is there? With a son, there might be hope. Like the White Lady: after a lifetime of suffering, her son became the top examination graduate and she could hold her head high at last. What have I?” “Could you not marry again and have one? There is a Wu Tiekou on Second Road whose predictions everyone calls extraordinarily accurate. I shall take you to see him sometime.” Had the Seventh Lady consulted him herself? She said she had, and that his prediction had certainly proved true. He had foretold a bijie period in the first half of this year. Asked what that meant, she explained that it was a period of friendship and brotherhood. Had they not become as close as sisters at their first meeting? Luo was persuaded and asked when they could go. “His business is tremendously busy. You must book in advance, whether for a full reading or the year's fortunes. I shall send someone to register and let you know the appointment.”

Back at home, the Seventh Lady immediately asked Gu whether he knew Wu Tiekou well. “We are companions at courtesan banquets. Why do you ask?” “I have a set of birth characters…” “Enough! It is all a charlatan's formula, saying whatever suits the listener. Believe him and you invite trouble.” “That is precisely what I want him to do. Let him examine these birth characters first, and then say what I instruct.” “What you instruct? Whose birth characters?” “Sister Luo's. Snake year, the fifteenth of the sixth month, a jiazi day and a jiazi hour.” Gu began to understand. “Very well. What do you want him to say?” “Before that, I have two questions. Will he follow my instructions? And can he make it sound convincing?” “He will certainly follow them—if you pay a larger fee.” “That is a small matter. I am afraid he may fail to make it convincing, or even reveal the trick. That would ruin my plan.” “He is devilishly clever and will not give himself away. Whether his account withstands scrutiny depends on whether the other person knows the subject.” “Why?” “An expert can find faults and expose gaps. His profession has a saying: submit your reading to challenge, and you put your life in another's hands.” “Tell him not to worry. His life will certainly be safe.”
