Towers of Light — Chapter Six, Part One

CHAPTER SIX: A WINDING ROAD TO MARRIAGE

Ten days later, Luo Sijie received a letter from home. As she had requested, her mother had asked Mr Wu to write it. Mr Wu was the keeper of the Temple of Lord Guan, a warm-hearted man with a thorough understanding of the world. Reading Luo’s letter first, he had wondered why she had specifically requested him as the writer of the reply. Then, listening to Auntie Luo’s delighted account of Hu’s visit, he understood. Her daughter probably had not been certain whether Hu would call in person, which explained why she had omitted the name of the man carrying her letter and gifts. Yet his actions mattered greatly to her. Mr Wu must therefore tell her everything in detail: that was precisely why she had asked him to write. He had guessed her intention perfectly, and the letter fully met her expectations. His language was plain and clear. She first had Old Ma read it aloud, then worked carefully through it herself. Once she could understand most of it, she put it inside her clothes and went to see the Seventh Lady. “Seventh Sister, I have a letter. Would you look at it for me?” “From whom?” “My mother. It is very long, and Mr Hu seems to be mentioned in the middle. I am afraid there may be something important that I ought not to ask Old Ma to read.” “I am not much better than you. If you cannot make it out, I may not be able to either. But never mind. One guest need not trouble two hosts. You asked Yingchun to write before; let him read this one too.” “Is Brother-in-Law Gu at home?” “Yes. A foreigner is coming to see him, and he is waiting.”

Gu was summoned and given the letter. He read and explained as he went. “Everything arrived. Mr Hu also sent a very substantial gift, eight things in all: ham, tea, Huadiao…” “You need not read those out,” the Seventh Lady interrupted. “Does the letter call Little Uncle Mr Hu?” “Yes. That is how Hangzhou people who respect him address him.” “Go on, then.” “On the seventh day of the fifth month, Mr Hu called on your mother. He was extremely courteous and stayed two full hours, talking about your recent life in Shanghai…” Gu smiled and stopped. “What is amusing?” his wife asked in surprise. “Your mother says she was pleased to learn that you had met the two of us. She calls it ‘the happy encounter with benefactors.’ That is far more than we deserve.” “My mother is right. You are certainly my benefactors.” Luo asked whether the letter also mentioned her daughter. “It does. Your mother says Mr Hu liked her very much, asked all sorts of questions, and talked with her at length. He also gave her a present at their first meeting: a pair of twisted-wire gold bracelets.” “Just think, Seventh Sister! Such costly things for a child. But I do not quite understand. Why did he have those bracelets with him? Did he know before going that I had a daughter?” “Not necessarily. Little Uncle has so many social obligations that he keeps gold watches and all sorts of other things. When a present is needed, he can simply produce one.” “So that is the explanation.” Her doubt resolved, Luo asked Gu to continue.

“Your mother says you mentioned coming home, and she misses you very much. If you cannot spare the time, perhaps she might come to Shanghai instead.” Before Luo could answer, the Seventh Lady exclaimed, “Let her come! Bring her here for the summer. She can stay two or three months before going back.” Luo nodded. “Shanghai is cooler than Hangzhou. Let me think about it.” “There is another passage, a postscript from Mr Wu. Quite interesting.” Gu smiled. “He says that since Mr Hu’s personal visit, the tea drinkers at the temple have talked of little else for days. Everyone calls him a generous and loyal man. Mr Wu thinks he might even be the benefactor destined to enter your life.” The words touched the deepest part of Luo’s heart. Composed as she was, she could not help blushing. With great tact, the Seventh Lady deliberately changed the subject. “What tea drinkers? What temple?” “The Temple of Lord Guan, near our house. Mr Wu, who wrote this letter for my mother, is its keeper. He sets out tables and sells tea, with storytellers performing. That is how the temple’s worship and incense are supported.” At this point Gu’s foreign visitor arrived. While he received him, the two women continued talking. Luo would very much like her mother to stay, but had no suitable person to escort her. The Seventh Lady thought that no difficulty at all: she need only write to Hu. “No, that would not do.” Luo kept shaking her head, and only when pressed explained, “I owe him too much already.” “Then what harm in owing him one kindness more?” “I am afraid I could never repay him.” “There is a way of doing that too…” On reflection, the Seventh Lady decided not to speak too plainly. Luo asked no further question, and the matter was left for the time being.

They chatted until the lamps were lit. The Seventh Lady proposed an early dinner followed by a visit to a Western circus. Luo agreed to dine but declined the circus: it would finish late, and she would feel guilty at making her hostess escort her so far home. “That is easily settled. Stay here. We can talk some more as well.” After considering it, Luo accepted. When they returned from the circus after dinner, Gu had just arrived home too. “A’qi, please pack my things. Today’s visitor is the new manager of a German trading house. He wants to make a special journey to Hangzhou to see Little Uncle and take the opportunity to visit West Lake. I have no choice but to accompany him.” “You are going to Hangzhou? Wonderful! Bring Sister Luo’s mother back with you.” Gu paused, then remembered the letter and understood. “Gladly. I shall certainly arrange it.” With husband and wife having decided between them, Luo could do nothing but thank them. They discussed the journey, which Gu reckoned would take about half a month there and back. His wife had another idea. “Why not move into the British settlement? It would be easier for us to visit each other. Half a month is quite enough to find a house and move.” “Let me think.” “You need not. Let me think for you.” She considered whose house might conveniently be rented or bought, and an idea came. “Wasn’t Old Mi building a row of houses in a lane? Are they finished?” “Long ago.” “There are twenty-four houses in that lane—a long one,” Gu continued. “I am sure some must be available.” “Then that is settled.” Turning to Luo, the Seventh Lady explained, “Old Mi is Fukang’s deputy manager. He has made a fortune too. If the houses are his, one word is all it will take for you to move in.”

“There, you see!” Luo hastily rejected the suggestion. “I think it would be better to find somewhere else.” “Why?” She would only shake her head. At last the Seventh Lady understood. At this delicate stage in Luo’s relations with Hu, she wished to avoid suspicion and leave no obvious sign. The Seventh Lady found her shrewd, capable, and loyal to her friends, yet also somewhat affected. A frank woman herself, she had a way of cutting through complications that Luo could not manage. “I don’t care what twists and turns that wonderfully clever mind of yours is taking. To sum it up, and sum it up again: you are moving. Whether we rent, acquire the house under a redeemable pledge, or buy it, I shall decide. You need not concern yourself.” Luo yielded. “Then I shall do as you say, Seventh Sister. I leave everything to you.” Taking full charge, the Seventh Lady bought her one of the new houses built by Old Mi, Fukang’s deputy manager. The lane was called Fuhou Lane. Its twenty-four houses faced one another across a passage open at both ends. She chose one facing south. Downstairs were side rooms to east and west, a large reception room, and, at the back, a kitchen, servants’ quarters, and storeroom. The stairs were in the middle. Upstairs were five rooms of different sizes. The largest was a suite running the full depth of the house from south to north, ample accommodation for a beauty in seclusion. The other four were a sitting room, a dining room, and two guest rooms. The Seventh Lady personally selected all the furniture and ornaments, arranging them with an attractive richness and splendor. Everything was ready in only ten days, and she was thoroughly pleased with herself. On the eleventh morning she sent the carriage for Luo. “I have arranged your house. Come and see.” Luo was astonished and delighted. “It is wonderful! I only fear I lack the good fortune to live in such a house.” Ignoring this, the Seventh Lady asked what she disliked; anything could be changed at once. Luo frankly mentioned that curtains were still needed.

“The windows have been measured, and the curtains are being made. They will be ready tomorrow. When will you move?” “Wait.” Luo drew her down to sit beside her and hesitated. “To tell the truth, I love the house. But I am afraid it is beyond my means. House and furniture together must cost four thousand taels?” “Less than four thousand. I have a detailed account. You need not worry about affording it now. For the moment, regard it as my property, lent to you to live in free of charge. When you can afford it, I shall let you have it at the original price.” “Can such good things happen in this world?” “You don’t believe it? I hardly believe it myself!” The Seventh Lady laughed. “It looks as though Wu Tiekou’s words are coming true.” Luo remembered them perfectly. He had insisted she must become a concubine. If she insisted on being a principal wife, she would bring death to one husband after another. If she would neither accept the lesser position nor occupy the higher one, disaster would overtake her own life. In that case, the Wealth, Officer, and Seal represented in the first four of her birth characters would count for nothing. Only by willingly becoming a concubine could she enjoy good fortune. The Seventh Lady’s meaning was therefore plain. Yet Luo would neither acknowledge nor reject it. Her only course was to pretend she had not quite heard, ignore the implication, and deliberately change the subject.

“Moving is a troublesome business, Seventh Sister. I am afraid…” “There is no need to worry about every possibility. The furniture and fittings are all here. Give away what you can of your old furniture, and have a secondhand dealer take the rest in one lot. Packing clothes, jewelry, and household utensils cannot take more than a day. Where is the trouble?” “What about all my customers?” “That is more difficult.” After a pause, the Seventh Lady said, “I advise you to give up the business…” “No!” Luo interrupted. “It is not only for my own sake. This is a livelihood by which other people support their families. I cannot simply abandon them.” “That is easily managed too. Find someone capable to take your place. You might even receive a payment for handing over the business. The change between old and new management is bound to leave loose ends, so Old Ma can come to the new house later. Or he might stay with the new proprietor, which would save you still more trouble.” Her arrangements were so simple and decisive, yet so considerate of the details, that Luo could find nothing more to object to. “I truly admire you, Seventh Sister. There is only one thing left now: choosing a day.” “Exactly. Let us go back to my house, choose a day, and discuss everything further.”

After a short rest at the Gu house, the Seventh Lady had the almanac brought. Unluckily, none of the next eight or nine days was suitable for moving. The earliest favorable date was ten days away. “Your mother will be here by then. I think it would be best to have everything settled within three or four days, so that she can go straight into the new house when she arrives. It would please her and save a great deal of trouble. What do you say?” “Of course that would be best. But if the days are unfavorable, there is nothing we can do.” The Seventh Lady thought for a moment. “Yes, there is. As the saying goes, better to take the day you happen upon than labor over choosing one. Whatever day comes our way will be the day. How about that?” “How are we to happen upon it?” “Take the day your mother reaches Shanghai. She can rest briefly at my house and then go straight to yours. Move your essential things beforehand. In the evening, give a dinner for two tables and engage a troupe of mao’er opera performers to make it lively. It will welcome your mother at the same time. Two purposes served by one arrangement.” Luo approved. “When will Brother-in-Law Gu be back?” “Soon. Probably in another four or five days.”

Gu returned, but to Luo’s astonishment, her mother had not come. Mr Wu had arrived instead. He was in his fifties, short and stout, with a head of white hair and a red, bulbous nose. His features were odd, but his eyes were excellent. Their penetrating brightness held anyone he looked at and naturally inspired both affection and trust. The Seventh Lady took to him at once. After Gu’s introductions and the customary courtesies, she asked why Luo’s mother had not come. Mr Wu used the question to explain his visit. “The weather is very hot, and her mother is observing a vegetarian fast for Guanyin. A visit to Shanghai would be inconvenient in several ways. But she has some important things she wants me to tell her daughter privately. Thanks to Mr Gu, I have been brought along to broaden my experience of Shanghai.” “Excellent. Sister Luo and I became close at once, like true sisters. Since you are someone she respects, please stand on no ceremony here. Shall we send for her, or would you prefer to call on her?” “Her mother has sent a few things to eat and use. I had better take them to her.” “Then I shall take you.” He protested repeatedly that he could not accept such an honor. “There is no need for formality. I have two reasons for taking you myself.” She turned to her husband. “You probably haven’t heard that Sister Luo has moved. Into Old Mi’s house. I arranged everything.” “So quickly!” Gu turned to Mr Wu. “I don’t even know where her new home is. My wife really must take you.” “And while I am there, I can invite her to accompany you to dinner tonight, when we welcome you properly.” Mr Wu could only keep thanking her. They took sedan chairs instead of the carriage and went to Luo’s house. After delivering him and arranging for them both to come to dinner, the Seventh Lady hurried home, eager to hear Gu’s account of the journey.

“He is the bride’s matchmaker…” Soon after returning to Hangzhou, Hu had found time to discuss Luo with his mother and wife. Ordinarily, he could decide for himself to take a concubine, but Luo’s circumstances were different. Several consequential matters had to be settled beforehand, above all her title and her actual authority. In Hangzhou official households, a concubine was first called the New Miss. After six months or a year, when relatives and friends knew her, her surname would be used: Miss Luo, for instance. Several years might pass before she became yinainai, or Auntie. The more dignified title yitaitai, or Aunt-Madam, generally came only in middle age, after her children had grown. Hu, however, requested that Luo be addressed as Madam from the moment she entered the household. “Then what of your principal wife?” Old Madam Hu asked. “One is Madam and the other is Madam. How is anyone to know which is meant?” “Call one of them Second Madam.” His mother considered this. “What does she say?” She pointed vaguely in his wife’s direction. “I haven’t discussed that part with her yet. I wanted your approval before speaking to her.” Old Madam Hu knew her daughter-in-law was kind and weak. Even if reluctant, she would not openly oppose him. As head of the family, however, she herself had to consider its rules and could not agree lightly. “I must think carefully. Whatever we do must be defensible before others.” “It is defensible.” This gave Hu his opening to discuss authority. “With the establishment we now maintain, someone must take overall charge indoors. Your daughter-in-law is too unassertive, and her health is poor. You still have to attend to so much, which makes me feel a failure as a son. To be frank, you don’t fully understand affairs outside the house. Sometimes, even when you want to help, you cannot see how. I have thought it over endlessly. The only answer is to marry Sister Luo and put her in charge. If she manages the household, she must have the standing to do so. This is a special arrangement to meet the circumstances. It can be justified.”

“If you want her to manage the household, I must consider it still more carefully. You know how hard it is to be the person in charge.” “I do. She is exceptionally capable. She can certainly manage it.” “Ability is not everything. As the saying goes, ‘Unless you can play the fool and turn a deaf ear, you cannot be the elder of a household.’ The person in charge must bear injuries without complaint. Maids, older women servants, cooks, and chair bearers will all gossip behind her back. Has she the generosity to hear an insult aimed plainly at her—like cursing bald-headed scoundrels in front of a monk—and pretend she heard nothing, without the slightest displeasure showing on her face?” “As for that… I shall explain it clearly. She is sure to agree.” His mother shook her head emphatically. “Words and promises are one thing; the moment itself is another. Even a clay bodhisattva has a temper. If she loses patience and makes a scene, disrupting the household, you will regret it when it is too late.” Their perspectives differed. Old Madam Hu had met Luo years before, but could no longer remember even her appearance and could judge only by ordinary human behavior. Hu realized that he could not persuade his mother all at once. He must tell her more about Luo’s character and abilities. Once confidence grew, agreement would follow naturally.

It was at this point that Gu arrived in Hangzhou with the foreign visitor. After their business was settled, someone else was sent to accompany the foreigner around the Six Bridges and Three Tianzhu Temples. Gu could then speak fully with Hu about Luo’s requests and Mr Wu’s letter. He thought the time was ripe to discuss marriage. “Mother still has doubts. She is afraid Sister Luo can bear hard work but not unfair criticism.” “What do you think, Little Uncle?” “That depends first on how we treat her. If she refuses, there is nothing more to say. If she agrees, she must understand that I cannot divorce my principal wife to make her First Madam. She can only enter as a concubine. Yet if she has the title of Madam and authority over the household, surely reflecting on that will make it easier to swallow unpleasant gossip and remain at peace.” “You have put it very clearly. Let me speak to your mother.”

Gu had his own approach. He did not mention Hu at all, speaking instead of how well his wife and Luo suited each other, and how Luo knew when she was being treated well. Because the Seventh Lady had been kind to her, Luo listened to everything she said; the two were like sisters. Old Madam Hu held the Gus, her friends through adversity, in great respect, and felt particular affection and confidence toward the Seventh Lady. “Anyone Seventh Sister approves of must be sound. She must arrange this match. I must hear what she has to say before deciding.” Matters had suddenly taken a favorable turn, and success seemed near. But Gu was thoughtful and cautious, and saw little reason to encourage excessive optimism. “Please do not be too pleased yet. Whether the other party will agree is still unknown.” He told her how his wife had taken Luo to have her fortune read and how Luo had almost quarreled with Wu Tiekou. Naturally, he concealed their advance arrangement with the fortune-teller. Old Madam Hu listened closely, smiling more and more. “So she has such excellent birth characters. It really does seem destined. People of that temperament either refuse outright or, once they consent, keep every word they give.” Gu thought of another point. “Little Uncle, what does your wife think?” “She agrees,” Old Madam Hu answered. “I have spoken to her. She leaves the decision to me. Now, Brother-in-Law Gu, I entrust the matter to you.”

“If you give your approval and your daughter-in-law will not resent us, A’qi and I will do everything we can to bring it to a satisfactory conclusion.” Gu proposed that the Seventh Lady act as matchmaker for Hu, while Mr Wu might represent the bride. Hu agreed and sent Mr Wu a dinner invitation. Naturally overwhelmed by the honor, the temple keeper arrived punctually. His host introduced Gu and the other guests, drank a cup with him, and then excused himself from the table. Conversation over dinner remained general; the real business was not mentioned. Only afterward, over tea, did Gu draw Mr Wu aside. “We have been acquaintances in spirit for a long time.” Mr Wu was puzzled until Gu explained their respective roles in writing letters for Luo’s family. Though they had never met, each already knew the other’s handwriting. That was their acquaintance at a distance, and it made everything easy to discuss.

“Then the matter is settled!” the Seventh Lady said happily. “Mr Wu seems very concerned about Sister Luo. I wonder what he thinks of her new house.” Once she had left him there, Mr Wu had examined the house and its furnishings. Everything seemed new and exquisite. He had not imagined that Luo was so wealthy. With a woman he had only just met present, he had concealed his reaction for fear of appearing ignorant of the world. Now restraint was unnecessary, and his admiration and amazement showed without disguise. “I never expected a young woman like you to establish herself so splendidly. I have visited Shanghai twice before, but this is the first house of such beauty I have seen. The Gus are plainly people of standing. Their house is larger, but not as new. They have more furnishings, but fine pieces are mixed with ordinary ones, good with bad. Yours are so consistent throughout.” His praise gave Luo an indescribable pleasure. Few satisfactions could equal being seen by someone who had known one from childhood as a person of ambition and achievement, risen far enough to deserve new respect. She felt almost as though she had returned home in glory. Yet an emptiness followed: the glory was incomplete. “Neither house nor furniture is mine. How could I have risen far enough to maintain such a display?”

Mr Wu was not entirely surprised. “Even if Mr Hu bought them for you, once you use them, they are yours.” “It wasn’t he. They belong to the Seventh Lady.” “The Seventh Lady? Where did such a married aunt suddenly appear in your Luo family?” Luo laughed. “How have you got so confused? I mean Mrs Gu. Her maiden name is You, and she is seventh in her family. Everyone calls her the Seventh Lady; I call her Seventh Sister.” “Ah, so it is she.” The more he thought, the more confused he became. “Then why do the Gus call Mr Hu Little Uncle? Shanghai people use that word for an uncle. How can he be their young uncle?” Luo explained what she herself had learned only days before. The Seventh Lady’s brother, You Laowu, led the Songjiang Grain Transport Brotherhood. His master was Hu’s close friend. The Green Gang placed great weight on its rules of seniority, so although You was older than Hu, he honored him as an elder. His sister and Gu followed his usage. “Seventh Sister says the Songjiang group is called an exhausted brotherhood. It is large and has many grain boats, but little substance behind the name. Leading it is difficult, and Mr Hu has helped them greatly. That is why he has so much influence along the water route between Hangzhou and Shanghai.”

Mr Wu listened closely while considering his own position. He had agreed to act as matchmaker, but this was not a marriage to a principal wife through the full formal rites, and there was little distinction in arranging it. On the journey he had resolved that if Luo was not particularly willing, or Hu’s conduct outside Hangzhou had earned him an unfavorable reputation, he would have to withdraw. What he now saw made those earlier reservations seem unrealistic. He might as well turn to business. “Sister Luo, do you know why I have come?” The question embarrassed her. But this concerned the rest of her life; she must not let reluctance to speak create a misunderstanding. She answered steadily, “Has my mother asked you to tell me something?” “Yes. I thought that if she could not come herself, a letter would do just as well. But she disagreed, with reason. A letter to you and a reply would take a month or so between them. Better for me to come and ask you directly.” “What does she wish to ask?” “How you feel about Mr Hu.” Luo blushed. “Feel about him in what way?” She covered her shyness with a complaint. “You are being so vague. How can I answer?” Serving tea at the temple brought Mr Wu into contact with every sort of person, and his experience was considerable. Matchmaking, however, was new to him. People always spoke of a matchmaker’s formidable tongue: one sentence could make or ruin a match. He had never understood quite how a word could save a kingdom or destroy it, but now the meaning came naturally. If he simply said, “Mr Hu wants to take you as a concubine,” she would surely be angry and ashamed, refuse in indignation, and destroy the prospect. Had he thought poorly of Hu, that was what he would have said. Since he had now decided to join the pair, he chose the most pleasing words. “Mr Hu would like you to take charge of his household.”

Her heart leapt, but she hardly dared believe him. “Can that be true? Everyone calls him the God of Wealth. With such a grand establishment, how could I manage his household?” “There is no need for modesty. Your ability was evident from childhood. Mr Hu has always been an excellent judge of people. If he asks you to manage his house, he is certain you can bear the responsibility.” It did not sound like an idle joke. “Really?” “Of course. Without that undertaking, I would never have come. You would already be making a sacrifice in formal status. Without anything to compensate for it, do you think I would agree to arrange the match?” His wording was skillful. In place of the offensive word “concubine,” he spoke of a sacrifice in formal status, and Luo accepted the idea inwardly almost without noticing. “What do you think, then?” he urged. “If you have nothing further to say, I shall speak to Mrs Gu this evening. As your side’s matchmaker, I shall certainly press your interests.” “Why speak to the Seventh Lady? Is she…” “The matchmaker for his side.” “What does my mother think?” “She favors the marriage.” Luo’s feelings surged as she considered it from every angle. Some things were difficult to say even to Mr Wu. After a long pause, she replied, “You know that the Seventh Lady and I are like sisters. I think I had better ask her myself.”

“If I need only arrange a match you have settled yourselves, nothing could be better. But your mother has entrusted me with this, Sister Luo. When you speak to Mrs Gu, do not forget to leave your mother something to fall back on.” Luo did not understand. “What did my mother say to you?” Mr Wu regretted mentioning a safeguard. With the position Luo would occupy in the Hu household, her mother would not become a recognized fellow mother-in-law or visit on the footing of an ordinary relative. A fund should therefore be provided for her old age: that was the safeguard he meant. But in the present circumstances, surely not only Luo but Hu himself would think of it. The remark had been unnecessary. Unwilling to state his full meaning, he merely answered, “Your mother said nothing. It was my own thought. One raises children against old age and stores grain against hunger. You must make provision for her.” “So that is what you mean!” Luo answered readily. “Naturally, I have plans for that.” “Then I am reassured. When we reach the Gu house, speak to Mrs Gu yourself.”

Gu gave some thought to the dinner welcoming Mr Wu. As a new guest meeting his wife for the first time, he should, by ordinary custom, dine separately from the women. But there were only four of them. Dividing the company would create distance and make conversation awkward. Gu therefore decided on a Western dinner. Mr Wu had never eaten one in a private house. Fortunately, his host had considered the difficulties. There were many stories of mishaps at Western meals. Cutting one’s tongue with a knife was an exaggeration, but drinking the finger-bowl water as though it were plain drinking water was no joke; clattering knives and forks were commonplace. Gu provided chopsticks as well as Western cutlery. He avoided underdone beef, had every meat dish boned, and used few Western seasonings. The drinks, however, were foreign. Instead of distinguishing between those served before or after food, he set out several bottles—yellow, white, and red—for Mr Wu to choose. As they sat down, the Seventh Lady said frankly, “We are among friends. If you are not used to knives and forks, use chopsticks.” “Very well. Obedience is better than ceremony. I shall be frank too.” He happily picked up his chopsticks. “Have you seen Sister Luo’s new house?” The Seventh Lady softened the last part of the Chinese word, making it sound like “bridal chamber.” She was joking, but Mr Wu misunderstood. He thought Luo would remain in Shanghai and her present home would become the couple’s shared residence. How, then, could she manage Hu’s household? He had no time to examine the doubt, for he must answer. “It is excellent. Sister Luo tells me you arranged it all, Mrs Gu.” Before he could finish, Luo said, “Please call her Seventh Sister, or the Seventh Lady.” “Very well. Seventh Lady, you are a heroine among women!” “How did you think of that?” Luo laughed. “Even a compliment must fit. Seventh Sister isn’t some adventurer’s wife. Why call her a heroine?” Mr Wu himself felt the comparison was misplaced. “Before I came, the storytellers were performing the tale of Yue Fei. They had reached Liang Hongyu beating the war drums to defeat the Jin. I heard ‘heroine among women’ so often that it slipped out.”

“If you enjoy storytelling, I shall take you tomorrow.” Gu, a devotee himself, spoke eagerly. “Two performances at the City God Temple, one of Tales of Heroes and the other of Water Margin, are both celebrated. You must not miss the chance.” “They speak Suzhou dialect,” Luo pointed out. “Mr Wu may not understand.” “I understand, I understand.” In stiff Suzhou speech, he added, “Yinli, baizuo.” Everyone laughed. “He not only understands,” Gu said. “He knows the business.” The expressions were puns on Yinglie, Tales of Heroes, and Baishe, White Snake. Story-house patrons used them to mock freeloaders. Some stood furtively in a corner and heard an entire episode without paying a copper: “shadow standing,” yinli. Others sat quite openly at the back, then slipped away as soon as the attendant came to collect payment: “free sitting,” baizuo. Discovering their shared enthusiasm, Gu and Mr Wu felt the pleasure of meeting a kindred spirit and talked at length about storytelling and its different schools. Luo watched them, then said quietly to the Seventh Lady, “Mr Wu will be drinking until midnight. Let us leave the table.”
