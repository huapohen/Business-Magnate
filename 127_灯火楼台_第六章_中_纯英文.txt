INSTALLMENT 127
Towers of Light — Chapter Six, Part Two

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

The Seventh Lady had been thinking the same thing. Finding a pause, she interrupted the men's flow of talk with a few of the gracious remarks expected of a hostess, and the two women left the table. She took Luo Sijie upstairs to her bedroom, which Luo had always admired. Gu Yingchun had redesigned it in the Western style, with a spacious balcony and large glass doors hung with two layers of curtains, one gauze and the other velvet. When the velvet curtains were drawn back by day, sunlight passed through the gauze and filled the room, making it bright, splendid, and wonderfully refreshing. At night, the sight of countless lights from the balcony had a charm of its own. In summer, especially, nothing could be more comfortable than sitting there in the cool air and talking. “Will you have Chinese tea or foreign tea?” Foreign tea meant English tea with milk. The Seventh Lady had a complete silver tea service and followed the English custom of preparing the tea herself and pouring it for her guests, a rather elaborate business. Luo, intent on discussing serious matters, had no mind to appreciate it. “I'd like a cup of chrysanthemum tea.” The yellow and white Hangzhou chrysanthemums could be brewed like tea to calm the mind and dispel internal heat. “Is your mind rather unsettled?” the Seventh Lady asked with a smile. “I don't know why, but I've been feeling restless all this time.” “Let's sit out on the balcony.” She chose the balcony out of consideration for Luo. Discussing her own marriage was bound to make her shy; in the dim light she could conceal her embarrassment and speak more freely.

When the maid had brought their chrysanthemum tea, Luo, sitting with her face turned away from the light, gave a soft sigh. “Seventh Sister, perhaps it really is my destiny.” “Oh? The Hu family has asked Mr Wu to act as matchmaker. What did he say?” “I don't know whether to believe him. He said Mr Hu wanted me to run his household.” “That's true. Yingchun heard him say it too.” “Then it seems he really means it!” Luo felt more assured, yet her misgivings grew stronger. “Tell me, Seventh Sister, what standing would I have to run his household?” This, the Seventh Lady reflected, was precisely what troubled Hu Xueyan and what Luo meant to fight for. They were both formidable people: once either of them spoke, the words went straight to the heart of the matter. Although Gu had already told her exactly how things stood, she was unwilling to commit herself too readily. She did not know what further conditions Luo might put forward, so caution was necessary. “What do you think yourself?” she asked tentatively. “What standing would you need to manage the household?” “It is the housekeeper's position that matters. If your rank is low, the servants look down on you. Everything you say goes in one ear and out the other. Tell me, how could I run a household like that?” “Yes, that's a very practical consideration. I don't suppose Little Uncle fails to understand it. He will find some way to make the servants respect you.”

“What way?” Luo asked at once. “What did Brother-in-Law Gu say?” “He said Old Madam Hu had asked me to act as matchmaker. But I haven't dared agree yet.” Luo was both startled and delighted. “So Old Madam Hu herself is behind this? What about Mrs Hu?” “The old lady decides everything in their household. Mrs Hu is as virtuous as can be. Whatever the old lady says, she accepts; she obeys her in everything.” Luo felt easier on hearing this. But why had the Seventh Lady not dared accept the commission? She was too embarrassed to ask. “Why haven't I dared agree?” the Seventh Lady went on, answering her own question. “Though we felt like old friends from the moment we met, as close as real sisters, this is still your marriage, a matter for your whole life. You haven't talked it over with me in detail, so I don't know exactly what you want. If I rashly agreed and then failed to arrange the match, it might end by hurting our friendship.” “Seventh Sister, you can put that fear aside. Whatever happens, it will never hurt the friendship between us.” “That gives me courage. Sister Luo, apart from your formal status, what else is there? Tell me everything, one thing at a time. Then we can see what I can agree to on their behalf, what I can try to obtain for you, and what cannot possibly be done.”

“I wouldn't ask for anything impossible.” Luo thought for a moment. “My mother is my greatest difficulty.” Why should her mother present a difficulty? After a little thought, the Seventh Lady understood: Luo must mean whether the two families would acknowledge each other as relatives by marriage. In her view, whether this became a problem depended chiefly on Luo's own attitude. If she insisted that Old Madam Hu address her mother as a fellow mother-in-law, that would indeed be difficult. Otherwise, the Hu family could easily manage it. At this point, they had to speak plainly. “I understand what you mean. What else is there? Tell me everything, and we'll discuss the points one by one.” “Then there's my daughter, as you know.” “Your daughter naturally bears her father's surname,” said the Seventh Lady. “Surely you wouldn't want to take her into the Hu household?” “Of course not. What sort of arrangement would that be?” “Since you won't be bringing her into their household, she is your own concern. Whatever arrangements you make, the Hu family would have no business interfering. We needn't discuss that. What else?”

“One more thing: I can kowtow to the old lady, but to no one else.” “There!” the Seventh Lady replied immediately. “That is just why I was afraid to agree. I feared you might say something like this, a condition I couldn't even bring myself to put to them.” Luo herself felt she had asked a little too much. Having said it, however, she could hardly withdraw it of her own accord, so she remained silent. To the Seventh Lady, this naturally meant that she was no longer insisting and could be persuaded. “Now let me tell you what the family proposes. First, the form of address: all the servants will call you Madam. Second, you will make the required kowtow when you first enter the household; after that, all greetings will be exchanged as between equals. Third, once you have borne a son, you may wear a red skirt. These three instructions come from the old lady herself.” Luo considered them for a while. She felt that, on these three points, no amount of argument would gain her anything further. Better to concede gracefully and obtain concessions elsewhere. “If that is what you say, Seventh Sister, I'll accept your advice. But how exactly am I to enter their household?”

The Seventh Lady thought that Luo knew the answer perfectly well. A concubine was carried into the house in an ordinary sedan chair and, before the blazing red candles, made the kowtows that established her rank and the proper forms of address. Was Luo asking because she wanted to arrive in a decorated bridal chair? Certainly the ordinary procedure would be too humiliating for her, but there could be no question of the bridal chair either. Here, the Seventh Lady felt, was a real difficulty. After much thought, she could only say, “I'll try to find a way. Whatever we do, it must preserve your dignity. Ask Mr Wu tomorrow and see whether he has a good idea.” Their discussion had, in fact, gone very smoothly. Matchmaking always required negotiation back and forth, bringing the two sides closer step by step. Luo understood that patience gave matters time to come right. “There's no hurry,” she answered calmly. “Take your time thinking it over.” “You may not be in a hurry, but Little Uncle is probably eager to become a bridegroom.” With a smile, the Seventh Lady turned Luo's face toward the light. “I wonder what you'll look like dressed as a bride.” Luo could hardly have described the feelings this stirred in her. “You do love teasing me!” she said, springing to her feet. “I wonder whether Mr Wu has finished his dinner.” “Let's go down together and see.”

Hand in hand, they went downstairs, where Gu Yingchun was showing Mr Wu his little Western ornaments. The Seventh Lady made the customary inquiries about whether their guest had eaten enough, then asked where he was staying. “His room at the inn has already been booked,” Gu said. “Does Sister Luo have anything else she wants to discuss with Mr Wu tonight?” “It's too late tonight. Anything we need to discuss can wait until tomorrow.” “Then I'll take Mr Wu back to his inn. First thing tomorrow, I'll send someone there to accompany him to your house. In the afternoon, I'll show him around.” When Gu returned from seeing their guest home, the Seventh Lady was still awake, waiting to tell him about her conversation with Luo. She ended with the question of how Luo was to enter the Hu household. “To be carried in in a little chair and made to kowtow here, there, and everywhere—quite apart from the humiliation to Sister Luo, it would do us matchmakers no credit.” “For Little Uncle's sake, we can put up with losing a little face,” Gu said. “Don't add your own feelings to the problem. That will only make it harder to settle.” “Very well! But at least Sister Luo must be allowed to keep her dignity.” “That's rather difficult. To get both the substance and all the outward honours—I'm afraid there are few bargains as good as that in this world.” The Seventh Lady knew her husband had a point, but she had promised Luo a dignified arrangement and went on searching for a solution. “Go to sleep. I'm tired,” Gu said. Exhausted by his journey, he began to snore as soon as he was in bed. She could not close her eyes. At last she hit upon an idea of which she was particularly proud. She wanted to wake him and discuss it, but could not bear to disturb him, so she kept it to herself.

Early the next morning, while Gu was washing, the Seventh Lady awoke, drew aside the gauze bed curtains, and poked out her head. “It's all right now! I've found a way.” Gu stared, bewildered by this announcement without preamble. After a moment he understood. “You mean Sister Luo?” “Yes.” She got out of bed, her eyes still heavy with sleep but her face alight with excitement. “They can hold the wedding in Shanghai, with two wives of equal standing in separate households. Then she can have both the decorated bridal chair and the red skirt. What do you think?” “Everyone knows Little Uncle already has a principal wife in Hangzhou. What will they say when people ask?” “He can be heir to two family branches!” she answered at once. “Who's going to investigate their genealogy?” “There's something in that. I wonder whether Little Uncle would agree.” “That's for him to decide. We matchmakers will at least have offered a solution. Besides, I don't think he'll refuse.” Gu considered the idea and agreed to it, asking only, “What happens when she goes back to Hangzhou?” “Treat it like a bride's return visit to her parents: first she kowtows in the ancestral hall, then she meets the old lady and kowtows to her.” “That isn't a return visit to her parents. It's the formal presentation at the ancestral shrine. That would raise Sister Luo's standing.” Gu nodded emphatically. “It could work.” “If you think so, it's settled. Ask Mr Wu this afternoon and see what he says.” “If it can be arranged that way, what objection could he have? But you're wrong to say it's settled. It can only be settled when Little Uncle agrees.” “Then write quickly and ask him.” Gu thought there was no need for such haste. He also thought there was no need to argue with his beloved wife over it: she would not know whether he had written or not. “I'll write,” he said.

Mr Wu visited Luo that morning. In the afternoon, Gu took him sightseeing by carriage, and when they had completed their tour, Mr Wu himself asked to dine at the Gu house so that they could discuss the marriage. “I've talked to her. She says the Seventh Lady knows what she wants. Still, since I'm acting as matchmaker, she has asked me to discuss a few points with you.” “Yes, Mr Wu. Please go ahead.” “First, whether the two families will deal with each other as relatives by marriage can be left aside for now. But she wants Mrs Hu to take her daughter as a god-daughter. The girl will be visiting the Hu house, and the servants must address her as the family's young god-daughter.” “And Mrs Hu's children must call her their younger sister,” the Seventh Lady added with complete confidence. “Leave that to me.” “The second point is more difficult. She says you made her a promise, and she has asked me to find out what arrangement you have in mind.” “We have thought of one, but I can't commit us to it yet. Mr Hu must approve it before it can be agreed.” “Of course. In matchmaking, both parties must consent. It is rare to find someone so decisive and ready to take responsibility on Mr Hu's behalf as you are. But there can be no harm in discussing it first.”

The matter was consequential. If she herself said something wrong, the Seventh Lady thought, it would look awkward to withdraw or alter it. Better to let her husband explain while she watched Mr Wu's expression and supplied a correction or addition at the right moment. At her prompting, Gu described the plan they had discussed that morning. He explained it perfectly, and she felt no need to amend anything. Mr Wu's manner, however, puzzled her. As he listened, his brows drew together. She did not know this was simply his customary expression when thinking hard; she supposed that he was dissatisfied with their proposal and began to resent it. When Gu had finished, she asked coldly, “What does Mr Wu find lacking in this arrangement?” “It isn't a matter of something lacking. I think it is very inappropriate.” Now even Gu was astonished. “Please tell us why. What is inappropriate about it?” “Mr Hu is famous throughout the country. Many people admire and support him, but plenty are jealous and would like to teach him a lesson. If one of the censors in the capital submitted an impeachment, it could cause serious trouble.” “An impeachment? Against Mr Hu?” “I don't understand either,” the Seventh Lady put in. “What wrong would he have committed for a censor to impeach him?” “Seventh Lady, please be patient and let me explain.”

Mr Wu's forebears had served as clerks in the criminal section of the Qiantang county office in Hangzhou Prefecture for four generations. His eldest brother had now inherited the post. The Great Qing Code was a family field of learning, and he was naturally familiar with its provisions on households and marriage. He could therefore give the couple a detailed explanation. Using succession to two family branches as a pretext for taking two wives of equal standing, he said, was a crafty practice established by custom, with no explicit basis in the code. Since the custom had long been followed, the authorities did recognize it, but only under certain conditions. As the saying went, there must be “one son between two branches” before he could serve as heir to both. Hu Xueyan's case plainly failed to meet that requirement. “Consider his title. If there is an Eldest Mr Hu, there is also a Second Mr Hu. It is like the Li family of Hefei: where there is an Eldest Mr Li, Li Hanzhang, there must be a Second Mr Li, Li Hongzhang. Since Mr Hu has brothers, one of them could be adopted as heir by a childless uncle. Why should he himself have to carry on two branches?” “That makes sense. Calling him Eldest Mr Hu shows plainly that he has brothers.” Turning to his wife, Gu said, “Whatever else we do, we cannot invoke succession to two branches.” “And without that, he cannot take two principal wives. If he insists, quite apart from what the Qing code may say, he will already have violated the standards expected of an official. This is called favouring a concubine at the expense of one's wife. A censor need only submit an impeachment: the facts would be there, with no way of evading them.”

The Seventh Lady broke into a cold sweat. “It's fortunate you explained this, Mr Wu. I nearly made a mistake that could have brought Little Uncle down.” “The fall would not be very serious, but it would look bad. To tell the truth, Mr Hu is my second concern. I must think of Sister Luo first. If her wish for a decorated bridal chair and a red skirt brought on all this trouble, Old Madam Hu and Mr Hu would certainly be displeased. Those inclined to sneer would say, ‘Trouble the moment she enters the house! She must bring bad luck.’ Think how hard her life would be after that.” “You have considered everything, Mr Wu. Your judgement is truly exceptional.” The Seventh Lady's admiration was sincere. “Besides, people might not originally have known her status. Then the fact that she was a concubine would be hawked about like the Court Gazette.” “Hawking the Court Gazette” was a Hangzhou expression handed down from the Southern Song. If an ordinary person's name suddenly appeared in the gazette, something newsworthy must have happened. The sellers would shout it out to attract customers until everyone in every street and alley knew. If Hu were reprimanded by imperial decree for favouring a concubine at his wife's expense, Luo's name would appear in the edict. The Qing Palace Gate Bulletin corresponded to the Southern Song Court Gazette, making the Seventh Lady's comparison especially apt. “Exactly,” Mr Wu said. “Then people everywhere would know, not just in Hangzhou and Shanghai. She would truly have sought honour and gained disgrace. I think she'll understand once I explain it clearly.” “Yes, yes!” Gu put in hastily. “Then please explain it to her tactfully. If we can settle this one point, I think our match will be made.”

Naturally Luo could appreciate the difficulty, but she still felt disappointed and dissatisfied. At the same time, she was deeply grateful to the Seventh Lady for speaking up for her and trying so hard to find a solution. Feeling all the more able to confide in her, she brought Mr Wu's explanation back for discussion. “Let me offer you a little advice,” the Seventh Lady said. “A hero need not fear a humble beginning. It is a good outcome that brings real honour. You aren't a narrow-minded woman; don't keep dwelling on this. I'll be your dowry wet nurse and go with you to your new home. How would that be?” In Jiangsu and Zhejiang, when a girl from a wealthy family married, her personal maid and the wet nurse who had brought her up often accompanied her to her husband's home, retaining their old titles. In putting it this way, the Seventh Lady was saying that, if there was humiliation to endure, she was willing to share it. Such affection was not always to be found even between sisters of the same blood; it ought to make up for everything. “Seventh Sister,” Luo said, her eyes reddening, “I wonder how many wooden prayer fish I must have worn out in a previous life to earn the chance of knowing you in this one.” “Knowing me is nothing remarkable. Marrying Little Uncle is the blessing you earned in a former life. A woman can only help her husband prosper; however great her abilities, they have their limits. If her husband is an incapable Prince Adou, even a Zhuge Liang can do nothing but sigh. Little Uncle has so far used only two or three tenths of his ability. If you can bring out the remaining seven or eight tenths, you will rank among the finest of women. Why worry about formal status?”

These words awakened boundless ambition in Luo. “Seventh Sister, I'll tell you what I really think,” she said excitedly. “I've often thought that, if I were a man, I could certainly make something of myself. It's just my misfortune to be a woman. Mr Hu says he is putting the household in my hands, but I don't think he necessarily means to limit me to keeping it in order. He will listen to my ideas about business too. I'd very much like to try my hand at it.” “Yes,” the Seventh Lady said tactfully, “though that comes second. If your ideas are good, he will certainly take your advice, and that amounts to doing the work yourself; you needn't carry it out with your own hands. In my view, your greatest enterprise will be mining—mining a man's abilities. Do you understand?” “No.” Luo laughed. “You have so many tricks, Seventh Sister.” “I'm quite serious. This isn't a trick. As I just said, you must dig out the seven or eight tenths of Little Uncle's ability that he hasn't yet used. If you can do that, you'll have opened a gold mine. Every other business will be a small affair beside it.” At first Luo had thought she was joking. Now she considered the words carefully and gradually understood. “I see what you mean,” she said gravely. “It hadn't occurred to me before. I thought only of showing what I could do myself. Now I understand that, to demonstrate my own ability, I must devote my efforts to bringing out Mr Hu's.” “Exactly!” The Seventh Lady clapped her hands with pleasure. “You are clever, after all. You can think things through and see to the heart of them.”

Only the decorated bridal chair for bringing her to her new home was omitted; in all other respects, the arrangements followed the Six Rites of marriage as closely as possible. First they exchanged cards bearing the birth dates and hours; then came the betrothal gifts. The monetary gift was twenty thousand taels of ready silver, deposited with Fukang Bank in Hangzhou to earn interest for Luo to support her mother. There was a house as well, again bought outside Luosimen. Luo's new home in Shanghai had also been transferred into her name, and Hu naturally settled the purchase price and other expenses advanced by the Seventh Lady. Jewellery was the most important part of the betrothal gifts. There were only four kinds, but they were more valuable than the eight kinds commonly given: newly strung pearl flowers, diamond bracelets, jade earrings, and red jade hairpins. Luo had chosen them herself. Hu had told Gu to ask the Seventh Lady to accompany her in making her selection, then have the jeweller deliver the pieces directly to the Shanghai branch of Fukang Bank for inspection and payment.

“Yingchun said to me yesterday that, since we're as close as sisters, this is like marrying off a sister of our own, and we ought to provide a dowry. He's quite right,” the Seventh Lady told Luo. “I think you should choose that yourself too. For all our sakes, pick the best and don't stand on ceremony. To be frank, Yingchun can still afford a few thousand taels.” Once she was married into the Hu family, Luo thought, there would be plenty of opportunities to help Gu and repay his kindness. There was no need for polite refusals. Still, she was embarrassed to let them spend too much. “I can't refuse such kindness from you and Brother-in-Law Gu. But things needn't be expensive. What matters is that one likes them, wouldn't you say?” “Exactly. Let's choose the furniture first. Are you free tomorrow?” “Yes.” “Then tomorrow afternoon. We may as well go to Changfa again.” Changfa Furniture Shop, in Nanshi, was the largest furniture firm in Shanghai. Luo had bought the furnishings for her new home there. “Good. Changfa it is. I'm expecting a visitor at home today, so I must go.” The Seventh Lady sent her home in her carriage. As soon as she arrived, Luo told Old Ma to hire another carriage: she wanted to take her maid to Nanshi on an errand.

When she alighted at Changfa in Nanshi, the owner, Mr Li, recognized his regular customer and hurried out to greet her himself. “Miss Luo, what brings you here today? Do come inside. Please, come in.” “I've come to look at a set of furniture.” “Ah, yes!” Mr Li was all smiles. “What room is it for?” “The bedroom.” A bedroom set meant, first and foremost, a bed, but since she wanted a whole set, it should naturally include tables, chairs, and stools as well. Mr Li began by asking about the material. “Would you prefer hongmu rosewood or zitan, Miss Luo?” “Zitan, of course.” “If you like zitan, I have a remarkable set. You mustn't miss the opportunity.” “Very well. Let me see it.”

Mr Li led her into a room at the back. The moment she entered, she was dazzled: the zitan furniture was decorated with mother-of-pearl inlay, which caught the light in brilliant flashes. On closer inspection, the pieces were splendid, delicately worked, and very appealing. “This doesn't look like local work,” Luo said. “The designs and workmanship are different.” “You really are a first-rate judge, Miss Luo. You saw it at a glance. This set came from Guangdong. They call the wood suanzhi there; it's what we call zitan. But coming from Guangdong is nothing extraordinary. It has a history that will astonish you.” “Why?” “It was originally made as tribute—” “Tribute?” she interrupted. “You mean it was originally for the emperor?” “Exactly.” “Mr Li,” she said with a laugh, “aren't you afraid a boast like that will burst at the seams? How could furniture meant for the emperor end up in your shop?” “You don't believe me, do you? There is naturally an explanation. Please sit down and let me tell you.”

Mr Li invited her to sit in an armchair and took a lower place beside her. He was an able businessman, and his clerks and apprentices were quick to act. She had scarcely sat down before freshly brewed tea in a covered bowl and four stemmed fruit dishes were brought in. Luo had come intending to buy furniture, and a sale was certain, so she accepted Changfa's hospitality without ceremony or even a word of thanks. “First, please take a good look at the pieces, Miss Luo.” She had intended to do just that. The armchair was compact and elegant, at exactly the right height, with a well-proportioned back. As soon as she sat down, her elbows rested naturally on the arms; it was extremely comfortable. Now she bent her head to examine it closely. Both the material and the craftsmanship were excellent, without a flaw. After the chair, she studied the rectangular nesting tables beside it. There were three, taking up little space when pushed together and proving very useful when drawn out. The tea bowl and all four fruit dishes stood on them without the least crowding. “The pieces are good,” Luo said. “But the design doesn't look like palace furniture. Surely palace pieces should have dragons and phoenixes, not ‘Five Blessings Supporting Longevity.’”
