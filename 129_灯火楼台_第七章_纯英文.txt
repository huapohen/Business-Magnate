CHAPTER SEVEN: A WIFE WHO BRINGS HER HUSBAND PROSPERITY

Once Luo Sijie married into the Hu household, she truly entered upon a fortune that advanced her husband. Hu Xueyan's enterprises flourished like “fresh flowers upon brocade,” as Dream of the Red Chamber puts it. Their prosperity, of course, rested on Zuo Zongtang's support, while Zuo regarded Hu as his indispensable right hand. As the western campaign advanced from victory to victory and the court granted every request Zuo made, Hu rose with the tide and found everything going smoothly. Since the Daoguang reign, benighted rulers and foolish ministers had provoked troubles at home and abroad. The Muslim uprisings in the northwest had likewise been stirred up by corrupt officials. The forces then known as the Gansu Muslims fell into three main divisions, western, southern and northern, with three principal leaders. In the west was Ma Duozhi, based at Xining in Qinghai. In the south was Ma Zhan'ao, whose base was Hezhou, also called Linxia, in the Gansu–Qinghai region. The northern leader, Ma Hualong, was the most formidable of the three. His influence extended over Ningxia and Lingwu, and his stronghold was Jinjibao. It stood at the beginning of the Hetao region, the great bend of the Yellow River celebrated in the saying, “The Yellow River brings a hundred troubles; only its bend grows rich.” Besides controlling the profits from tea and horses, he had a major source of wealth in Huamachi, a salt lake to the east. More than five hundred fortified villages encircled Jinjibao like stars surrounding the moon. Ma was extremely respectful towards Mutushan, the general at Ningxia. Zuo saw what lay beneath this behaviour, and the first objective of his western campaign was to capture Jinjibao.

Before attacking Jinjibao, however, he had to sever the link between the Nian armies and the Gansu Muslim forces. The Nian were divided into two main groups, the Eastern and Western Nian. Explaining their name, Zeng Guofan wrote, “Paper is twisted to burn in oil; hence the name nian.” Anything made by twisting thin paper into a strip, such as the spills used for lighting water pipes, was called a nianzi. The Nian armies could gather and disperse with equal ease. Though they looked like an undisciplined rabble, their unpredictable movements over a thousand li left pursuers exhausted and made them formidable opponents. Sengge Rinchen's Heilongjiang cavalry chased them relentlessly. Whenever they saw it coming, they withdrew. Yet as soon as the cavalry halted, innumerable Nian soldiers would somehow appear all around it. That was how Prince Sengge Rinchen had met his death. He had been fighting the Eastern Nian. Zhang Zongyu, leader of the Western Nian, moved between Henan and Shaanxi, crossing the Yellow River from Henan and pressing north towards Yan'an and Mizhi. His north–south front stretched over a thousand li. He hoped to join Ma Hualong's front, which extended a similar distance from west to east. Once the two linked up, the Western Nian would be beyond control, and there would be no telling when the Muslim uprising could be suppressed. Zuo's initial strategy was therefore to keep them apart and prevent a junction. When Luo married into Hu's household, the Western Nian had just been defeated. The two empress dowagers received Zuo with gracious praise, and he declared that he could suppress the Muslim uprising within five years.

Zuo first stationed his army at Xi'an, then advanced gradually northwest. Headquarters moved first to Qianzhou, then to Jingchuan in Gansu. Turning north, he captured Zhenyuan and Qingyang, took in 170,000 surrendered people and famine refugees, and established agricultural settlements for them. The seeds and farming implements were all procured by Hu's Transport Bureau and shipped to Gansu. When Zuo's advance troops approached Lingwu, Ma Hualong saw that his base was in danger of destruction and submitted a letter asking for pacification. That meant he was willing to surrender: his followers might be absorbed into the Qing army, dispersed, or provided with some means of livelihood. Turning weapons into peaceful relations would have been the ideal outcome. But countless rebels had accepted pacification only to rise again. Zuo was deeply experienced, and Ma had a reputation for repeated reversals. He might play tricks on Mutushan, but Zuo would not be fooled. Ignoring the appeal, he prepared three months' provisions and advanced on Jinjibao.

The general commanding the attack was Liu Songshan, a man from Zeng Guofan's own immediate home district. He had risen from the ranks to become a brigade commander through distinguished service. In the tenth year of Xianfeng, when British and French forces invaded, Sengge Rinchen had taken his troops to defend the emperor. The removal of that fierce cavalry force from the southeast greatly affected the war there. Li Xiucheng, just beginning to enjoy Hong Xiuquan's confidence, convened a military conference at Wuhu and divided the Taiping forces for an advance on several routes. He himself marched south from Wuhu and captured Yi County in southern Anhui. Other formidable Taiping commanders, Li Shixian, Huang Wenjin and Li Jiyuan, successively took Ningguo and Huizhou, followed by Fuliang, Duchang and Raozhou in Jiangxi. Zeng, based at Qimen, was cut off from his Hubei supplies to the west because the route through Jiangxi had been broken. From the east, confident troops led personally by the two Lis pressed upon him. Encircled, he accepted his advisers' proposal to counterattack Huizhou and reopen a supply route to Zhejiang. He therefore moved his army to Xiuning, east of Qimen and west of Huizhou. One night the Taipings attacked, and every camp broke except Liu's. Liu formed his men in the moonlight to meet the enemy, who dared not approach. Troops fleeing from the other camps, unable to see clearly, mistook his ranks for a Taiping blocking force and turned to run. Only when Liu displayed the proper recognition signals did they realize that headquarters still held and the old commander was safe. Their terror subsided. Since his defeat at Jinggang, when he had thrown himself into the water and been rescued, this had been Zeng's next great crisis. He had even written his last letters. His escape was entirely due to Liu, whom he thereafter treated as a man of exceptional national worth.

When Zuo received command of the western expedition, its scale demanded cooperation from all the armies; this was no merely local operation. Zeng placed his most valued force, Liu Songshan's, under Zuo's command. Zuo had originally owed his advancement to Zeng, but they had later quarrelled over military funds. When Zeng Guoquan captured Jinling and allowed Hong Xiuquan's son to escape, Zuo openly denounced the matter in a memorial. Their relations broke down, and they had long ceased corresponding. Only now did Zuo acknowledge, “In loyalty to the country's interests and discernment of men, I am ashamed to fall short of the Grand Secretary.” He compared Zeng's placing Liu under his orders to giving a daughter in marriage. Needless to say, he entrusted Liu with important duties. Liu fully justified both Zeng's recognition and Zuo's expectations, winning almost every engagement against the Western Nian and the Gansu Muslim forces. Before enlisting, he had become engaged at home but had not married. In more than ten years of service, he returned home only once, to recruit men. Not until the Western Nian were defeated did he finally marry at Luoyang. Bride and groom were both over thirty. After a honeymoon of only ten days, he entered Shaanxi. Clearing the four districts of Yu, Yan, Sui and Fu, he advanced on Lingwu and took it in a single battle. Unable to defeat him, Ma Hualong again sought pacification while appealing everywhere for help. But the Muslim armies at Xining, Hezhou, Lintao and Jingyuan, overawed by Liu's reputation, dared not move rashly. Liu launched a major assault. In the first lunar month of the ninth year of Tongzhi, he was struck by cannon fire while attacking a fortified village outside Jinjibao, fell from his horse and died. His nephew Liu Jintang took command. In the eleventh month of that same year, Jinjibao finally fell.

The western army won more often than it lost and advanced steadily because its weapons were superior, its morale high and its discipline good. With Gu Yingchun's help, Hu bought any new Western firearms or ingenious military equipment that could be useful, without first asking Zuo for instructions. Supplies arrived on time; the troops never feared an interruption in provisions or pay. Naturally, morale was high. Officers and men admired Hu for this and were grateful. But only Zuo fully understood that their discipline too owed much to him. Since the closing years of Xianfeng, when Saishang’a, grandfather of the Tongzhi emperor's empress of the Aludai clan, had lost his army and squandered two million taels of military funds from the ministry treasury, the provinces had been fighting their own wars. Hence the various Chu, Hunan, Huai, Zhejiang and Guangdong armies, all called government troops. They came from many sources. Men recruited in their commanders' home districts formed the majority, but there were also bandits and men transferred from elsewhere. Their backgrounds differed, as did their officers' methods, and discipline varied greatly. Within the Hunan Army, Peng Yulin's men were the best disciplined and Bao Chao's the worst, reflecting their commanders' differing views. But Bao was brave and skilful, a favourite of Zeng's, and was indulged in many things. Zuo's forces were equally mixed, yet they all observed discipline. Half the credit belonged to his strict command; the other half to their wholehearted devotion, which made them unwilling to disobey. For that devotion, Hu deserved acknowledgement. Hunan men served all over the empire without anxiety for those at home because his extensive network of banks and convenient remittances supported their families regularly. When officers or soldiers died, he sent men to provide death benefits and support the survivors without waiting for Zuo's instructions. Everyone thought: the marquis cares so much for us; how could we bear to violate his discipline? They did not know that Hu was helping the marquis maintain it.

Hu managed to attend to public duties and private business alike. Banks, pawnshops and silk firms opened one after another, his enterprises expanded, and his reputation as the God of Wealth grew ever louder. Everyone, beginning with Old Madam Hu, attributed it to Madam Luosi. That title had arisen because they wished to avoid calling her Second Madam but could think of nothing more suitable. A learned man had offered a precedent: “In the Shunzhi reign, Gong Zhilu, one of the Three Great Masters of the Lower Yangtze, married Gu Meisheng, who came from the Qinhuai courtesan quarter. Gong's principal wife addressed her as Madam Gu. Why not follow that example and replace the ‘Sister’ in ‘Luo Sijie’ with ‘Madam’?” Thus she became Madam Luo Si. Servants who did not understand the reasoning assumed the title came from her family home outside Luosi, or Snail, Gate, and it became Madam Luosi. What most impressed their neighbours, however, and made Hu feel indebted to her, loving and respecting her all the more, was her reputation for extraordinary virtue: she arranged for him to take eleven more concubines.

About a year after her marriage, Madam Luosi confided her troubles to the Seventh Lady, who had come to Hangzhou to offer incense at the Three Tianzhu Temples. Hu had exceptional sexual energy. Being his sole nightly companion had become more than she could manage, and the pleasure no longer outweighed the strain. Hu consequently began lingering among the courtesans. Entertaining at their houses had long been an almost daily duty for him, but previously he had confined himself to dining and drinking there. Gradually he began treating the brothels as residences, frequently staying all night and even receiving visitors and transacting official business there. That was cause for concern. “Seventh Sister, he is riding high now. He cannot afford this run of romantic adventures. First, it harms his health. Second, it delays important business. Third, it damages his reputation. And fourth, what if every employee follows his example, whoring and gambling without restraint? Even if they don't imitate him, they will be discouraged. They work so hard to help him build his enterprises, only to discover that he is such a disappointment.” The Seventh Lady knew that these last words expressed her friend's own resentment. Still, she was puzzled. “Everyone knows that Little Uncle has never overcome his weakness for women. But why should it have reached such a degree?” Madam Luosi could not suppress a complaint. “It is all the fault of our Third Uncle Liu!”

Hu had decided to open a medicine shop. He had long wished to do so, and an affront had finally prompted him. The previous summer, his mother had suffered an attack of sha after exposure to the heat. The traditional remedy was to scrape the skin with a copper coin, producing a bright red streak, after which the symptoms promptly subsided. Old Madam Hu had been thoroughly scraped and was already out of danger, but her son remained anxious. He sent for a physician, then had the prescription taken to be made up, repeatedly telling the servant to hurry. Even so, the man did not return for two double-hours. Hu was exceptionally particular about anything concerning his mother, and he flew into one of his rare tempers. Only after the scolding ended did the servant explain. An epidemic was spreading that year, and customers had to queue for medicines. After waiting for more than a double-hour, he had lost patience and squeezed to the front, hoping to have his prescription filled out of turn, as people sometimes did when seeking a consultation. “Please hurry. Our old lady is waiting to take it.” “Who doesn't have an old lady at home?” the shop assistant replied. “If you are in such a hurry, why don't you open your own medicine shop?” The scolded servant now vented his resentment on the assistant, embellishing the account until he rekindled Hu's anger. Stamping his foot, Hu declared, “Very well! I shall open one and show him.” Liu Bucai was ordered to make the preparations and set off that very day for Zhili to buy medicinal ingredients. Along with them, he brought back several hundred dogskin plasters for Hu to try.

These dogskin plasters were a kind of sexual stimulant. While buying medicines, Liu's generosity and humour had won him many friends. Over wine, their conversation inevitably turned to women. One man from Suzhou mentioned Hong Jun, the top graduate of the previous imperial examination and now Education Commissioner of Hebei. Hong had recently written home asking someone to find him a concubine. His letter was frank: the purpose of taking a concubine was to enjoy life while one could. There was no need for excuses such as “Of the three failures in filial duty, having no heir is the greatest,” when a man lacked a son, or “There is no one to look after me,” when he lived away from home. Such claims merely deceived oneself and others. Enjoying life while one could, he said, had two sides. First, one must be timely: take a concubine early, for by middle age strength begins to decline. To face beauty with insufficient vigour was to disappoint not only oneself but another person. Second came enjoyment, which naturally required a beautiful concubine. His listeners responded to such frankness with equal sincerity and eventually found him a celebrated Shanghai courtesan of exceptional beauty, Sai Jinhua. Another man lamented that in youth, while building a career, one had ample energy but no money. Once established, one had money but insufficient energy: concubines everywhere, broad fields lying untilled, and perhaps the risk of being cuckolded. What greater regret could life offer? This reminded Liu that Hu might feel the same. He asked whether there was a good aphrodisiac that strengthened virility without harming the body. Someone directed him to a small medicine shop near Beijing's Drum Tower. It sold externally applied dogskin plasters, said to act gently, unlike internal stimulants that exhausted a man like draining a pond to catch its fish. The proprietor, however, was ashamed of making and selling them. A close friend's introduction was required, and they were prepared only to order, never sold over the counter. Liu's popularity enabled him to find a suitable intermediary. He paid heavily for a batch. After trying them, Hu gave free rein to himself in bed.

“There is no remedy,” the Seventh Lady said, “unless you can accept the situation generously.” She meant: could Madam Luosi allow Hu to take another concubine and share his favour? Madam Luosi considered her position, halfway between principal wife and concubine. The old lady trusted and favoured her, and she lived peacefully with the principal wife, but managing the household inevitably earned servants' resentment. Suppose a formidable new concubine arrived and, egged on by servants, allied herself with the principal wife, disregarding the rule that earlier entry conferred seniority and opposing her openly and covertly at every turn. She might not be afraid, but she would constantly be angry. That danger could not be ignored. As she reflected, the Seventh Lady spoke again. “Last autumn Yingchun had a fever. During his convalescence he couldn't go out and was dreadfully bored, so we hired a storyteller to perform at home for two hours every afternoon. The book was Tales of Heroic Sons and Daughters. Its account of women's jealousy made considerable sense.” “What did the storyteller say?” “He divided jealous women into those who manage jealousy well and those who don't, with three grades in each. Those who don't are laughable, pitiable or terrifying. For example…” “Never mind those who don't,” Madam Luosi interrupted. “Very well. Those who manage it well are also divided into three grades: ordinary, skilful and divine. We can leave out the ordinary. Take the skilful. A first-rate woman like you might see Little Uncle marry someone who surpassed even you. Yet if you remained generous, showed no disturbance and cared as attentively as ever for his food and daily comforts, he would feel indebted to you and you would retain his favour. That is skilful jealousy.”

By that account, Madam Luosi thought, the principal wife belonged to the skilful grade. What, then, was the divine? She asked aloud. “Nothing particularly extraordinary, once explained. With your ability, it would not be difficult. In a word, combine kindness with authority. If she behaves properly, treat her well; cherish her even more than Little Uncle does. If she breaks the rules, speak to her and keep her in order. When she has children, understand their needs better than their own mother and be better at bringing them up. Then who in the whole household will not respect you? Who will not call you virtuous? That is truly divine jealousy.” Every word went straight to Madam Luosi's heart, and she saw a further implication. In the household hierarchy, the old lady came first, the master second, the principal wife third, and she herself only fourth. Apart from the servants, there were people to govern her but nobody for her to govern. If she personally selected several more concubines for Hu and trained them through kindness and authority until they sincerely acknowledged her, she would at last display the full dignity and power of the household's manager. A smile appeared on her face without her noticing. The Seventh Lady smiled too. “Well, Sister Luo? Would you like to practise this jealousy that does not look like jealousy?” “I only fear I may not know how. Keep an eye out for someone for me, Seventh Sister.” The Seventh Lady realized that she had decided to act on the advice. It had begun as casual conversation; even if meant as a plan, it would require long consideration. She had never expected such immediate acceptance.

At that thought, the Seventh Lady suddenly felt a weight on her shoulders. As the saying went, “If you want discord at home, take a concubine.” Women like Madam Luosi were exceedingly rare. Besides, Hu had chosen Luo for himself. Even if things had gone badly after she entered the household, the Seventh Lady could not have been blamed. This was different: it now seemed entirely her proposal. If trouble followed, everyone, beginning with Old Madam Hu, would resent her. She must therefore think carefully on her friend's behalf. “Sister Luo, things sound fine in the planning but are not so easy to carry out. If Little Uncle wants to marry someone from a courtesan house, you must refuse. Such women have habits that make them difficult to manage.” “Yes. She must be a girl from a respectable family. I shall make it clear to him first: he may look for someone himself, but I must see her. In fact, there is no need for him to look. I shall ask people to choose someone for him.” She did as she said. Over three or four years, the women she found, together with those Hu chose himself, made eleven additional concubines. Including her, they numbered exactly twelve, like the Twelve Beauties of the novel.

With more family members and an expanding business continually bringing new personnel, the house proved inadequate despite repeated extensions. Eventually the limits of the site meant either complete rebuilding or finding another home. Hu discussed it with Madam Luosi. He proposed buying land and building a new residence elsewhere, moving his two younger brothers there along with the various people who worked for him, and demolishing the vacated buildings to make a garden. He also wanted a gallery-lined residence where his Twelve Beauties could live together. Madam Luosi approved of the latter but objected to the separate house. “Asking Second Master and Third Master to move out will hurt your mother. The relatives will gossip too. You ought to reconsider.” At the thought of hurting his mother, Hu abandoned the proposal at once. “But there isn't enough room. We must do something. Have you a better idea?” “I hear the Liu house next door is for sale. The owner of the rice shop behind us has died, and his two sons keep quarrelling over the division of the estate and who should run the shop. They also want to sell the building and divide the cash. Why not buy both properties and break through the walls to join them to ours?” This revived Hu's appetite for grand display. Silk prices happened to rise sharply that year. Through his control of the silk trade with foreign houses, he made four hundred thousand taels in three months. He decided on a grand garden. “Let us pretend I never earned that money. I shall follow your suggestion, but on a larger scale. Streets must bound it on all four sides, making one great, regular block, with the grandeur of a prince's mansion.” Madam Luosi was naturally pleased by such distinction. Hu sent men to persuade the surrounding households, offering substantial inducements. All but two agreed to move and sell.

One holdout was a wine warehouse. It said the stored wine was too difficult to move and refused to sell, firmly but politely. The other was different. It was a tiny barbershop at the corner of Yuanbao Street and Wangxianqiao Straight Street, a place everyone had to pass when entering or leaving. To have a gap in the estate at such an important junction—and a dilapidated barbershop at that—was as intolerable as a peerless beauty with one eye blind. “What does she want?” Hu demanded. “If she wants a good price, let her name it. Whatever she asks, she can have.” “She” was Old Mrs Cui, the proprietor's mother. Her son was stubborn too, but the Hu household's frequent visits and courteous persuasion, together with the prospect of money, had begun to move him.

His mother, however, refused absolutely. Widowed young and left without support, she had struggled to bring him up, only to see him become a barber. Rather than blame herself for choosing that trade for him, she accused Heaven of blindness. Over time she had grown contrary and bitter, hating the wealthy in proportion to their wealth. Hu's promise to pay whatever she asked touched precisely her sore point. “Tell your employer that he may be the God of Wealth, but we are paupers. We have no dealings with him. Let him make his fortune. He is neither the King of Hell nor a judge in the underworld. I have no reason to fear him.” The negotiator was Zhang Zihong, a literary hanger-on in Hu's household, famous for his mild temper. Even he lost patience. “He may not be the King of Hell or an underworld judge, but he is a circuit intendant.” “Does that put him above the law? My son and I have only our two lives. Let him do as he pleases.” When Hu heard this, he was so angry that he ate nothing all day. His household retainers, accountants, managers and servants proposed all kinds of measures: send the county bailiffs, burn down the house, or forge a deed and sue her. Hu could not bring himself to do it. Madam Luosi also feared that coercion might lead to a death and restrained the servants from any rash act. Even after the great mansion was completed and Yuanbao Street repaved, the barbershop remained.

The completion was celebrated with three days of banquets. On the first day, the guests were the three principal provincial authorities, the prefect of Hangzhou, the magistrates of Renhe and Qiantang, and expectant circuit intendants, with local gentry invited to keep them company. Before the meal, Hu personally led them through the garden. Following its winding paths around a rockery, they saw an extraordinarily splendid tower rising among the trees to the southeast. What puzzled them was the gold light flashing from the carved railings all around it. At that distance, nobody could account for it. “Mr Hu,” Governor Yang Changjun asked, “what is that building?” “The residence of my wife.” Hearing that it belonged to the women's quarters, Yang could not inquire further, but private questions brought the explanation. It was called the Hundred Lions Tower. One hundred lions carved and polished from zitan stood on the railing posts. Their protruding eyes were cast in gold and glittered in the sunlight so brightly that one could scarcely look at them. “Why call the ladies' residence Hundred Lions Tower? Does he not know the saying about the lioness of Hedong roaring?” “No, that isn't it. The lady is called Madam Luosi, and he built the tower for her.” Yang asked how she had acquired the name and what her origins were. Once informed, he felt that Hu had gone rather too far and quietly wrote to Zuo at Suzhou in Gansu, expressing some criticism.

Zuo disagreed considerably with Yang but could not conveniently say so outright. As it happened, his eldest son also wrote to criticize Hu, giving him a suitable occasion to express his view. A man's enjoyments, he said, should be proportionate to his deserts. The world did not fully understand Hu's services, but he did. The western campaign owed its present success entirely to Hu; a little excess in his pleasures need give him no shame. He cited Tao Shu, his closest lifelong friend and the father of his child's spouse. When Tao was Governor-General of Liangjiang, his son-in-law Hu Linyi, a Hanlin academician living idly at Jiangning, spent his days seeking singers and beautiful women, all at the expense of his father-in-law's integrity allowance. Once Tao's household accountant asked whether Hu Linyi's extravagance ought to be restrained. “Let him spend,” Tao replied. “He will serve the country one day. Then, even if he has money, he will have no time to spend it.” Hu Xueyan's circumstances differed, but compared with the great benefits he secured for the country, even extravagant personal pleasures counted for little. These remarks eventually reached Zhejiang. Deeply grateful, Hu served Zuo with still greater loyalty and effort. Madam Luosi, however, grew uneasy. In private talks with the Seventh Lady, both agreed that a tall tree catches the wind: it was time to become less conspicuous. Yet Hu needed only to ask, “How are we to cut back?” and she had no answer. For Hu depended on credit; credit rested on the confidence others placed in him; and that confidence was created by the grandeur of his establishment. The display could only grow, never shrink. Otherwise, a single casual remark—“Mr Hu is no longer what he used to be”—would instantly set off endless speculation. Those who bore him grudges, and those who had no grievance but were merely envious, would fan the rumours. His credit would be shaken, with consequences too terrible to contemplate.
