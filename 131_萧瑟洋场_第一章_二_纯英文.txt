HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Desolate Foreign Settlement

Chapter 1 — Part 2 of 4

Dexin chose each word carefully and couched the message in guarded language. After rereading it, he said, “Grand Secretary Bao and his brother are not on good terms. Second Master Sen may have no influence with him. Should we ask Manager Wang to sound out Saltpeter Li as well?” “Yes. I agree.” Dexin revised the draft. Hu called in A’xi, his young personal attendant, whose particular duty was to look after the Western leather bag that accompanied his master whenever he left home. Inside it was a telegraphic codebook. Hu and Dexin encoded the message themselves. By the time they had finished, the setting sun was touching the hills. “I had not intended to go back to the city,” Hu said, “but now I must. The telegram has to be taken to Shanghai to be sent. I shall dispatch someone reliable and have him wait there for the reply. If it is a matter of twenty or thirty thousand taels, I shall advance it for you. More than that would hardly be worthwhile.” “Indeed, indeed. I leave everything in your hands. I cannot thank you enough.”

Hu Xueyan and Dexin then set off for the city together. Their official ranks were the same, but Hu had been granted the privilege of wearing the yellow riding jacket, so his ceremonial retinue was more imposing than that of Dexin, the serving provincial treasurer. Hu’s tall official placards were used only for display, however. When he traveled, there was simply one mounted outrider in front and four mounted attendants behind. His eight-man sedan had three teams of bearers, who took turns at the poles. Hu’s bearers were themselves addressed as “master” at home. When one returned, a maid would call, “The master is back! Hurry and bring water to wash his feet.” Yet however desirable the position, carrying Hu’s sedan was no easy task. He demanded both speed and steadiness: the men must keep pace with the leading horse, yet never spill the tea resting on the armboard inside. Thus, although Hu and Dexin started at the same time, Hu’s sedan took the lead from the first step and soon left Dexin far behind.

Approaching Yuanbao Street, Hu could see the lanterns, decorations, and brilliant lights from a distance. The birthday hall itself was fairly quiet. Since particular days had been assigned to the guests, only a few of the closest relatives would venture to call unannounced. Hu alighted, exchanged a few words in the hall, and immediately turned to the business of securing an office for Dexin. He had just summoned a capable household servant and begun giving his instructions when Madam Luosi quietly appeared, leaning on a little maid’s shoulder. Seeing that he was occupied, she seated herself some distance away in a velvet chair. “Leave first thing tomorrow morning and wait in Shanghai for news. As soon as the reply from Beijing arrives, come straight back. The sooner the better.” The servant assented and withdrew. Madam Luosi rose and approached. “Why have you come back to the city instead of staying at Lingyin with the old lady?” “Two governors-general’s posts have fallen vacant, which should mean two vacant governorships as well. De Xiaofeng wants one of them. I had to come in to make arrangements for him.” As he spoke, Hu noticed something unusual in her expression. Looking at her closely, he asked, “What is this? Have you been crying?” “Do not talk nonsense! Why should I cry on the old lady’s happy day?” She immediately changed the subject. “Were there many guests?”

“Everyone who ought to have come was there,” Hu said. “There are not many officials of the third rank or above to begin with. From tomorrow onward, each day will be busier than the last. What worries me most is the day after tomorrow. Everyone says they are going to watch the fun. I wonder whether something ridiculous will happen.” The schedule had been rearranged, and the third day was now reserved for the foreign guests. The very phrase “foreigners offering birthday greetings” excited curiosity. People wondered what the foreigners would actually do: kowtow or bow with clasped hands? Would they use chopsticks to eat the birthday noodles? If they could not, what would they use? A fork would never hold them; perhaps they would have to scoop them up with their fingers. All this talk of waiting to enjoy a joke made Hu uneasy. He feared an embarrassing scene that would cost him face. “Oh,” Madam Luosi said, reminded of something, “there is a present here you should look at.” She called through the window for a servant. Ruixiang, her trusted senior maid, entered. Having already heard her mistress, she said at once, “The gifts from the foreign gentleman have been taken to the master’s study.” Hu reflected that he had received countless birthday presents over the past month or so. If these alone had been sent to his study, there must be something special about them. “Who sent them?” he asked.

“I am not sure either,” Madam Luosi replied. “They came through the customs office at Gongchen Bridge. I wanted you to have a look. There is a formal visiting card with them; that will tell you.” “Very well. I shall go to the study.” “Yes, do. They are about to begin dinner outside, and I must put in an appearance and see to the guests. What about you? Where will you eat?” “I am too tired to eat much. Some congee will do.” “You must at least have some of the old lady’s birthday noodles.” Madam Luosi turned to give an order. “Ruixiang, tell the small kitchen to make a bowl of fine noodles in chicken broth. If the broth is too rich, have them skim off all the fat.” The three went their separate ways. Alone, Hu followed the long passage, which even on ordinary nights was lined with lamps and easy to walk along, to his study in Jingjian Pavilion.

Jingjian Pavilion was one of the garden’s finest sights. A still pond lay before it and an artificial hill behind. The stair rail had an iron core sheathed in porcelain cylinders shaped like bamboo joints, made to order at Jingdezhen in alternating crimson and green. Inside stood an immense mirrored panel that drew into its surface the pond outside, the mandarin ducks on the water, the red bridge across it, and the willows along the banks. The design followed Jian Garden, Prince Gong’s retreat beside Shichahai outside Xuanwu Gate in Beijing, but surpassed it in delicacy. On entering, Hu stood before the mirror for a while. Distant towers and nearby galleries were hung with birthday lanterns and decorations. Their reflections fell into the pond and then appeared anew in the mirror: mirrors within mirrors, images within images, until reality and illusion could no longer be distinguished. As he gazed, absorbed, he heard a young voice. “Master, the room is open.”

Hu looked up. He seemed to have seen the little maid before. “What is your name?” “Xiaomei.” “Oh. Have you just been assigned here?” “No. I have been here a long time.” “A long time? Why do I hardly ever see you?” As he spoke, he stepped into the study and caught sight of a great heap of presents. Forgetting Xiaomei for the moment, he looked first for the visiting card.

The formal card was signed, “He Lubin, your humble friend, honored by your instruction.” The sender was the Englishman Robert Hart, Inspector-General of Customs. He had lived in China for more than twenty years, spoke excellent Beijing Chinese, and could read Chinese writing. An admirer of Chinese culture and a foreign servant of the throne entitled to wear an official hat button, he had given himself the Chinese name He Lubin, fashioned from the sound of his English name. A gift list accompanied the card. There were four birthday presents: a complete set of fine porcelain tea things, a large silver bowl filled with sweets, a whole bolt of woolen cloth, and a purple sable muff suitable for an elderly lady.

“Someone come here!” Hu had in mind the two senior maids who looked after Jingjian Pavilion, the sisters Qiaozhu and Qiaozhen. But it was Xiaomei who came. “Neither of the two Qiaos happens to be handy,” she said with a play on their names. “They have both gone to Lingyin with the old lady.” Her quick speech made Hu think she was quite capable of doing an errand. “You will do just as well. Find two people to carry these four presents outside, and have them sent straight to Lingyin for the old lady to see. Say that they are from—” He stopped. To explain that He Lubin was Hart, and that this foreign dignitary also wore a red button on his official hat, would be too complicated; the messenger might not convey it clearly. “What would you like, Master?”

“I want to write something.” Xiaomei immediately went to the desk, lifted the inkstone’s cover, poured in a small spoonful of clean water, and began carefully rubbing the inkstick. Hu sat down, dipped his brush, and with considerable effort wrote six characters on the visiting card: “That is, Hart, Inspector-General of Customs.” As the ink was still wet, Xiaomei picked up the card and pursed her lips to blow on it. She was blowing busily when Ruixiang arrived.

At the sight, Ruixiang first stared, then snapped at Xiaomei. “Get out! What right have you to be in here?” The Hu household had adopted the conventions of great aristocratic families, with several ranks even among the servants. A young maid like Xiaomei, employed on rough household work, was not supposed to appear before the master. To do so was to overstep her station. Ruixiang ordinarily presumed on her position as Madam Luosi’s confidante and treated the other maids as beneath her. She was unpopular, and Xiaomei was not inclined to submit to her. Blamed without cause, the girl felt deeply wronged. Young as she was, she had a sharp tongue and retorted at once. “Qiaozhu is away, and the master came in. Was I not to attend to him? I did not go looking for a chance to curry favor. Why are you shouting at me? We are all lowly servants. Who do you think you are impressing, putting on the airs of a wealthy young lady?” “What!” Ruixiang’s face went white with anger. “How dare you answer back!” She rushed forward to strike her. Xiaomei stood her ground and spoke quickly. “This is the old lady’s happy day. Would you dare hit someone?”

Ruixiang was checked. Only with an effort did she lower her raised hand. Grinding her teeth, she cursed. “If it were not for the old lady’s celebration, I would tear you apart, you filthy little—! Just wait. I shall deal with you.” Now Xiaomei was frightened. She knew only too well how much power Ruixiang wielded. In desperation she dropped to her knees before Hu Xueyan. “Master, you see how it is. Please be fair to me.” “There, there,” Hu said soothingly. “It was I who told her to grind the ink. If you will not excuse her for her own sake, do it for mine. There is no need to tell your mistress.” With the master himself pleading for her, Ruixiang had recovered some face and vented her anger. “Still kneeling there in the hope of a reward?” she shouted. “I shall reward you with a good beating! Get out!” Seeing tears well in Xiaomei’s eyes, she added, “This is the old lady’s happy day. Do not dare start crying!” Xiaomei did not dare. She withdrew with tears brimming in both eyes. Hu felt deeply sorry for her but could hardly comfort her in Ruixiang’s presence. Fortunately, a ready means of drawing Ruixiang away lay before him: the birthday gifts had to be sent to Lingyin. When Ruixiang went downstairs to summon help, Hu stepped into the gallery and said softly, “Xiaomei, do not be afraid. Do not be upset. Tomorrow I shall speak to your mistress and have you promoted.” When Hu spoke of “your mistress” to the servants, he usually meant Madam Luosi. “I do not want that,” Xiaomei replied. “How could I ever have a peaceful day under Ruixiang?”

Hu was about to ask her more when Ruixiang unexpectedly returned. On going downstairs, she had met Second Madam Yang, one of the household’s four chief women stewards, whose special charge was to supervise everyone entering or leaving the garden. Carrying a palace lantern herself, she was escorting Gu Yingchun to see the master. Ruixiang exchanged tasks with her, and each turned back: one went to find people to deal with Hart’s presents, while the other brought Gu upstairs. “Why have you come back?” Hu asked. Gu had intended to stay at Lingyin in preparation for receiving the British visitors who would offer birthday greetings the next day. But news that Hart had unexpectedly arrived in Hangzhou had brought him back to find out more.

“I only discovered it was Hart when I read his card just now,” Hu said, pointing to the four presents. “Look, I was about to send these to Lingyin for the old lady to inspect.” Gu examined the gifts appreciatively and nodded. “By foreign standards, this is a very substantial present.” It was plainly a sign of the sender’s high regard, and Hu could not help feeling pleased. After a moment’s thought, he said, “I wonder where he is staying. It is too late today. First thing tomorrow morning, you and I should go and call on him. That is only proper, since we are the hosts.” “He is staying with Dr Meitenggeng.” Meitenggeng was an English missionary and physician who had come to Hangzhou to preach and established a hospital at Dafangbo in the center of the city. There was a bridge there that had been called Guangji Bridge in the Song dynasty, and the hospital took the same name, Guangji, which also conveyed the idea of bringing help to all. Hu had made a large donation when the hospital was founded and could count the doctor as an old friend. “Since he is staying there,” he said, “I shall send someone to ask the doctor to tell Hart that we will call tomorrow morning, and to find out what time would suit him.” “There is no need to send anyone. Calling on a doctor in the evening cannot be considered improper. I shall go myself; it will be more satisfactory.”

“Very well. Thank you for the trouble. Have you eaten?” “I have been so busy I forgot to be hungry. In fact, I am not hungry now.” “Neither am I. I shall wait for you, and we can eat together when you return.” “Good.” “Ruixiang, show Mr Gu down.” Then Hu suddenly asked, “When did these presents arrive?” “About three in the afternoon,” Ruixiang replied. “Dr Mei sent someone with them.”

“At that hour!” Hu frowned. “We ought, properly, to have sent over a banquet.” “We did not send a banquet,” Ruixiang put in. “But we sent a first-class hotpot, four kinds of pastries, and a basket of juicy flat peaches. Madam had me prepare a gratuity envelope for the messenger [the amount is corrupt in the source]. She told him to explain to Dr Mei that our master was at Lingyin and therefore did not know who this foreign dignitary was, but that he must in any case be a good friend of our master’s. Dr Mei was like one of the family, and we asked him to take the trouble to entertain the guest on our behalf. When our master returned tomorrow, he would thank him in person.” Ruixiang rattled it all off in one breath, setting out everything clearly. Hu thought Madam Luosi had handled the matter most suitably. “A good thing I did not have her come to Lingyin,” he said with satisfaction. “Otherwise there would have been no one capable of attending to it.” “A good thing, too, that a strong commander has no weak soldiers.” Ruixiang recognized Gu’s compliment, smiled prettily at him, then turned her head away. Gu smiled as well, two crow’s-feet showing at the corners of his eyes. When Ruixiang returned from seeing him out, she said to Hu, “I suppose you will not want the noodles now. I have told the small kitchen to prepare a few delicate, refreshing dishes. Where would you like dinner served?”

“Here will do,” Hu replied. Then he added, “I had no idea you were so vicious! A woman may be formidable; that is all very well. But viciousness will only hurt her in the end.” “Madam runs the household. Someone has to play the villain for her. Would you have her be the villain herself while we stand by putting in kind words for everyone?” Hu could find no answer to that. After a moment’s thought he said, “Even if you must play the villain, there is no need to behave like a fool. Those filthy insults you used—do you not hear how ugly they sound?” Ruixiang flushed. She wanted to defend herself but could think of no excuse, and stood rooted to the spot, at a loss what to do.

Hu offered a literary quotation. “‘A person must first demean herself before others will demean her.’ Suppose she had turned your own obscenity back on you,” he went on. “How would a young woman like you have held up her head?” In Hangzhou, the expression he used referred to a girl in the bloom of youth, the age at which feelings were most tender and easily stirred. Ruixiang was ashamed and remorseful. Her eyes reddened, and she was on the verge of tears. “Why, why, why!” Hu exclaimed in astonishment. “You forbade her to cry, and now you are about to cry yourself. What is the matter? Have I spoken too harshly?” At once Ruixiang checked her tears. She drew from beneath her arm a white silk handkerchief embroidered with a spray of daphne, the flower whose Chinese name was her own, and blew her nose. “Who is crying?” “Best not to cry. Bring me the dominoes, then go out front and see how far the dinner has got.”

Ruixiang fetched a box of ivory dominoes, tipped them onto the square rosewood table, and went downstairs. Left alone, Hu played “Passing the Five Gates” to while away the time. After several unsuccessful rounds, he turned from the game to divination, seeking a sign of his future. Following the rhyme for consulting the domino oracle—“Lay the full set out in line; count the openings that you find; three successive layouts mark, then judge the high, the low, the middle”—he counted sixteen openings in the first layout, twenty-one in the second, but in the third only one matched pair and one unmatched combination, six openings in all.

Hu knew the method well. He worked out the result as “highest auspice, highest auspice, lower middle.” He could more or less remember the verse, but he needed the book for its “Explanation” and “Judgment.” Finding the section on the domino oracle in Elegant Pastimes of the Ladies’ Chamber, he opened it and read: “With a fair wind, hoist your sail in time; / Cross safely the whale’s vast waters, ten thousand miles. / Should you reach the turn where the sail follows the Xiang, / Rein in the gallant horse before it takes the downward slope.” As he recited it, he thought, “There is something in that.” He read on: “Explanation: Do not torment yourself over your undertakings. Their fulfillment lies before your eyes. Whatever you attempt will prosper, but everywhere you must handle others tactfully.” At the last phrase, he suddenly slapped the table and exclaimed, “Today’s reading is uncannily accurate!” He had scarcely finished when someone asked, “What are you doing?” Looking up, he saw Madam Luosi entering with a hand on a little maid’s shoulder; Ruixiang followed behind. “Have the guests gone?”

“Not yet, but every table has someone to attend to it,” Madam Luosi replied. “I heard that Brother-in-law Gu had come back and gone out again. I wondered whether something important had happened, so I came specially to see.” “He has gone to Dr Meitenggeng’s. He will be back as soon as he has had a word with him.” Hu then went on to the “Judgment” that followed the “Explanation.”
