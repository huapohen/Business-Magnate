HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Desolate Foreign Settlement

Chapter 2 — Part 1 of 6

CHAPTER TWO

Most silkworm-raising households in Jiangsu and Zhejiang both produced and marketed their own silk. They could, of course, sell their cocoons to cocoon firms holding licenses from the ministry. But those firms offered low prices, and the members of the trade had agreed to a uniform rate. Once a family decided to sell cocoons, exploitation was unavoidable. Moreover, cocoons were bought only on specified days. Sometimes the growers could not wait because they needed money urgently; sometimes the cocoons could not wait, since in time the moths would bite their way out and ruin them. Unless driven by necessity or guided by some special calculation, families therefore raised their own silkworms and made their own silk. This provided a livelihood for many people. From boiling the cocoons onward, silk-making involved a multitude of operations. After reeling came twisting and beating, scouring and dyeing, twisting weft silk into warp silk, and the various stages of preparing and drawing out the warp. Only after the final joining of the threads could the silk go onto a loom to be woven into fabric. Once a mechanized silk-reeling mill appeared, however, cocoons went in at one end and silk emerged at the other. The beating and drawing of threads were no longer needed, and the workers who had performed them lost their livelihoods. More serious still, almost every rural household in Jiangsu and Zhejiang had a reeling wheel. Women of all ages relied on it for supplementary income. A poor widow’s savings for her coffin, a girl from a modest household’s dowry—countless such sums came from a single wheel. If that wheel became useless, there would indeed be weeping all along the road.

For this reason, people had long been coming to Hu Xueyan with petitions, asking him to intervene and restrain the mechanized mills. His power was immense. He regularly held ten thousand bales of silk worth three million taels of silver, enough to dominate the market. That was why Jardine Matheson had even persuaded Hart, a dignitary of the second rank, to negotiate terms. The terms were very good. “A commission on top of the market price” meant two sources of income. The market price already included the customary commission of two taels and five mace per bale, divided equally between the intermediary who introduced the foreign buyer and the silk warehouse. An additional commission would be at least another tael per bale. Here was a handsome profit for no effort, an opportunity others would have coveted; Hu could only let it go. The difficulty was that he could not disregard Hart’s feelings. At the very least, he must find a way to refuse without costing Hart face, so that the latter could still give Jardine Matheson an acceptable account of the meeting. After turning the matter over, he decided to play for time.

“Mr Lubin,” he replied calmly, “we Chinese have a saying: ‘In business, speak as a businessman.’ Terms as good as Jardine Matheson’s are more than I could hope for. But you must know what happened in Guangdong, where the silk-reeling machines were smashed. If I signed a contract with Jardine Matheson and disturbances followed, I should not be the only one to suffer. The whole locality would suffer too. Consider it: from our governor here in Zhejiang to the Grand Council and Zongli Yamen in the capital, would they not all blame me? You have spent many years in Beijing and know how formidable the censors are. Would they let me off?” His case seemed watertight. Then he abruptly changed course. “But”—he emphasized the word—“you are not merely speaking for Jardine Matheson. Your concern is the wealth and strength of China. We must find a way to make this succeed. Let me work out a suitable arrangement with everyone concerned. Provided there is no unrest, the market is not disrupted, and the families who have lived by raising silkworms and reeling silk retain a livelihood, I shall certainly do as you ask and contract with Jardine Matheson alone. As for the extra commission, out of regard for you I could never accept it.” It was a handsome speech. But Hart, famously shrewd and thoroughly versed in Chinese social dealings, reflected that a satisfactory plan meeting all three conditions—no unrest, no market disruption, and a living for the silkworm-raising families—might not be devised in ten years. The promise to contract exclusively with Jardine Matheson and the refusal of extra commission were thus generous words with nothing behind them. Nevertheless, he could appreciate Hu’s care. The thing was plainly impossible, or at least something Hu would not do at the cost of silencing his conscience and disregarding the consequences. The first half of his reply would have been sufficient. Everything after “but” was an additional mark of respect for his guest, and the intention deserved gratitude.

Hart nodded deeply. “You are a man who understands reason, Mr Xue—far more enlightened than those venerable gentlemen in Beijing. My journey has not been in vain.” “You are too kind,” Hu replied. “If everyone were as understanding as you, anything could be discussed.” A servant brought in another dish, briefly interrupting the conversation. It was Gu’s invention, called Fried Shrimp Cakes. At first sight it resembled fried flatfish; only on tasting it did one discover how different it was. Pounded shrimp was mixed with finely minced chicken breast, wrapped in tofu skin in rectangular parcels, coated in breadcrumbs, and fried. The method resembled Hangzhou’s fried “ringing bells,” but the ingredients were far more luxurious. Hart’s teeth were poor, and he particularly appreciated the dish. This supplied a lighter topic. He freely admitted that the pleasures of the table were a major reason for his reluctance to leave China. “Lord Dong often invites me to dinner,” he said longingly. “To my mind, his cooks are the finest in the world!”

Lord Dong was Dong Xun, Minister of Revenue, who managed affairs at the Zongli Yamen. A native of Yangzhou and an accomplished host, he employed two cooks who had worked in the households of that city’s Eight Great Salt Merchants. Both could prepare entire banquets made from mutton or from eel. In entertaining foreigners, Dong also adopted the salt merchants’ displays of cultivated taste: gatherings for pitch-pot or riddle guessing, sometimes even translating Homer and Byron into ancient-style or regulated Chinese verse. Hart, enamored of Chinese culture, felt a particular affinity with him. “Bai Letian wrote a poem while in your city of Hangzhou: ‘I cannot bring myself to leave Hangzhou; / Half of what holds me here is this lake.’ I should like to alter it: ‘I cannot bring myself to leave China; / Half of what holds me here is this—’” Hart was stuck. Scratching his graying hair, he repeated “this” for some time. Then his eyebrows suddenly rose. “Fare! ‘Half of what holds me here is this fare.’”

Hu felt secretly ashamed: he had no idea what Hart was talking about. Gu understood at least part of it and asked, “I hear that you and Lord Dong often exchange poems. A remarkable accomplishment!” “I could hardly claim to exchange poems with him. But we often discuss poetry and lyrics.” Hart continued, “My son has studied Chinese writing from childhood. Lord Dong recommended his teacher. He has now begun composing eight-legged essays, and he hopes eventually to gain a degree through the examinations. Lord Dong has promised to submit a memorial on his behalf. I do not know whether permission will be granted.” This Hu understood. A foreign child reading Chinese and writing eight-legged essays was strange enough; that he should actually hope to sit the examinations was unheard of. “It will certainly be granted,” Gu answered. “Such admiration for China on the part of both father and son is rare. His Majesty is sure to show exceptional favor.” “I only hope so.” Hart suddenly said, “That reminds me of something. Let me mention it now before I forget. Mr Xue, there is a matter in which I should like your help. Jardine Matheson sent someone to Huzhou to buy silk, and the deposits have been paid. Now we hear that when the new silk comes onto the market, the sellers do not intend to deliver. If that really happens, I fear there will be an open rupture.” Hu had heard something vaguely about the affair. It involved a Christian convert named Zhao, but he knew neither the details nor exactly who was concerned. He had, however, already weighed the significance of Hart’s words.

“Mr Lubin,” he asked, “tell me first exactly how you would like me to help Jardine Matheson, and I shall see what can be done.” “Your word carries enormous weight. Since Jardine Matheson has paid the deposits, I should like you to give instructions that delivery be made on time.” Hu reflected that Hart was exceedingly cunning. This might be a trap. If he agreed too readily, Hart could go back to Beijing and say that all the silkworm growers and silk producers acted solely on Hu’s word: when he said their silk could be sold, they sold it; when he said it could not, nobody dared sell. The Zongli Yamen might then order him, in the interests of friendly foreign relations, to ensure that Jardine Matheson could buy silk directly in the countryside. What a difficulty that would create! “I cannot possibly accept your description of my influence,” he replied. “Whether people sell their silk is their own affair. I have no right to interfere, and even if I did, they might not listen. But business must be conducted fairly. To accept a deposit and then refuse delivery is indefensible. Whatever the difficulty, at the very least the deposit must be returned. Since you have specially asked me, I must do all I can. Let us put it this way”—he paused to consider—“I hear the affair involves a man named Zhao who works at a church. I shall send Brother Yingchun down expressly to deal with it for you.” “I am most grateful.” Hart clasped his hands in thanks.

“May I ask,” Gu said, “whether Jardine Matheson could send someone to make contact with me?” “Ailike, the proprietor of Jardine Matheson, is here in Hangzhou,” Hart replied in English. “Are you not well acquainted?” “Yes, very well. I had heard he was here, but I did not know where to find him.” “Come to my place,” Dr Meitenggeng put in.

“Very well,” Gu replied. “I shall call at Guangji Hospital tomorrow morning.” When the guests had gone, Hu still had things to discuss with Gu. The drinking was over and the company dispersed. Most of the women of the Hu household were at Lingyin attending on the old lady, taking twenty or thirty maids with them. The resulting quiet was almost lonely. “A rare opportunity, a rare opportunity! Tonight we brothers can really speak from the heart. Yingchun, it is warm. Let us sit outside.” Outside meant the gallery in front of Jingjian Pavilion, built unusually broad to allow the scenery beyond to be reflected inside. The seats by the railing were in a corner to the west. The moon of the eleventh day of the third month was already large. Its clear light fell obliquely across the two men, giving their faces a faintly somber and withdrawn look. “Yingchun,” Hu said, “I have had a very strange thought these past few days. There is a saying: ‘A man surrounded by good fortune does not know how fortunate he is.’ I wonder whether it is true.” Gu could not answer, having no idea what had prompted this strange thought.

“My mother is always telling me to cherish my blessings. But how does one cherish them?” “Well…” Gu thought as he spoke. “I suppose it means not carrying things too far—not using up all one’s good fortune.” “Quite. But then one can scarcely speak of enjoying good fortune at all. Once that thought is in your mind, every sip of tea and every mouthful of rice makes you wonder whether you are going too far. What pleasure is there in a life like that?” Gu felt this was something of a sophistry, but he could not refute it. He could only ask, “What do you suggest, then, Uncle?” “I think it is precisely the man who does not know how fortunate he is who truly enjoys his good fortune.” “Do you mean one need not cherish one’s blessings?” “No, that is not what I mean. I mean that enjoying oneself is one thing and making money another. Do not confuse the two. To make money, one must use one’s brains; to enjoy oneself, one must stop thinking about how to make money.” Gu laughed. “Uncle, the more I hear, the less I understand.”

Hu smiled back. “The more I think about it, the less I understand it myself.” Then he abruptly changed direction. “Let us think of a way to make money—no, a way both to make money and enjoy ourselves.” Gu considered for a while, then laughed. “There is one way, but I doubt it can be done. Even if it could, I am afraid neither you nor I would agree to it.” “Tell me. What is it?” “‘To visit courtesans and have them pay you—there is no business like it under Heaven.’ That is a way to make money and enjoy yourself at once.” “Not necessarily!” Hu seemed about to say more, then stopped. “Enough. Let us talk about practical business. I rashly gave Hart my word today. You must make sure I do not lose face over it.” “That goes without saying. Once you have spoken, I must see it through. Fortunately, after today my duties here will be over. Tomorrow morning I shall see Ailike; in the afternoon I shall settle up with the people I brought. The day after tomorrow I can leave.” “Whom will you take with you?”

After a little thought, Gu said, “One clerk from the silk firm will be enough. If I need anyone else, the banks, pawnshops, and silk firms in Huzhou can all provide men. But there is one thing I must take.” “What is it?” “An official dispatch from the provincial treasurer’s office.” “Why?” Hu asked eagerly. “It must be addressed to the Huzhou prefecture and say something like this: reports have been received that a Christian convert in Huzhou, one Zhao, abuses his influence to bully others and acts in ways intolerable under the law. Gu is therefore being sent specially to investigate in secret, and the prefecture is to give him every assistance.” By “Gu,” he meant himself. He had purchased the title of expectant assistant prefect and paid further money at the Ministry of Personnel to be assigned to Zhejiang. He did not actually wish to hold office or take an official assignment. Such a title merely afforded many conveniences and could even bring opportunities for personal advantage. This was one occasion when it would prove useful.

“With the status of an investigator acting under the provincial authority’s orders, I can bargain with Zhao. If we cannot come to terms, it will still be time enough to present my credentials to the Huzhou prefecture.” “Not a bad plan,” Hu said. “Let us call on De Xiaofeng together tomorrow morning.” “I have already arranged to see Ailike in the morning. Could we call on Treasurer De in the afternoon?” “I am only afraid they may not get the dispatch ready that same day.” Hu added at once, “There is no harm in leaving a day later.” “Very well. Then we shall definitely leave the day after tomorrow.”

Hu changed the subject. “Yingchun, when you see Ailike tomorrow, ask for his accounts. Exactly how much has he advanced in deposits? To whom, and in what amounts? He must provide an itemized list of names.” “Well…” Gu hesitated. “He may not be able to do that. His accounts are at the firm.” “Never mind. He can prepare it when he returns to Shanghai. Tell him that once the list arrives, and we have checked that there are no fictitious entries, we shall pay the whole amount. He need not worry.” “Uncle,” Gu warned gravely, “that is dangerous.” “You mean there is a risk? If we do not bear it, who will?” Gu thought for a moment. “In that case, why not pay him the money first and make a handsome gesture of it?” “That is precisely my intention. And it is not merely a handsome gesture. I want him to see the difficulties and retreat. Besides, all his suppliers will then pass into my hands.” Gu laughed. “You are the shrewder man, Uncle. That never occurred to me.” At this point Ruixiang appeared, asking where the late supper should be served. “Bring it here,” Hu instructed. Gu had no appetite at all and was rather tired. He wanted to go to bed early, but felt that leaving now would slight Ruixiang’s attentiveness and disappoint her. So he stayed.

Once supper appeared, his appetite returned. There was only a very thin gruel of fragrant rice and six accompaniments. Apart from drunken crab, all were vegetable dishes such as cold dressed bamboo-shoot tips. After days of rich eating, he had been longing for something plain, and his sluggish appetite revived. Before serving the gruel, Ruixiang asked, “Would you like a glass of wine, Mr Gu?” “Gladly. I should like some brandy.” “We have some brandy in which Madam has steeped ginseng. I shall fetch it.” She filled two bowls of gruel first, then brought the medicinal liquor in a crystal decanter. The crystal glass she chose was right too: a large-bowled, short-stemmed glass designed for brandy. It reminded Gu of a dinner at home the month before for a wealthy French merchant whose family owned wine cellars. There were rules for everything: drinks before and after the meal, red wine with one dish and white with another. Gu possessed a full set of glasses, but the maidservants did not understand the rules and had not prepared them properly. Somehow the Seventh Lady learned of it and could only fret helplessly in bed. With Ruixiang there, she would not have to worry. Thinking this, Gu unconsciously looked up at Ruixiang with a smile. She was pouring the brandy and did not notice, but Hu, watching quietly, saw it all. “Xiangyin will be making an inspection tour in the fourth month,” he said. “He is certain to visit the arsenal in Shanghai, and naturally I must be there to wait for him. Yingchun, once Mother’s birthday is over, I think I shall have Sister Luo go ahead to see Sister Seven. Then I can take her place when I arrive. That way we shall attend to both ends. What do you think?”

“What could be better?” Gu replied. “Sister Luo must stay at my house on this visit.”
