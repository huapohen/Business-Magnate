HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Desolate Foreign Settlement

Chapter 2 — Part 2 of 6

“Of course, of course. She must stay with you. It would be inconvenient otherwise.” Gu felt that there was more behind Hu’s words, but could not guess what it was. The thought of Zuo Zongtang’s inspection visit to Shanghai, however, brought several other matters to mind. “What preparations should we make for Xiangyin’s arrival?” “Ah, I thought of that some time ago, but Mother’s birthday left no opportunity to discuss it. You know his two weaknesses: he loves outward marks of prestige, and he is always intent on bringing down Second Master Li. I thought we should first find out what sort of reception Li had when he came to Shanghai as Governor-General of Liangjiang. If we give Xiangyin a grander reception, he will be pleased.” “I remember that Li was appointed in the fourth year of Tongzhi. That was more than ten years ago, and things have changed. The Ever-Victorious Army was under his command, so naturally he was invited to review it. The foreign warships now stationed at Shanghai are there for their own countries’ purposes. They may not invite him aboard for an inspection.” “That reminds me of something. Warships can fire salutes. When his vessel reaches Gaochangmiao, let a dozen or more foreign warships on the Huangpu fire together. The sound will carry as far as Kunshan and Songjiang. That should give Xiangyin all the prestige he wants.” “It can be arranged. Foreigners are quite willing to confer that sort of empty honor. The Russian ships, however, may refuse.” Gu was thinking of Zuo’s hostile attitude toward Russia during the Ili affair. Hu thought circumstances had changed and the Russian commanders might no longer bear that old grudge.

“Yingchun, arrange it early and settle everything beforehand. Someone else might help smooth the way with the Russians. Are Russia and Germany not rather close?” “Very well. I shall look for a way.” “If there is time, it would be quite good for Sister Luo to travel with you.” As he said this, Hu noticed Ruixiang listening closely. He beckoned her over. “Ruixiang, Madam is going to Shanghai to see the Seventh Lady. You are to go with her.”

“Yes, Master.” “There is something else I want to ask. Madam is thinking of leaving you in Shanghai to help the Seventh Lady manage the house. Would you be willing?” “I would not presume to manage the house. The Seventh Lady already has someone to do that.” “Then what about caring for her during her illness?” “That would certainly be my duty. If you and Madam instruct me, of course I shall attend to her.” “We could hardly ask you to serve us,” Gu interposed. “But she lies ill in bed without anyone she can really talk to. She is bound to feel depressed, and that makes recovery more difficult. Let me thank you in advance.” He rose. “Please do not, please do not!” Ruixiang reached out to press his shoulder and keep him seated. Only after extending her hand did she remember that she must avoid any appearance of impropriety. Blushing, she stepped back twice and lowered her head. “Good! Then that is settled,” Hu said with a double meaning. “Yingchun, you can go to Huzhou with an easy mind.”

The Hu household owned twelve boats. The best were two official-style vessels, one large and one small. Gu’s party numbered only four, so they took the smaller, towed by a little steam launch, and reached Nanxun, north of Huzhou, that same day. This town on the southern shore of Lake Tai concentrated the wealth of the prosperous southeast. Less famous than Jingdezhen in Jiangxi or Foshan in Guangdong, it was richer than either, for it produced the finest silk in China, known as Qili silk. Gu knew Nanxun well enough. He had come once with Hu and twice on his own. This was his second visit within a year, but last time it had been late autumn, with red leaves on the tallow trees; now it was late spring, with grass growing high and orioles on the wing. They moored at the far end of the western market. From the stone landing, a towpath paved with blue stone led to a broad blind alley. Only at its end did they see, on the left, an imposing pair of black-lacquered doors with bronze rings. A plaque of polished brick inlaid with lettering above the lintel bore four seal-script characters: Lotus Pond Retreat.

“This is the place,” Gu told Lei Guiqing, who followed him. “Whenever I come alone, I stay here.” Finding a pull-ring on the door, he tugged it twice. A bell rang inside, and presently the door opened. The woman who answered was in her twenties. She wore a pale blue-green cotton monastic robe, but her hair hung long below her shoulders. On the boat, Gu had already told Lei how unusual this private Buddhist retreat was. Such foundations were generally established for the concubines of deceased officials or wealthy householders. Some of these women were younger than the heir and his wife. After the old master’s death, they could neither leave the family to make new lives nor endure its restrictions. The new master would often pay to build a private retreat, endow it with roughly a hundred mu of good farmland, and support the woman in a permanent vegetarian life of Buddhist devotion, practiced without shaving her hair. The woman presiding over Lotus Pond Retreat was different. She had once been a famous Suzhou courtesan with an establishment of her own. On two occasions she had become attached to patrons with whom marriage had already been discussed. One died of illness, the other met a violent death. Disillusioned with love, she built Lotus Pond Retreat and followed the Pure Land teaching of Master Lianchi to repent the karmic debts of her former life. Taking the religious name Wuxin, she became its mistress. Even in her worldly days, her generosity and skill in entertaining had been famous throughout Shantang. Religious retirement had not changed her nature. She still received male guests whose company she enjoyed and allowed them to stay at the retreat, but they must entertain no amorous designs. If a man failed to understand, she possessed all Wang Xifeng’s skill in dealing with Jia Rui: she could make him suffer a humiliation he dared neither complain of nor remedy.

Gu had met her once when she was still a courtesan. On his first visit to Nanxun, hearing people speak of her, he had made a special call. Cultivated and witty, with a rich store of miscellaneous knowledge, he could make something interesting of almost any subject. His consideration for others made him welcome wherever women entertained, and he was equally welcome in this retreat from the world. He and Wuxin got on extremely well. After that first visit, she had insisted that whenever he came to Nanxun, he must make her house his lodging. This time, however, he would have to disappoint her. “Xiaoyu,” he said to the woman at the door, “this is Third Master Lei.” “Please come in, Third Master Lei.” As she closed the door, Xiaoyu asked, “Mr Gu, why did you not write ahead?” “The business that brings me to Huzhou arose quite suddenly. Where is your teacher? And where is the little lapdog?” Wuxin had an exceptionally responsive little dog. Whenever it heard Gu’s voice—or even his footsteps—it would come waddling up, the gold bell at its neck jingling, to wag its tail and bark at him. Now there was neither sound nor movement from it, which puzzled him. “Madam Huang has invited my teacher over,” Xiaoyu replied. “She should be back soon. Please sit in her rooms.” Wuxin’s quarters occupied an open pavilion five bays wide, with a Buddhist shrine in the center. The two eastern bays had been joined into a reception room kept spotless. Xiaoyu ceremoniously showed them to their seats. Soon a girl of twelve or thirteen, dressed like Xiaoyu, came to serve tea.

“Is she a junior fellow disciple of yours?” Gu asked. “I did not see her last year.” “She came in the first month of this year.” Xiaoyu then called to her. “A’wen, this is Mr Gu, and this is Third Master Lei.” A’wen shyly greeted them, then addressed Xiaoyu. “Third Senior, Old Buddha Granny says Teacher will stay at the Huangs’ for the vegetarian meal before coming back. Granny is going home to see her grandson.” Gu knew the arrangements here and understood what she meant. Old Buddha Granny was an excellent vegetarian cook. If she was away, there would be no one to prepare the visitors’ meal. A’wen was tactfully alerting Xiaoyu. Before Xiaoyu could speak, Gu said, “We shall not eat here. There is plenty of food on the boat, and in this warm weather it would be a pity to let it spoil. Oh, and I cannot stay here this time. Third Master Lei and I shall sleep aboard.” Xiaoyu smiled. “Mr Gu, let us wait until my teacher returns before deciding all that.”

Gu nodded and asked about recent happenings at the retreat. Soon A’wen brought refreshments. Snacks at such private retreats were valued for their ingredients rather than their appearance. The square date-paste cakes looked unremarkable, but one bite revealed an incomparable fragrance and sweetness. On his first visit, Lei had intended to do no more than taste one politely. Instead he found himself eating three in succession. Satisfied, they were preparing to take their leave when Wuxin returned with graceful steps, surprise and pleasure lighting her face at the sight of them. Gu introduced Lei, and the usual courtesies followed. She looked thirty-five or thirty-six, with a quiet elegance. But an occasional turn of her eyes flashed like lightning with a peculiar, compelling power, and Lei felt his heart stir. When she spoke to Gu, he was astonished: she addressed him directly by his given name. “Yingchun, did you not say you would come in the second month? Why have you left it until now?”

“I meant to see you before going to Old Madam Hu’s birthday celebration. But once I reached Hangzhou, I could not get away.” “That is a strange story,” Wuxin replied. “I heard long ago that her birthday festivities would last seven days. Those seven days are not over yet. How have you managed to get away now?” “There is some urgent business to attend to. I wonder whether you have heard of someone: Zhao Baolu.” “Asking me about him is like asking a blind person for directions.” “From the way you say that, I think I have come to the right person.” Gu smiled. “You may follow different paths, but you are both well-known figures. You can hardly be unaware of him.” “What sort of ‘well-known figure’ am I? Do not talk nonsense, Yingchun. You will make Mr Lei think this Lotus Pond Retreat is still full of worldly passions.” “No, no!” Lei hastily protested. “I could never misunderstand it so.” “I am joking. And I should not be afraid even if you did misunderstand. Do not trouble yourself.” Wuxin turned back to Gu. “Why are you inquiring about Zhao Baolu?” “Someone has entrusted me with a matter of business.”

“It is a long story,” Gu said. “If you know the man, tell me what he is like.” “On his character, you had better not ask me.” She called outside. “Xiaoyu! Xiaoyu!” When Xiaoyu came, she said, “Tell them about your aunt’s conversion.” Xiaoyu was taken aback and did not know how to begin. Gu supplied an opening. “I am trying to find out about Zhao Baolu.” “Oh, that man who makes a living out of religion!” Xiaoyu said contemptuously. “Jesus this, Jesus that—and he could swindle a person to death without answering for it.” “Did he cheat your aunt?” “Yes. It is shameful to speak of—” Seeing her reluctance to go into details, Gu tactfully said, “There is no need to tell an embarrassing story. What I want to ask is this: has he paid deposits on a large quantity of silk?” “There are deposits, but they were never paid out.” “What do you mean?” “He said a Shanghai foreign firm had commissioned him to buy silk. The price was good, with a thirty-percent deposit. He had people sign receipts first and told them to come for the money next day.” Xiaoyu spoke indignantly. “When they went, he said the church needed repairing and urged them to contribute. After endless coaxing and pressure, the meek ones gave in. The bolder ones said, ‘No deposit, no silk. Let there be a lawsuit when the time comes.’ But he holds the written receipts. Who knows what will happen?”

“Then they should take it up with him at once. Delay only breeds trouble. Later he will have all the evidence on his side.” “Mr Gu, they have the silkworm babies to look after.” In fact, before she spoke, Gu had already realized that his question was ill judged. Around Lake Tai, the third and fourth months were known as the silkworm months. Every household pasted red paper on its doors, stopped visiting its neighbors, and observed numerous taboos. Raising silkworms was exhausting work. One lapse of attention could bring silkworm plague or another disease and destroy a family’s food and clothing for the whole year. Though they knew they ought to deal with the matter promptly, they could only put it aside for the time being.

“Yingchun,” Wuxin asked, “you must have some reason for inquiring into this?” “Of course. It is why I have come. He has been commissioned by Jardine Matheson in Shanghai to buy silk here, but he is spreading word that delivery may not be possible and that there may be a lawsuit—perhaps even a dispute involving the church. Foreign merchants who do honest business do not want such a dispute. They would rather recover their deposits and waive the interest. I have come to deal with it on Jardine Matheson’s behalf.” “It will be difficult. If he is preparing to make it a religious dispute and means to evade his obligations, you may find him too much for you.” “Surely he must listen to reason?” Wuxin considered for a while. “Go and try first. If you cannot reach an agreement, we shall see.” She seemed to have some means of helping, and Gu felt easier. “We shall go into the city first thing tomorrow,” he told Lei. “If the discussion goes well, excellent. If he will not behave reasonably, we shall come back and talk it over.” “Good,” Wuxin said. “Old Buddha Granny is away today. Tomorrow I shall have her prepare some good vegetarian dishes for Mr Lei.” Even so, the vegetarian dinner Xiaoyu prepared was delicate and flavorful. There was also home-brewed mixed-fruit wine, excellent in color and bouquet. Lei drank with contented enjoyment and grew increasingly cheerful. Afraid he might lose his composure in drink, Gu prevented him from having too much, finished the meal quickly, and took his leave to return to the boat.

Early next morning, just as they were about to cast off for the city, two small sedan chairs stopped before the gangplank. Two pale-faced young scholars stepped out. Only on looking closely did Gu recognize Wuxin and Xiaoyu. Since they had come in disguise, it was best not to greet them openly. Gu’s chief concern was that their boots, stuffed inside with cotton, made walking difficult. Crossing the swaying gangplank might be dangerous. He therefore helped the boatman steady the bamboo pole extended to the bank as a handrail, repeatedly cautioning them, “Slowly, slowly. Keep your footing!” Once teacher and disciple had come aboard with nervous steps and been shown into the cabin, Gu asked, “Are you going into the city too?” “Yes.” Wuxin’s eyes traveled around the cabin. “What a beautiful boat. It is a privilege to have a ride in it. Xiaoyu, draw the gauze curtains.” The windows had two layers of curtains, one of white patterned foreign gauze, the other of purple-red velvet. With the gauze drawn, the cabin remained bright, but people on the bank or in other boats could not see clearly inside.

Wuxin removed her blue satin skullcap, with its false queue attached at the back, and shook her head twice. The long hair concealed beneath it fell loose. Then she sat down, took off her boots, and gently massaged her toes. Her behavior had an air of free and unconventional charm. Gu found it unremarkable, but Lei, seeing it for the first time, could not help entertaining romantic thoughts. “Do you know why I am going into the city?” Wuxin asked. “I was just about to ask. Tell me where you want to go, and I shall have the boatman take you there first.” “I am not going anywhere. I shall wait for you aboard. If you settle things with Zhao Baolu, so much the better. If not, I shall find a friend to help you.” So she had come expressly to assist them. Lei admired her all the more as someone out of the ordinary. “Mistress Wuxin, such readiness to help from someone who has left the world is truly rare.”

“I can hardly count myself as having left it. And even for those who have, human feelings and the ways of the world remain the same.” “Yes, yes.” Lei pressed his palms together. “The Buddha is merciful!” There was something comical in his manner, and everyone laughed. When the laughter subsided, Gu asked, “What sort of friend will you find for us?” “That depends. I shall find whichever of my friends can be useful to you.” This surprised even Gu, to say nothing of Lei. Wuxin seemed to know people everywhere and possess extraordinary influence. Where had all these connections come from? Lei wondered the same thing but could hardly ask. Wuxin was frank enough. Reading their thoughts in their faces, she smiled. “You must be wondering how someone who does not come from Huzhou knows so many different people here. There is nothing remarkable about it. I know a good many wives, and we get on very well. Through them, I have come to know their husbands.” “Oh, that reminds me,” Gu said. “Yesterday you went to Madam Huang’s, did you not?” “Yes. Her husband may be able to help you. Mr Huang is—” His given name was Yi. An expectant magistrate, he had been assigned to collect taxes on bamboo and timber in Huzhou. When Zeng Guofan sent boys to study in America early in the Tongzhi reign, Huang had accompanied them as the officer responsible for their practical arrangements. He had spent six months in America and was considered well versed in foreign affairs. Whenever dealings with foreigners arose in Huzhou, both the prefect and the county magistrate would turn to him. He was a figure of considerable consequence in the city.
