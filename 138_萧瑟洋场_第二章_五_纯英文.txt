HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Desolate Foreign Settlement

Chapter 2 — Part 5 of 6

Zuo’s temporary headquarters were at Tianhou Temple. Shao Youlian, the Shanghai intendant, led the prefect of Songjiang and the subordinate county magistrates in paying formal respects. Next came the commissioner of the Jiang Customs and directors of the municipal councils, followed by the civil and military officials stationed in Shanghai. Shao then joined the intendants holding appointments there, including Hu Xueyan and Sheng Xuanhuai, in a banquet honoring the marquis and grand secretary. By the time it ended, the first night watch had begun. Hu and Gu naturally stayed until last. “Your Excellency has had a tiring day,” Hu said. “Please retire early. We shall return tomorrow to pay our respects.” “No, no!” Zuo waved a hand. “I inspect the arsenal tomorrow and return to Jiangning the day after. There are many things I must discuss with you. Do not hurry away.” Hu had offered only the customary polite words. Since Zuo was in good spirits and wished him to stay, he could ask for nothing better. He assented and sat down.

“After all that argument over land defense and maritime defense, in the end I have to attend to it myself. What tricks fortune plays!” Zuo threw back his head and laughed so loudly that the roof seemed to shake. Only Hu understood that the laughter was directed at Li Hongzhang. In the fifth month of the eleventh year of Tongzhi, Russia had taken advantage of the Muslim rebellion in Xinjiang to send troops into Ili. In the third month of the thirteenth year, Japan used the incident involving shipwrecked Ryukyuans as a pretext to invade Taiwan. Threats on land and at sea followed one another, provoking debate over which kind of defense should take priority. Its principal opponents were Zuo and Li. Zuo managed affairs in the northwest, Li commanded Beiyang; each had his own responsibilities and convictions. Considering the issue of great importance, the court ordered governors-general and governors throughout the provinces to state their views. Wang Wenshao, then Governor of Hunan, happened to be back in Hangzhou to visit his ancestral graves. Hu asked him whether he favored land or maritime defense.

“What do you think?” Wang countered. “As Governor of Hunan, you should naturally speak for a Hunan man.” “Quite. A man governing a province must not offend its powerful families. I have been unable to decide this matter. Your words have settled it. My friendship with Lord Li must be put aside for the moment.” Wang’s ties with Li were deep, but for the sake of governing Hunan smoothly, he decided to support land defense. In his memorial he wrote: “Defenses on both river and sea urgently require preparation. Yet a threat to the coast cannot arise without a cause; the crucial matter lies in the military situation on our western frontier. The Russians occupy our Ili and show every sign of holding it indefinitely. Each day our troops delay gives them another day’s advance. Nothing could be more urgent.” This memorial gave the land-defense argument the advantage. Soon afterward the Tongzhi Emperor died, and the dispute subsided temporarily. It revived in the first year of Guangxu, when Empress Dowager Cixi ordered princes, grand secretaries, and the heads of the Six Ministries and Nine Courts to confer on maritime defense. Li memorialized for an end to the western expedition; Zuo naturally opposed him. Finally, with Wenxiang’s support, Zuo was appointed imperial commissioner to direct military affairs in Xinjiang. Plainly, the maritime-defense argument had again lost ground.

In practice, however, the case for land defense had arisen from the Ili affair. After Zeng Jize’s mission to Russia settled the Sino-Russian dispute, it ceased to be raised. When Zuo completed his western campaign, he was summoned to the Grand Council, then soon appointed Governor-General of Liangjiang. Li revived the old question and this time won a complete victory. The court accepted his maritime-defense plan in full. Three measures were to come first: establishing a base at Yingkou to train a new naval force; raising funds to continue building steel-armored warships, with government loans due for repayment by China Merchants temporarily retained and diverted to the purchase of ironclads; and simultaneously building docks and forts at the important harbors of both Nanyang and Beiyang. Just as the work gathered momentum, Old Madam Li died in Hankou. Li Hongzhang returned home to observe mourning, and Zhang Shusheng, Governor-General of Liangguang, was transferred to act in Zhili. The coastal-defense preparations temporarily came to a halt.

“If Beiyang can manage maritime defense, why should Nanyang not do so? Most of the money comes from Liangjiang anyway, making it still more proper for Nanyang to take charge. I mean to begin with docks and forts. I have already sent for Lord Peng. The reason I must hurry back to Jiangning is that he is coming down the Yangtze on an inspection and should arrive within days. It would hardly be courteous for the host to be absent when his guest arrives.” Having said all this in one breath, Zuo suddenly called, “Xueyan!” “What are your instructions, Your Excellency?” “Is Focke in Shanghai?” “Yes. He was about to return home, but on hearing of your inspection he specially postponed his departure by one steamer. He will certainly call on you tomorrow.” “Will he come back after returning to Germany?” “Yes, indeed.” “Good. Then we can discuss the matter further and take advantage of his journey home to have him purchase any new and effective weapons.”

Hu was about to reply when a personal guard lifted the curtain and entered with a document folder. Without speaking, he approached Zuo and opened it. There was a sheet inside. Zuo read it and immediately passed it to Hu. It was the decoded text of a confidential telegram: “Shanghai office respectfully reports to Marquis Zuo: [confidential] Marshal Yuan to govern Liangguang; public appointment decree imminent.” The signature was the single character “Yun.” Hu knew the message came from Xu Yongyi. Marshal Yuan was, of course, Zeng Guoquan, whose courtesy name was Yuanfu. Hu smiled. “Liangguang is a good place. This time Ninth Marshal Zeng will not be like last year, when he insisted on leaving the governorship-general of Shaanxi and Gansu after less than six months because conditions were too harsh.” Zuo nodded and considered for a while. Raising his head, he said slowly, “Sending Zeng the Ninth to Liangguang shows that Zhang Zhenxian will not return there. He is to be confirmed in Zhili. Xueyan, I must use this opportunity for a thorough reorganization. Let Nanyang belong to Nanyang and Beiyang to Beiyang. We must remove Li Shaoquan’s invisible meddling hand.” “Yes, Your Excellency.” Hu reflected that Li’s influence in Nanyang was deeply rooted and would not be easy to eliminate. If it could be done, however, an entirely new situation would open before them. It was worth a great effort.

“I am going to inspect the arsenal tomorrow. You had better come with me and see what can be improved.” “Certainly. I shall attend you early tomorrow morning.” When they left the headquarters, it was only a little after nine, and Shanghai’s foreign settlement was at its liveliest. As they climbed into the carriage, Gu’s driver quietly said, “Master, your appointment with Seventh Miss is tonight.” “You remember it more clearly than I do. Did Seventh Miss specially ask you to remind me?” The driver merely grinned, cracked his whip, and drove south. “Who is Seventh Miss?” Hu asked.

“Seventh Miss Aiyuelou. She has just come from Suzhou.” “What does she look like?” “Presentable, that is all. But her skill in entertaining is first-rate.” “From the look of things, it is not only her entertaining that is first-rate.” Hu laughed. “She knows how to hold on to a client too.” “You will see for yourself, Uncle.” Soon the carriage stopped at Zhaorong Lane off Baoshan Street. Seventh Miss Aiyuelou’s establishment was the second house on the right. An attendant called, “Rear wing!” and a serving woman immediately came to receive them.

Hu and Gu waited in the courtyard. The woman’s hair was covered with red flowers, her face thick with white powder. Ugly and outlandish, she looked like a demoness. Yet when she spoke, her soft, delicate Wu dialect sounded like that of a girl of seventeen or eighteen: a true “beauty on the other side of the wall,” as Suzhou people called such women. “Oh, Mr Gu, why are you so late? Seventh Miss has been waiting and waiting!” On discovering Hu, she made a greater fuss still. “Oh, my goodness! What an occasion! How have we managed to invite the God of Wealth himself?” Her cries brought windows opening upstairs and several pretty faces peering down into the courtyard. One called loudly, “Mr Hu! Do you remember me? Fourth Miss Xiangyun! Come and sit with me later.” Hu had met so many women in his visits to the pleasure quarters that he could not remember this Fourth Miss Xiangyun. He merely answered, “Certainly! Certainly!” Following the serving woman upstairs, they found a woman of about twenty-four holding up the curtain at the rear room’s door, smiling as she waited. As they entered, Gu said, “Seventh Miss, I suppose you have never met Mr Hu?”

“Never met him? Of course I have.” She curtsied. “Mr Hu, you have grown more prosperous-looking.” “Oh? Where did we meet, Seventh Miss?” “At Shantang, near the end of the year before the year before last. Governor Le’s eldest son gave the dinner. You even called me over to entertain you. A great man has too much to remember, but I have always treasured it.” She came forward to undo Hu’s buttons and remove his riding jacket. The scent of her hair stirred his memory. Passing through Suzhou near the year’s end, he had been entertained by the eldest son of Le Fangqi, Governor of Jiangsu, whom he already knew well in Shanghai. The dinner had been given at a courtesan’s establishment at Tiger Hill. He vaguely remembered calling one of the women over during the banquet. Her face seemed familiar, but her name escaped him. It had certainly not consisted of three characters. “You were not called Aiyuelou then?” “I was called Xifang.”

“That explains it.” Hu smiled and asked politely, “Have the last few years treated you well?” “I came to Shanghai hoping for better things.” Aiyuelou glanced at Gu. “Since Mr Gu began supporting my establishment, things have gradually improved.” “And with the God of Wealth arriving so unexpectedly today, they will improve still more!” the serving woman put in. Hu and Gu were accustomed to such flattery and paid it little heed. Aiyuelou, however, found it grating. “Why do you talk so much?” she said, giving the woman a look that sent her out. Fruit trays had now been set out. Once the men were seated, Aiyuelou offered melon seeds and tea and continued the courtesies. “When did Mr Hu arrive?” “Two or three days ago,” Gu answered for him. “But this is his first evening out at a courtesan’s dinner.”

“Oh! And you came to me on your very first outing! What an honor. Thank you!” “Had I known you were old friends, I should have brought our Little Uncle yesterday.” “Why Little Uncle? Your surname Gu is only half of Hu. How can you be uncle and nephew?” “Excellent!” Hu laughed. “Yingchun, this is the first time I have heard that your surname is half a Hu.” Gu laughed too. Turning to the young attendants, he said, “You may all call me Mr Half-Hu from now on.” “That would be no fun!” Aiyuelou replied. “Half a pot of wine to drink, and not even one complete winning hand at mahjong! Would that not be a miserable fate?”

Hu liked her quick wit and ready tongue. Gu saw it and said, “Uncle, why do you not take over as host tonight?” They had visited the pleasure quarters together for many years, and Gu occasionally yielded his place in this way. Hu was considering whether to accept when Aiyuelou spoke. “That will not do! Mr Gu, how can you escape paying for tonight’s dinner? If everyone is enjoying himself afterward, we can move to the front room for another round. Would that suit you, Mr Hu?” “The front room? Fourth Miss Xiangyun’s?” Since she had already arranged their course, Hu, well accustomed to these social games, had no objection. He merely asked Gu, “Whom shall we invite?”

“Whom would you like to see?” Hu named four or five men known for making good company in the pleasure quarters. Gu’s brush flew as he wrote the invitations. Counting seven hosts and guests in all, he said, “Let us make Eight Immortals Crossing the Sea.” He wrote another invitation: “Urgently inviting Mr Sha Yixin, staying at Changfa Inn on Third Road, to join us.” After signing it, he added in smaller characters, “A distinguished guest awaits an introduction. Please do not decline.” By an odd chance, this specially invited man was prevented by illness from coming. An old acquaintance arrived instead. Lin Maoxian, whom both Hu and Gu knew, was a Ministry of War official newly appointed prefect in Fujian. Passing through Shanghai, he was staying at the same inn. On hearing that Gu was giving a courtesan’s dinner—an occasion one could customarily join without invitation—he relieved the tedium of travel by appearing unannounced.

“Excellent! Excellent!” Gu welcomed him warmly. Lin was entertaining, a brilliant talker with a store of anecdotes. A dinner in the pleasure quarters needed such a man to keep it from falling flat. The banquet was laid. Though called a “double table,” it was still only one round table. Aiyuelou approached Gu with a bundle of silver-mounted ivory chopsticks wrapped in a fresh white cloth. “Are all the guests here?” “One is still missing, but let us begin.” Hu now spoke. Though a courtesan’s house was no government office, seating still reflected rank and standing. He was unquestionably entitled to the place of honor, but before anyone could invite him to it, he declined. “Mr Maoxian cannot possibly refuse the first seat tonight—” “Mr Xue, Mr Xue!” “Hear me out. If my reasons are unsound, you may refute them.” Hu raised a hand to stop him. “First, you have come from afar. Second, you have something to celebrate. Third, apart from Yingchun and myself, everyone here meets you for the first time and ought to show you courtesy.” All agreed that his reasoning was sound. Lin clasped his hands. “Then forgive my presumption.” Once Aiyuelou had arranged the places, he sat first.

Hu naturally took the second seat. The others were old friends and did not distinguish among their places, leaving only the host’s seat for Gu. As soon as he sat down, a young attendant brought a black wooden tray holding brush, inkstone, and a stack of summons cards for the courtesans. “Mr Maoxian, whom shall you call?” “I know nothing of the place. I arrived only yesterday, and this is my first visit. Recommend someone.” “Call Fourth Miss Xiangyun,” Hu suggested. “I remember she has a ready tongue. She would suit Mr Maoxian’s conversation.” Gu considered briefly, wrote the name, and asked, “And for you, Uncle?” Hu knew so many women that he merely smiled. “Choose for me.”

Gu nodded. “I shall call two for you. Ninth Miss Haoyuan, and—” “No, wait. I remember. Let the other be Fifth Miss Jiaofeng.” “Why call her?” Gu frowned. “Never mind. I have something to discuss with her.” The summons cards were completed and sent out. First to arrive was Fourth Miss Xiangyun, close at hand in the front room. Moving on tiny bound feet, she leaned on the shoulder of a maid of thirteen or fourteen. “Which gentleman is Mr Lin?” she asked on entering. “Here!” Hu pointed. “This is Mr Lin from the capital, now an appointed prefect. Fourth Miss, I have specially arranged this introduction for you.” Xiangyun had already been displeased that Hu had not called her for himself. Discovering that he meant to hand her over to someone else made her angrier still. “Thank you!” she said rapidly, casting her almond-shaped eyes aside. Everyone could see that she was angry.

Hu had actually made the arrangement out of consideration for his guest. Lin patronized courtesans in Beijing but preferred the establishments called tea rooms. The small refined-singing houses of the Eight Great Alleys were like Shanghai’s changsan establishments, while tea rooms corresponded to the yao’er class. The former professed to sell conversation rather than sexual favors; a man had to spend a substantial amount before becoming an intimate guest. The latter were more direct. Even on his first visit, provided terms were agreed, he could have the candles extinguished and stay the night. Lin liked prompt gratification, which explained his preference for tea rooms. That was why Hu had recommended Xiangyun, notorious among the changsan courtesans for being accommodating in bed. Meeting Lin by chance on his travels was a rare pleasure, and Hu wanted to arrange an enjoyable night for him. Xiangyun might not understand his intention, so he had better explain. It should be done before Haoyuan and Jiaofeng arrived. After Xiangyun had made the usual round of toasts and polite remarks and finally reached Hu in the second seat, he smiled. “May I call you over for a private conversation?”

“As you please. What am I, after all? When you want me, one wave and I come. When you do not, you kick me away into the eastern ocean.” Hu smiled and turned to Lin. “Forgive me, Mr Maoxian. Fourth Miss and I have a misunderstanding over someone else’s affairs. Let me borrow her to clear it up, then return the jade intact to its rightful owner. Will that do?”
