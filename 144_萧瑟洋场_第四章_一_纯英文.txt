HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Desolate Foreign Settlement

Chapter 4 — Part 1 of 2

CHAPTER FOUR

This ought to have been good news, but Yuan Shikai had a guilty secret. There was nothing he could do except wait for the flaw to emerge and then find a remedy. At last Zhou Jialu, a member of Wu Changqing’s secretariat who handled correspondence and had also taught Yuan, summoned him for a question. “Weiting, are you a secretary in the Secretariat Office?” “What is the matter?” Yuan neither confirmed nor denied it, first seeking to learn what had happened. “Look at this.” It was an official communication from Beiyang concerning the Qing Army’s recommendations for rewards. Yuan Shikai, secretary in the Secretariat Office, had received imperial approval for promotion to deputy prefect. But after searching its records, the ministry could find no secretary of that name. He was to be instructed to submit an explanation. Yuan felt both delight and anxiety. His delight was that he had built a tower from level ground and obtained a fifth-rank deputy prefecture. The post carried military duties—hence its other designation, “sima”—but its holder was a civil official with unlimited prospects, making it more valuable than a second- or third-rank military command. His anxiety was that the secretaryship in his record was fraudulent. If that underlying appointment did not exist, his dream of becoming deputy prefect would collapse. Inwardly he was in turmoil; outwardly he remained calm. “Mr Zhou,” he said with a grin, “why not guess?” “There is no need. The ministry certificate for a purchased appointment that you brought originally had the right surname, Yuan, but the wrong personal name. You must have borrowed it.” “Yes, yes. Nothing escapes you, Mr Zhou.” Yuan made a formal bow. “Please help me through this.” “That goes without saying. If the truth were reported, even Commander Wu would be punished for failing to detect it. There are two ways now, one easy and one troublesome. You must choose.” “Please explain them.” “The easy one first: use the name on the certificate. It is not only easy but convenient. A single official note stating that Yuan Shikai is a name adopted by Yuan So-and-so, and that you are reverting to the original name, will suffice.” Yuan did not want this easy solution. He was already well known in Korea. More importantly, everyone at court, from Empress Dowager Cixi and Prince Gong to the secretaries of the Zongli Yamen, knew that a man called Yuan Shikai had distinguished himself there. With a different name, who would recognise a mere deputy prefect?

That, however, was not the reason he gave. “To be frank, Mr Zhou, the certificate belonged to a nephew of my clan who has died. Restoring his name could cause all sorts of unforeseen complications. Please tell me the difficult method.” “You must find someone to approach the Ministry of Personnel on your behalf. Its clerks have more tricks than one can imagine. They will certainly find a way. But ‘Keep the fire going, and the pig’s head softens; provide the money, and official business gets done.’ Your affair may cost a thousand taels.” “Yes, yes. I shall do as you advise.” “Good. I shall hold the papers back for now. Approach the ministry, and we shall decide what to do when we see the result.” “Thank you, thank you.” Zhou smiled. “Weiting, I have a bit of doggerel for you.” Taking up his brush, he wrote at one sweep on a sheet of official draft paper. Yuan read it: “A crooked Henan scholar was his start; / The borrowed secretaryship is plain. / Once he displayed his statesman’s art, / Seven severed heads became his gain.” Zhou roared with laughter. Yuan could only join in with a few dry chuckles to show how lightly he took it. Back in his tent, he naturally thought of Xu Shichang, a provincial graduate and a thoroughly reliable man. Entrusting the matter to him would ensure success. But if, as Zhou said, it would cost a thousand taels, where was the money to come from?

After much thought, Yuan could only turn to Zhang Jian. Zhang also managed the supply office and controlled the flow of provisions and pay. One nod from him would secure the thousand taels. Yuan greeted him with “Mr Zhang!” Zhang was taken aback. Yuan called Zhou “Mr Zhou”, but had always addressed Zhang simply as “Teacher”. Now he had changed it to “Mr”, and added the surname. Why? It was awkward to challenge him immediately, so Zhang replied coldly, “What can I do for you?” Yuan realised his mistake too, but would not correct himself. He explained his difficulty indirectly and asked Zhang to help him through it. “I would not presume to call it that. But as friends, we ought to help one another. The supply office holds public money, which I cannot lend privately. There is only one course: your official allowance is two hundred taels a month. Give me five signed acknowledgements, and I shall advance you five months.” “Good. How should I write them?” In a “sealed acknowledgement”, the word “acknowledgement” signified that a matter was concluded. The wording was not particularly pleasing, but ordinarily no one troubled about it. Yuan, however, was sensitive. When he took the form away and read it, he found it stated that he had received two hundred taels for a specified month, counted in his presence, with no deficiency in purity or weight, and that he would make no subsequent claim for compensation for any shortage. It seemed designed to prevent him from repudiating the receipt, and he was already displeased. Then he consulted the Expressions of the Six Ministries, a book he used as a guide to official life. Under Personnel was an entry for a “voluntary undertaking”: once a case was decided, or property ordered returned, the person concerned signed a document declaring willing obedience. Yuan flew into a rage. So a sealed acknowledgement was this sort of meek submission, merely replacing the signature with a seal! He felt that the supply office was treating him intolerably. Moreover, in the present recommendations Zhang had merely risen from assistant county magistrate to eligibility for a seventh-rank magistracy. Yuan was a deputy prefect, a “fifth-rank occupant of the yellow hall”. Why should he give a sealed acknowledgement to the supply office? He complained furiously, but without the documents he could not obtain the money and had to swallow his anger and comply. Zhang heard that he had been cursing behind his back—“What sort of thing is this supply office?”—and found his petty arrogance in success contemptible. Nevertheless, he continued to help him.

“Weiting, is this money to be used in Beijing?” “Yes.” “To whom will you send it?” “A childhood friend named Xu. By now he is probably a newly successful provincial graduate.” Zhang understood. His friend had sat the northern provincial examination, whose results were now out. He might have passed, but Yuan, far away in another country, had not received the news; hence “probably”. “Very well,” Zhang said. “I cannot give you silver to send, of course. A banknote might be lost in the post, which would cause trouble. I have a method, if you agree.” “Please explain, Mr Zhang.” “Mr Zhang” again! After hearing “Teacher” so often, this second use jarred. Still, Zhang patiently continued. “Our army’s pay is issued through the Beiyang Office in Tianjin. I shall give you an order to collect the money. Send it to your friend, and he can draw it there directly. Would that not be convenient?” “Excellent. Thank you for your trouble, Mr Zhang.” “What is your friend’s personal name? It will be safer to name the person authorised to collect it.” Yuan agreed. “Xu Shichang. The characters for shichang come from ‘May his descendants prosper for five generations’.” “Where is he from?” “Must that go on the order too?” “Not for its own sake. I must specify his status. To sit the northern provincial examination, he must be either an Imperial Academy student or a county licentiate. If the latter, I must state the county. That is why I ask.” “He is a licentiate. His family originally came from Ningbo in Zhejiang and moved to Tianjin in the Qianlong reign. His great-great-grandfather served as magistrate of Nanyang in Henan, died in office and was buried in Ji county. The family has lived there since. So he can be considered a Zhejiang man, a Zhili man or a Henan man.” “Then his licentiate registration must still be in Tianjin. If he had qualified in Ji county, he would have had to sit the Henan examination.”

Zhang issued the order, naming “Xu Shichang, formerly a licentiate of Tianjin” as the recipient. By the time it reached Xu, he had indeed become a provincial graduate. He and his younger brother Xu Shiguang had taken the examination together. After its three sessions, during the month’s wait for the results, his eagerness for a degree and anxiety over success or failure robbed his food of flavour and his sleep of ease. A close friend advised him to seek a divination lot. The friend was Ke Shaoban, courtesy name Fengsheng, from Jiaozhou in Shandong. “The lots at the shrine of Immortal Lü in Liulichang are extraordinarily accurate,” he said. “Every question receives an answer, and every answer proves true. Why not try?” Xu readily agreed. At the shrine he found the worship even more fervent than at the Fire God Temple to its west, which strengthened his faith. He prayed sincerely and drew a lot bearing this verse: “The mystic work has laid its base; / Feed the fire well, make no mistake. / When twelvefold towers yield their inner space, / The elixir forms, and cranes awake.” “It seems my work is not yet complete,” Xu said. “Perhaps there is no hope in this examination.” “Not so,” Ke replied. “I think it refers to the spring examination. The second line, ‘Feed the fire well, make no mistake’, means that after success in the autumn, you must study harder and practise more full examination papers. Next year you will pass the metropolitan examination and gain one of the top three places in the palace examination. Would that not be breaking through the ‘twelvefold towers’?” Xu was delighted by this interpretation. Then he read the pronouncement beneath the poem: “Bring glory to those before and abundance to those after; make the family flourish greatly.” His happiness increased still further.

On the day the list appeared, he waited from midnight until dawn and from dawn until noon. At last a messenger arrived with good news—but it left Xu Shichang acutely embarrassed. His younger brother, Xu Shiguang, had passed in ninety-fifth place. Rewards were paid and the provincial hostel bustled for a while. Once it grew quiet, Shichang could not help shedding miserable tears. “Elder Brother, I thought your examination essays better than mine,” Shiguang consoled him. “You must be among the top five. It is too early for that news.” When the provincial results were posted, the sealed names were opened and written out one by one, a process lasting from the previous midnight until the following evening. By custom, the list began with sixth place. The top five, the “Leaders of the Five Classics”, were disclosed last, by which time it was evening. When those scripts were opened, every servant and labourer in the examination compound held a red candle, making the place bright as day. This was called “making a stir over the list”. Dusk had not yet come, which was why Shiguang said the news was still to arrive. “News!” There was another commotion outside. Shichang listened intently. “Your household’s eldest young master, Xu Shichang, has passed the Shuntian provincial examination of the Renwu year in one hundred and forty-fifth place!” Could it be true? Of course it was. He might misread the gold-printed announcement, but the messenger, nicknamed “Three Firsts in Succession”, was demanding his reward. Even twenty taels did not satisfy him. “The eldest young master and the second young master—double happiness at your door! At least a hundred taels is called for.” Surely this could not be false. After bargaining, they finally sent him away with forty. That was only the first announcement; second and third messengers followed, costing a few more taels. The hostel was lively that night. With both Xu brothers bringing honour to the family, congratulations flowed especially thickly into their courtyard. Not until after midnight was it quiet. Though physically exhausted, Shichang was too excited to feel sleepy.

“Younger Brother, how accurate it was!” Shichang suddenly sprang up and shouted, giving Shiguang a fright. “What thing was accurate?” “You must not call it a ‘thing’. I mean Immortal Lü’s lot. Look!” He pointed to the words. “‘Glory to those before’—Guang, glory, plainly says that you would rank before me!” “Ah!” Shiguang thought there was something in it. “True. The Immortal indicated that I would share in Elder Brother’s glory.” “But do not be too pleased with yourself. Your future achievements will not equal mine.” As the elder brother spoke in that tone, Shiguang could only remain silent. “What? Do you not believe me?” “It is not that. One can readily imagine that my achievements will be less than yours. But we were discussing the lot. You must have found some meaning in it. Until you explained, it was not for me to speak.” “Of course! It reveals the secret. Look: ‘Make the family flourish greatly.’ Chang, flourish—that means I, Xu Shichang, will bring glory to our ancestors.” Shiguang had no answer beyond repeated agreement. “Only Elder Brother can make our Xu family flourish.” Shichang grew solemn. “Tomorrow we shall bow at the shrine: first to thank the Immortal for his guidance, and then to ask his advice on our next steps.” Shiguang obeyed. The next day they returned and each drew a lot about what to do. Shiguang’s advised him to return home to study and come again for the metropolitan examination in spring. Shichang’s read: “What gain in going from your door? / More than in sitting home once more. / Though no stair reaches Heaven’s height, / Each step will raise you towards the light.” “You go home, Younger Brother. I cannot. The lot is clear: going out is better than staying home. I should study in Beijing.”

Shiguang naturally accepted his decision and went home alone. Shichang found a tutoring place in the household of Zhang Zhiwan, Minister of War. The two families were old friends. Zhang invited him to study alongside his son, Zhang Ruiyin, and help with correspondence, lodging him in the rear courtyard. It was spacious. After setting up a bedroom and study, Xu still had rooms to spare and considered installing a shrine to Immortal Lü. Before he had quite decided, he dreamed that the Immortal said, “If you truly wish to offer me incense, you must keep it secret. When my wanderings tire me and I need a brief rest, I shall come and use your place as a quiet retreat from the world. I must not be disturbed.” Usually he remembered a dream upon waking, then soon forgot it entirely. This one remained perfectly clear even as he washed the next morning. He regarded such a visitation as momentous and was determined to follow the divine instructions. Doing everything himself, he chose the most secluded room and quietly installed a shrine. Its door stayed shut all day. At night, only after the courtyard gate had been closed and bolted would he open the secret room, burn incense and worship. He also obtained a set of “Immortal Lü’s Divination Lots”, so that he could seek guidance whenever in doubt.

When Yuan’s letter arrived, he naturally drew a lot to ask whether the matter could be managed. The answer was not only yes, but that he must act quickly: delay would make him too late. He immediately asked Zhang Ruiyin whether he knew anyone at the Ministry of Personnel. “What is the matter?” “A friend, Yuan Weiting, has a difficulty.” Xu explained it in detail. “That belongs to the ministry’s Civil Appointments Department,” Zhang said. “For such business, a clerk is more useful than an officer.” “Exactly. Yuan stressed the same thing in his letter. When I asked whether you knew anyone, I meant whether you had a sufficiently friendly connection among the clerks.” “How could a family such as ours be on friendly terms with petty clerks? Let me ask Old Niu at the gate.” Realising his blunder, Xu blushed. “Yes, yes. I misspoke. Please ask Old Niu for me.”

They summoned the gatekeeper. “We know a man called He,” he said. “He has considerable influence in Civil Appointments. But I do not know whether he is in Beijing.” “Why? Where else would a ministry clerk be?” “Old He comes from Puzhou in Shanxi. Some time ago I heard he intended to take leave and visit his ancestral graves. I do not know whether he has gone.” “Go there at once,” Zhang Ruiyin said. “Find out whether he is still here. If he is, tell him about the matter.” Xu explained everything to Old Niu and asked him to negotiate. There was a commission to be earned, so the gatekeeper eagerly went straight to Clerk He. That evening he returned. “A close thing! One step later and we would have missed him. His luggage was already on the cart, and he leaves first thing tomorrow.” “Oh? Did you speak to him?” “Yes.” “Is there a solution?” “How could they lack solutions?” Old Niu laughed. “The only question is whether we can agree on the price.” “What price has he named?” Zhang asked.
