HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Desolate Foreign Settlement

Chapter 4 — Part 2 of 2

“Clerk He says the movements into and out of office make this a serious matter. If Mr Yuan’s secretaryship cannot be traced to its proper origin, promotion to deputy prefect is out of the question. There will also be an investigation into the discrepancy in his record. Then it will no longer concern the Ministry of Personnel.” “Which ministry would it concern?” Xu asked. “Punishments.” “Good heavens!” Xu was alarmed. “He could be prosecuted?” “That is what they say. We need not believe every word, but Mr Yuan has this weakness and must ask their help. We can only listen to an extravagant demand.” “How much?” “Two thousand.” Exactly twice the available sum. Looking troubled, Xu handed Yuan’s letter to Zhang Ruiyin. After reading it, Zhang asked, “Old Niu, did he tell you how he means to do it?” “Would he tell me, Young Master? I pressed him for ages. All he would say was, ‘The matter is not difficult, but even if I told you, you could not do it.’” “Very well. Bargain with him. Fifteen hundred at the most. Mr Xu’s friend is not an outsider.” This hinted that Old Niu must not inflate the price for his own benefit. “Yes. With your instructions, Young Master, I shall do my utmost. But I must negotiate with him tonight. He leaves at first light.”

After negotiations that night, they settled at twelve hundred taels: half in advance and half when the business was complete. The next day Xu went to Tianjin, collected the thousand taels from the Beiyang Office, deposited them at Fukangfu Bank and first obtained a note for six hundred, which he handed to Zhang. Zhang was thorough. He summoned Old Niu. “You had better arrange a meeting with Clerk He, so that everyone can speak plainly face to face. If anything goes wrong, Mr Xu must have an account to give Mr Yuan.” “Yes.” Old Niu arranged for them to meet at a restaurant. Ministry clerks occupied a low social position. Though He dressed more elegantly than a rich young dandy, he addressed Zhang as “Young Master” and Xu as “Mr Xu”, standing while he spoke and observing every courtesy. When the banknote was handed over, he placed it on the table, bowed and said, “To explain to you both: the task itself is no more than lifting a hand, but it must pass thirteen gates. Failure at any one of them will stop it. I shall keep the note for now without cashing it until the work is done.” “Very well. Thank you for your trouble,” Zhang said. “How should Mr Xu reply to Mr Yuan?” “Tell him that the name under which the secretaryship was purchased was genuine. As a descendant of a military family, he subsequently volunteered for service and adopted the name Shikai. Let Beiyang send that explanation to Personnel. Once the change of name is approved, his promotion is assured.” The device was simple once explained. Without connections smoothed at the ministry, however, a change of name could destroy an entire career. If the clerks wished to obstruct it, any excuse could delay the matter for six months or a year. After one barrier was passed, another would arise. Until enough money had been spent, no appointment warrant—the document commonly called a ministry certificate—would be issued. While the change remained unresolved, the man could neither be assigned a post nor take one up. Waiting alone was a substantial loss. Consequently, when a name was wrong in examination results or Personnel’s appointments, many willingly endured it and continued under the error. A fraudulent “name change” as smooth as Yuan’s was rare indeed.

Even so, the exchange of official documents took half a year. By then the situation had changed again: Li Hongzhang’s return to his former post was settled. After Zhang Shusheng and his son inadvertently offended Zhang Peilun, Li realised that Zhang Shusheng seemed inclined to borrow Beiyang indefinitely without returning it. He seized the opportunity, cultivated Zhang Peilun and worked through Li Hongzao to secure a decision that both men should return to their substantive posts. But less than a year had passed since his mother’s death, and the Korean disturbance had been quelled. With no further need for force, there was no justification for summoning him from mourning to military service. Fortunately, foreign affairs offered many difficult matters. On the pretext that he was needed to negotiate with the foreign ministers, Li was retained, awaiting a suitable moment for the formal order restoring him. Travelling from his Hefei home to Beijing, he spent many days in Shanghai and gained a strong impression of Zuo’s intention to expel his influence from Liangjiang. Their views on foreign and military affairs also differed sharply. Over the dispute with France in Vietnam, Li believed that “their demands cannot be satisfied, while our military funds cannot bear the strain”. A peaceful settlement was necessary; force must not be used. Zuo, by contrast, advocated supporting Liu Yongfu’s Black Flag Army in helping Vietnam resist France. He supplied arms to Cen Yuying, governor-general of Yunnan and Guizhou, for onward delivery to Liu, and formally wrote the Zongli Yamen that “the choice between war and peace can be settled in a word”. At present, he argued, “not only do many Western countries consider France in the wrong; they foresee that discord with China must leave the affair without a conclusion and bring irretrievable regret”. France itself feared the great risks of war with China, but, having mounted the tiger, could only bluff. If China wavered, it would fall into the trap. His assessment was that war accorded with justice, suited the circumstances and conformed to opinion both in China and abroad. Because he kept arguing thus, the court specially ordered Li to Guangdong to supervise Vietnamese affairs. This was nominal: Li need not actually go there, since he could negotiate peace with the French minister Bourée in Shanghai or Tianjin. But if Zuo continued urging armed intervention, Li might eventually have to command a war against France from Guangdong—a prospect that threatened to ruin him. The Zongli Yamen was cautious and would not readily accept Zuo’s argument from “justice”. Nevertheless, his advocacy made Li’s negotiations with Bourée more difficult.

Britain, too, gave Li headaches. British merchants wanted to connect their submarine telegraph cable at Wusong to Shanghai by a land line; Zuo firmly refused. They sought a reduction in the cocoon levy; he opposed that as well. Most troublesome was his proposal to raise the likin on imported opium and native-grown opium. He regarded opium’s harm as limitless and proposed using taxation as a means of suppression. After imperial approval, he and Li negotiated with the British minister, Thomas Wade. Wade proposed increasing the import duty while leaving inland transit levies unchanged. Zuo agreed in principle, but China demanded a hundred and fifty taels per chest and Britain offered only eighty. The gap was too wide for agreement. After Zuo took a provincial appointment, Li continued the negotiations. Wade, recalled to Britain, hoped to conclude the matter on British terms and return home with enhanced standing. Li wished to help, but Zuo refused to compromise and opposed the terms as governor-general of Liangjiang, producing a deadlock.

Their greatest conflict of policy concerned the navy. Li had originally advocated maritime defence, and greatly admired Zhang Peilun’s proposal for a special Naval Yamen and a modern fleet. But after reaching Liangjiang and inspecting the coast and Yangtze, Zuo changed his earlier preference for land defence. He invited Peng Yulin, whose career had begun in the naval forces, to discuss modern warship construction, and work was already under way. Having founded the Fujian shipbuilding establishment, Zuo could not be dismissed as an amateur. It was therefore foreseeable that he would never permit Beiyang sole authority over the future modern navy. In a dispute between land and sea defence, defeating his argument would have removed the difficulty. Now that he too favoured maritime defence, they would compete to carry out the same enterprise. Li could not tolerate sharing authority or being unable to act as he wished. Whether he considered the present or the future, Zuo was a thorn in his eye that must be removed. The first step was to strip away his support. Li’s advisers agreed, and thought Hu Xueyan should be the first target. Shanghai Circuit Intendant Shao Youlian accordingly made a secret plan with Sheng Xuanhuai and others: they would place Hu in difficulty over repayment of a foreign loan.

Two loans handled by Hu remained outstanding. One was merchant funding contracted in the eighth month of the fourth year of Guangxu, with Chinese and foreign portions totalling six and a half million taels. Why had merchant funding been used rather than a foreign loan? There was a story behind it. In Guangxu’s third year, Hu had arranged a five-million-tael loan from HSBC, to be advanced and repaid in actual silver. Interest was one and a quarter per cent a month, with principal and interest repaid in fourteen instalments over seven years. Each instalment fell six months apart. As before, repayment was secured by bills issued by the Zhejiang, Guangdong, Shanghai and Jianghan customs. A supplementary condition stipulated that if the borrower abandoned the transaction after agreement, failing to supply the customs bills within three months, Hu would forfeit a hundred and fifty thousand taels. HSBC would incur the same penalty if it failed to deliver the silver within three months. Intervention by Shen Baozhen, governor-general of Liangjiang, caused repeated complications and long delay. The western campaign was then going well. Liu Jintang had advanced south from Urumqi and sent a force to join Shaanxi provincial commander Zhang Yao in an attack that recovered Turfan at one stroke. Bai Yanhu, one of the Muslim rebel leaders, fled west in haste. Liu reached Toksun at the western end of the Turfan basin, and more than twenty thousand Muslim followers surrendered. But the leadership passed through Hoxud and Karashahr, emerged from Iron Gate Pass and established contact with the Russian military at Korla. Meanwhile, shortages of provisions and pay prevented the expeditionary army from advancing west, to Zuo’s intense anxiety. At last the foreign loan was negotiated in the fifth month. Even the prospect was like imagining plums to quench thirst: army morale revived, and the supply offices in Shanghai, Hubei and Shaanxi found it easier to borrow from merchants for immediate needs.

Complications arose during the formalities. A loan from the British-owned HSBC ordinarily required the Zongli Yamen to notify the British minister of its terms. The minister then instructed the inspector-general of customs and the Shanghai consul to inform HSBC accordingly. If the loan could not be recovered, Britain could then raise the matter with China. In effect, the note was a Chinese government guarantee. HSBC therefore drafted it and gave it to Hu, who passed it through Zuo with a request that the Zongli Yamen act. Yet the bank’s draft said that interest must not exceed one per cent. Why, then, had Zuo’s memorial specified one and a quarter per cent a month? Robert Hart, the inspector-general of customs whose role was crucial, declared the loan could not proceed. An investigation was inevitable. On the basis of Hu’s explanation, Zuo memorialised that HSBC’s interest was indeed only one per cent. But the loan was reckoned in shillings and paid in silver dollars. Those dollars, commonly called “worn foreign silver”, circulated in the southeastern provinces but not in the northwest. The funds therefore still had to be obtained in actual silver measured in taels. The shilling exchange rate varied with London quotations, even between morning and evening. If it rose at the time of conversion, Hu would suffer a loss. He had therefore arranged with the German Tailai Trading House to guarantee the shilling conversion. This involved substantial risk, for which its additional quarter of a percentage point in interest was not excessive. Zuo insisted that the arrangement had been consistent throughout and merely had not previously been explained clearly. He strongly asserted that Hu had no ulterior motive in borrowing abroad. Plainly, he was taking responsibility and standing firm; the court could hardly refuse to respect his seniority. Inwardly, however, he felt badly about it. His trust in Hu diminished, though he could not do without him.

Hu naturally wished to make amends, and devised the merchant-loan method. At the end of that year, Guangxu’s third, Zuo wrote in reply: “This year our finances have been exceptionally strained. Had the prince at the Grand Council not pressed urgently for provincial assistance and arranged funds from the ministry, the whole undertaking would have been beyond saving. Complications arose at every turn in the foreign loan. Without your painstaking ingenuity and tireless support, nothing could have been done. Even now, the year-end payroll depends on the swift arrival of its first advance. After settling the Hubei debt, we must allocate money for famine relief in Shaanxi and the stricken people of Gansu. Of the remaining loan, after repaying the Shanghai office’s borrowings, enough will last only until the turn of summer and autumn next year. What is to be done after that, I do not know. You say that military affairs give comfort but finance gives grave concern, and preparations must begin a year ahead. That is a thoroughly practical and sound judgement. What I wished to discuss, you have already considered for me. Without putting yourself in my position and examining the whole situation, you could not have said a word of it. Across ten thousand li, we are of one mind without needing explanation.” Hu had indeed calculated it all: the year-end payroll, famine relief after drought in Shaanxi and Gansu, and repayment of the Hubei and Shanghai loans would leave another gap around the following summer and autumn. Borrowing for military finance required a year’s preparation. Yet another foreign loan was out of the question. Such loans were national obligations. Though the provinces were to provide the funds, successive disasters had impoverished them, and arrears in their contributions to the western campaign meant that the government would still have to find repayment. Further foreign borrowing could not even be proposed. Moreover, Zuo resented being subjected to Hart’s obstruction and was reluctant to borrow abroad, particularly from British firms. Hu recalled his words: “The interest and charges are too heavy. With merchant money, what Chu loses Chu gains, and that might be tolerable. But foreigners make great profits from us and still find fault at every turn. It is galling.” He had also said repeatedly, “Why must the customs and provinces issue bills, as though they were repaying Shaanxi and Gansu’s debts? The repayments come from contributions those provinces are already owed. I command the western campaign and have full authority to arrange its funds. Borrowing when assistance is late is simply managing cash flow. If merchants trust Shaanxi and Gansu and trust me, bills from those provinces should suffice. Why trouble the Zongli Yamen?”

Hu therefore devised a merchant-loan scheme. After repeated discussion, he undertook to assemble one million seven hundred and fifty thousand taels in merchant subscriptions, with HSBC “subscribing” the same amount. The combined three and a half million would be lent through a company called Qiantai. Zuo calculated that over seven years Shaanxi and Gansu would receive more than eighteen million one hundred thousand taels in provincial assistance. Even after repaying the foreign loan, at least ten million would remain, so a merchant loan of several million could certainly be discharged. But remittances arrived at uncertain times and in varying amounts. He therefore requested flexible terms: first, no customs bills; second, no fixed maturity, so that repayment could be early or late; third, pay when money was available and owe it temporarily when it was not, with interest continuing but no fixed interval between instalments. This was wishful thinking. Hu could secure only the absence of customs bills. The term was fixed at six years, instalments remained half-yearly, and interest was one and a fifth per cent a month. Merchant borrowing also required imperial approval. Zuo memorialised on the sixteenth day of the eighth month of Guangxu’s fourth year. A month later he received the court’s reply: “Merchant loans carry heavy interest. After annual allocations from the provincial and customs revenues for principal and interest, funds for the capital and other military requirements cannot be raised. Such borrowing is a last resort. In consideration of Zuo Zongtang’s undertakings, now approaching completion, the present proposal is approved. Hereafter, whatever the urgency, merchant loans at interest must not be resorted to lightly, imposing burdens on the future.” “Capital funds” covered the expenses of government in Beijing: civil and military salaries, pay for the Eight Banner soldiers, and the silver delivered to the palace three times a year for the private expenses of the two empress dowagers and emperor. It was the most important budget item. To say that even these funds could not be raised because of Zuo’s repeated borrowing was a severe rebuke.

Not until the previous year, when Zuo was summoned to Beijing, had he borrowed again. Obliged to arrange finance for Liu Jintang’s settlement of the western regions, he acted almost on his own authority and took a four-million-tael loan raised by HSBC through subscriptions. The risks on both loans rested entirely on Hu. Repayment of the three-and-a-half-million merchant loan had begun in Guangxu’s fifth year, and nearly half had been paid. Each instalment of principal and interest came to only a hundred thousand taels or so. Shao knew that would not trouble Hu; to obstruct him, he must use the loan contracted in the seventh year. Those funds were actually received in the fourth month of that year, at an annual rate stated as “nine li, nine hao and five”. For the first two years only interest was payable. From the third year, a million taels of principal fell due annually, in two instalments: the first in the fourth month of Guangxu’s ninth year and the second in the tenth, five hundred thousand taels each. Hu had handled earlier foreign loans without having to worry about repayment. The customs bills bore the provincial chiefs’ seals and, once collected at Shanghai Customs, were countersigned by the commissioner, who assumed joint liability. If provincial bills could not be honoured, he could withhold customs revenue to pay them. But with the last four-million-tael loan, Zuo had acted almost independently to free Liu Jintang of financial anxiety. Assuming that future provincial remittances would easily cover it, he had broadly promised that principal would “be repaid on schedule through the Shanghai Transport Office; if Shanghai has no funds, the lender shall be permitted to obtain payment on schedule from the Ministry of Revenue”. He feared that the Zongli Yamen and Li might prompt Hart to obstruct the loan, and accepted concessions to secure it. The promise quietly placed the entire risk on Hu’s shoulders. If the provinces failed to remit, HSBC would certainly approach him. There was no need for the trouble of asking the British minister to negotiate with Revenue. With his resources, credit and willingness to shoulder responsibility, Hu would surely meet each five-hundred-thousand-tael principal instalment.

Even so, half a million taels was no small sum. Shao Youlian and Sheng Xuanhuai secretly agreed to “give him a squeeze” when the time came. They might not bring him down, but could at least damage his credit. That spring, Guangxu’s ninth year, relations with France deteriorated again. In the eleventh month of the previous year, Li and the French minister had reached a three-point agreement in Shanghai for a peaceful settlement of the Vietnamese dispute. But the French naval and colonial authorities separately told their foreign ministry that they objected to Bourée’s agreement. The navy had already sent reinforcements to Haiphong in northern Vietnam. A political change in France then brought a new cabinet. Its foreign minister, Challemel-Lacour, supported the military position, repudiated the agreement, recalled Bourée and sent Tricou as special envoy to negotiate in China. Curiously, Bourée himself travelled from Shanghai to Tianjin to see Li and urged him to uphold their earlier agreement, even accusing the French government of breaking its word. With a French minister opposing his own government, Li felt confident of peace once more. He therefore followed his original plan and requested leave to return home for his mother’s burial. He was still unwilling to resume his former office, but had accepted the Beiyang commission in order to begin construction of the naval harbour at Lüshun. During his leave, Zhang Shusheng would naturally act in that capacity.

But in the second month, while Li was at his ancestral home in Hefei, French forces resumed hostilities in Vietnam. They not only captured Nam Dinh but directly infringed Chinese interests. A China Merchants ship carrying rice was detained at Haiphong, and its warehouses at Haiphong and Thuan An were occupied; the grain and other property within them were confiscated. Besides formally seeking aid through the Ministry of Rites, the Vietnamese government specially sent its “Minister of Punishments”, Fan Shen, to China, emulating Shen Baoxu’s weeping appeal at the court of Qin. The court was shaken. Moral criticism rose, and all called for a firm response. Even Zeng Jize, the first-rank Marquis Yiyong and minister to Britain and France, telegraphed to urge military aid to Vietnam and no concessions to France. Many provincial chiefs also favoured war. Cen Yuying had long prepared for it, but Zeng Guoquan, acting governor-general of Liangguang, would not lightly begin hostilities, to the critics’ great displeasure. Prince Gong, directing the Zongli Yamen, protested diplomatically to France while obtaining approval to order Li back at once to his substantive post as governor-general of Zhili. Another decree then sent him, in that capacity, urgently to Guangdong to supervise Vietnamese affairs, placing the defence forces of Guangdong, Guangxi and Yunnan under his command. Zuo was simultaneously ordered to prepare troops in the lower Yangtze region for possible dispatch south. Hu happened to be in Jiangning and said to him, “There ought also to be a decree returning Governor-General Zhang to his substantive post in Liangguang. Otherwise, when Li reaches Tianjin, will there not be two governors-general of Zhili?” “The interesting point is precisely that no decree sends Zhang Zhenxuan back,” Zuo replied. “The Zongli Yamen knows Li will never go to Guangdong, and probably will not return to Tianjin either.” “Please enlighten us, Your Excellency.” “Out of respect for the late Lord Wenzheng Zeng, Li has always treated Zeng the Ninth very considerately. During the siege of Jiangning, Zeng’s weary army had made no progress. Li had already recovered Changzhou and was ordered to help, but kept his troops where they were rather than share Zeng’s glory. Think: if he goes to Guangdong now, what is to become of Zeng?” “Yes, yes.” Hu considered it. “And you say he will not go to Tianjin because his arrival would send Zhang back to Guangdong, again displacing Marshal Zeng?” “Exactly.”

“Then the capital’s order restoring Li, with no instructions about Zhang or Zeng, means he is to hold the title for the moment without actually resuming the post.” “Correct. You can infer the rest from one point. You understand.” “What will he do, then?” Zuo pondered for a long time. “What do you think?” “I think he will still come to Shanghai.” Zuo nodded. “I too think he can only stay in Shanghai first. Besides, he cannot quite relinquish it.” Hu answered at once, “I had intended to take leave of Your Excellency, return to Hangzhou and go to Shanghai afterwards. In these circumstances, it seems better to go straight to Shanghai.” The provincial and customs contributions assigned to Shaanxi and Gansu for repayment of principal all passed through Shanghai Customs. Only by going there could Hu discover what had actually been remitted. “Good. Make that your first business in Shanghai and write at once with the position. We must know which remittances are missing so that we can press for them early.” Hu’s estimate proved correct. Li petitioned to remain temporarily in Shanghai, coordinating affairs, assessing the military situation in north and south, and deciding his next move accordingly. His meaning was that if the lower Yangtze forces could not spare troops for the south, war need not follow; peace would be preferable. Prince Gong understood and obtained approval. At the end of the third month, Li arrived in Shanghai by a specially arranged steamer and established headquarters at Tianhou Temple.
