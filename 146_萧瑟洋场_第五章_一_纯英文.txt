HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Desolate Foreign Settlement

Chapter 5 — Part 1 of 3

CHAPTER FIVE

The sight of Gu Yingchun gave Hu Xueyan a shock. He had grown so thin that his whole appearance had changed. “Yingchun, what—what has happened to you?” Gu sighed deeply. “Little Uncle, my luck has been terrible. I was careless too.” “What happened? Tell me at once.” “I am going to lose everything. All because I listened to Xu Yuzhi.” Xu was a wealthy Guangdong merchant whom Hu also knew well. His personal name was Run. Both capable and fortunate, he had learnt business in a Shanghai foreign firm, winning the foreigners’ high regard. From the age of twenty-two, he had joined partners to establish banks, silk firms and tea warehouses, profiting handsomely in every venture. By the second year of Tongzhi, when he was twenty-six, he had accumulated a hundred thousand taels or more. Through the Jiangnan military supply office he purchased the rank of an assistant departmental director, adding a peacock feather, and became a prominent gentleman of the Shanghai foreign settlement. In Tongzhi’s tenth year, he consequently received a commission. Zeng Guofan, governor-general of Liangjiang and Nanyang minister, had decided to send young boys abroad to study. Preliminary enquiries suggested selecting them in Guangdong. Most Chinese residents in America came from that province, whose people were receptive to new ideas. Fathers and elder brothers would not be unduly alarmed at sending young relatives across ten thousand li of ocean; meanwhile, the boys could be visited in America by older countrymen speaking familiar dialects, easing their homesickness. As a leader of Shanghai’s Guangdong merchants, Xu received the task. He planned carefully, choosing a hundred and twenty promising boys to travel in four groups of thirty. The first sailed early in the seventh month of Tongzhi’s eleventh year under Yung Wing. Most were from Guangdong, especially Xiangshan, Xu’s own county.

There were only five boys from elsewhere: two from Jiangsu, one from Shandong, one from Fujian, and one whose family came from Huizhou, though he had been recruited in Guangdong. The last was twelve, born in the year of the Xinyou coup, and named Zhan Tianyou. His father, Zhan Zuoping, worked as a machinist at the Fujian shipbuilding establishment, while the family lived in Guangzhou. When Tianyou applied, someone advised his father to let him study law so that he could return and become an official. Zhan insisted that his son learn technical skills, and the newest ones at that. The second group sailed in the fifth month of Tongzhi’s twelfth year, led by Huang Pingfu, whose family was linked to Xu’s by marriage. Besides the thirty government-supported boys, seven more from Guangdong went at their parents’ expense. Their families asked Huang to take them to America: attitudes had indeed changed, and privately funded overseas study had begun. The third group left in the eighth month of Tongzhi’s thirteenth year. This time, apart from two boys studying technical crafts and one studying machinery, all attended ordinary schools: the older boys at middle schools, the younger at elementary schools. Even the older ones were only thirteen, including Tang Shaoyi of Xiangshan and Zhu Baokui of Changzhou, Jiangsu. The youngest were at least ten. The fourth group departed in the ninth month of Guangxu’s first year. Ten additional places brought the total to forty. All would attend ordinary schools and choose a specialisation after completing middle school, according to their aptitudes. More boys came from other provinces, though these were still confined to Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Anhui. Zeng had initiated the scheme but did not live to see the first departure, dying in office in the second month of Tongzhi’s eleventh year. Li Hongzhang subsequently supported it. Xu thereby gained Li’s appreciation and received a Beiyang appointment as associate director of China Merchants, working alongside Sheng Xuanhuai.

During those seven or eight years, Xu’s enterprises had flourished. He remained far below Hu in wealth, but was a substantial Shanghai merchant. Apart from charitable undertakings, Hu had no business dealings with him. Gu, however, came originally from Guangdong, often encountered him when dealing with foreign firms, and had become close to him. They shared several ventures, including property speculation. Xu held more than two thousand nine hundred mu of land, fifty-one foreign-style houses and at least two thousand commercial premises. Their book value exceeded two million two hundred thousand taels, but too much capital was tied up. Vacant land produced nothing and still incurred taxes, while rents from the premises were limited. A British friend named Gulin, a knight keen on sport, widely connected and knowledgeable about business, proposed a partnership. Gulin also knew Gu, so Xu invited him to their discussions. “Let us form a large company and invest capital to build on all the empty land,” Gulin said. “Build one group of houses and sell it, then use the proceeds for the next. Once the vacant land is used, rebuild the old houses and keep renewing them. That is how great foreign cities, especially American ones, have grown.” Xu understood the cycle of building and selling. “But it needs a great deal of ready cash. Can you invest?” “Of course. Otherwise I would not propose a partnership. But I too must return home to raise subscriptions. Once we agree on the terms and circulate the prospectus in London, I believe we can raise the capital in less than three months.” “How much capital?” “That depends on you. Value your property—at its real value, of course—and I shall raise an equal sum.” Xu nodded and asked Gu’s view. “It is workable and fair. But I think our side should hold a larger share.” After consideration, Xu proposed definite terms: capital of four million taels, fifty-five per cent Chinese and forty-five per cent British. The Chinese side would contribute property at an agreed real valuation, and the British side remit one million eight hundred thousand taels in cash.

A lawyer drew up a preliminary agreement, which they signed. Xu also gave Gulin ten thousand taels to go home and raise subscriptions. But his property’s true value was only one and a half million. Another seven hundred thousand taels’ worth of land had to be bought to make up the Chinese share of two million two hundred thousand. “Brother Yingchun, good friends should share the profits. Will you subscribe that seven hundred thousand?” After examining his resources, Gu agreed to contribute five hundred thousand. This was at the end of the previous year. By the second month of the present one, enough land had been purchased—but disaster overtook Gulin. Soon after returning to London, he attended a royal hunting party. His horse stumbled and threw him headfirst, inflicting a grave brain injury. Two eminent physicians saved his life, but he developed epilepsy. The great company project quietly died. Xu and Gu were hit hard. In the Vietnamese dispute, France had repudiated the settlement and prepared for war, sending Admiral Courbet east with a fleet. Its legislature also voted five million francs for military expenditure. Rumours swept Shanghai. The most widespread said that French warships were continually cruising outside Wusong and had decided to attack the arsenal. Timid people were already fleeing. In this atmosphere of panic, no one would even enquire about property.

“Of my five hundred thousand taels, three hundred and fifty thousand were borrowed. Money is now desperately tight. All thirty-odd Shanghai banks are trembling, afraid of another panic. If everyone withdraws their deposits, any number may collapse together. My lenders are pressing hard—very hard. Their clerks come and sit at my door every day, demanding payment. I can only keep pleading with them.” Gu added, “And I am afraid A’qi will hear and worry, so I must constantly hide it from her. Little Uncle, imagine the life I am leading.” Hearing this and seeing his haggard face, Hu felt a sharp pang of pity. “Do not worry, Yingchun!” He struck his chest. “I shall settle it for you. Leave everything to me.” Gu hesitated without answering. Hu was surprised. Surely he should be delighted to have someone shoulder the whole burden? Why look so troubled? “What other difficulty is there? With our friendship, what can you not tell me?” “Little Uncle,” Gu paused, “do you really know nothing of the Shanghai market?” “What do you mean? Markets rise and fall. That is ordinary enough.” Gu stared, then at last spoke. “It seems you truly do not know. Let me tell you plainly: the man pressing hardest for repayment is Old Mi.” Hu’s face burned as though he had been slapped. His thoughts tumbled, and he could not speak. Old Mi was Mi Benchang, the managing clerk of his own Fukang Bank. “Pressing our own people so hard! Outrageous!” Hu was angry. Then another thought came: if even a friend was being pursued so urgently, Fukang itself must be in serious difficulty. He broke into a sweat, steadied himself and said, “You know that for years I have left Fukang to Mi and seldom asked about it. From what you say, its funds seem tighter than anyone’s. Tell me exactly what is happening.”

“You have come from Jiangning, Little Uncle. Did His Excellency Zuo not discuss the Shanghai market with you?” “What? Is the market—” “It has never been worse. Do you know how much ready silver there is in Shanghai now?” “How much?” Gu raised one finger. “Ten million?” “One million.” Hu was shocked. “Really?” “Ask Mi and you will see.” Hu still found it hard to believe. “If the market is so bad, someone should tell His Excellency Zuo. When we discussed Shanghai in Jiangning, he said that French military threats had affected trade somewhat, but it was nothing serious.” Gu gave a cold snort. “Which official nowadays does not deceive those above and bully those below? They all want to be magpies bringing good news; none will be a crow.” “Come. Let us go to Jixian Lane together.” Fukang stood in Jixian Lane in the British settlement, just across a road from Hu’s residence. He usually walked there at leisure. As they were leaving, the housekeeper, Mrs Chen, hurried out. “When will you be back, sir?”

“I do not know yet.” “Mr Mi has just sent an apprentice to say he knows you have arrived and will come at dinnertime.” She added, “We have found a very fresh shad today, which is rare. Do come back for dinner.” Hearing that Mi was coming, Hu hesitated. Gu said, “Then perhaps we should wait for him. Some things are more easily discussed at home.” Hu took his striking watch from his gauze waistcoat pocket. It was already half past four. “Then send word asking him to come early.” They returned to the drawing room for a private discussion. Hu’s chief concern was the first five-hundred-thousand-tael principal payment to HSBC. The sealed notes issued by the governor-general’s office in Shaanxi and Gansu were little more than a formality. Repayment came from provincial contributions collected through the Shanghai circuit intendant. Hu could cover a shortfall, but with money so tight he might be unable to do so. He must plan early. “Yingchun, the HSBC payment is due at the end of the month. Do you know how much Shao Xiaocun has received?” “Ten days or so ago I heard that half had arrived. More should have come in since. The shortage should be no more than a million or so. But there is only a million in ready silver in all Shanghai.” Gu drew a breath. “This may be troublesome too.” Hu’s heart sank. “My credit cannot suffer the slightest blemish. There is only half a month left. Have you any good idea?” “Not at the moment. Let us wait for Mi.” Mi did not arrive until dark. He said he had meant to come immediately upon receiving the message, but several large customers were withdrawing deposits. Arranging the funds had delayed him.

Hu knew what those arrangements involved. First a banknote was issued and the customer asked where he wished to collect the silver; then the paying branch was notified. Suppose he wanted fifty thousand taels in Jiangning. The fastest means would be used to alert Fukang there. If that branch lacked funds, they would look for another with a surplus. Shanghai Fukang was the head office and kept records of deposits and withdrawals at every branch. Once the position was clear, remittances through other banks could use one branch’s surplus to cover another’s shortage. But that was the recent practice. Earlier, customers asking for ready silver had been paid it. Hu asked where all that silver had gone. “It has dispersed inland,” Mi said. “Deposits are up at branches away from major navigable-waterway ports. By my reckoning, sixty or seventy per cent of the money has moved elsewhere; the other thirty or forty per cent is being kept at home. That will return to circulation only when the market calms.” Hu thought for a long time. “We must find a way to bring that thirty or forty per cent back sooner. Give me a list of the places where deposits have increased.” “As I said, everywhere except the major ports on navigable waterways.”

“People fear that French warships will reach those ports if war begins. What about Beijing?” “Deposits have risen greatly, and in large amounts. Grand Secretary Wen withdrew all his three hundred thousand here, but deposited four hundred and sixty thousand in Beijing.” Wen was Wenyu, Minister of Punishments, promoted to assistant grand secretary two years earlier. Withdrawing three hundred thousand but depositing four hundred and sixty thousand showed complete confidence in Fukang, and Hu was deeply reassured. “No wonder everyone wants to be an official. He has been in Beijing only three or four years and has accumulated another hundred and sixty thousand.” “No,” Mi corrected him. “A hundred thousand of it belongs to a member of his clan.” “Never mind. It all comes through him.” Hu asked, “With dozens of banks and only a million taels of ready silver, how is everyone managing?” “Entirely by working together and juggling the clearing accounts.” “Oh?” Since gaining Zuo’s patronage, Hu had managed the western expedition’s supply office and founded many enterprises. His commercial interest had centred on silk, while banking was left almost wholly to Mi. He had little knowledge of changes in its practices and did not quite understand what “juggling” the clearing accounts meant. Once he might have been content with a vague idea. Now that a crisis had arisen, he must understand it fully.

“Put simply, it is like friends who play cards together regularly. However large the stakes, they use counters. I lose and he wins today; you win and he loses tomorrow. It sounds lively, but all we do is count the counters and make entries, settling the balance at the proper time. It works only among regular friends. Let an outsider join, win a large sum and demand cash, and the trick cannot continue. So—” Thus Fukang had taken the lead in joining with a dozen or so of Shanghai’s major banks to establish a clearing association. Following the model of the bill-exchange institution founded in Japan in the twelfth year of Meiji, they exchanged notes instead of transferring silver. The exchange could not be conducted privately; everything had to go through the association. Participating banks first sent all maturing notes there. These were sorted, and senior clerks took them to the issuing banks for verification. If correct, a bank accepted its notes and issued a receipt called a “public clearing certificate”. These used five hundred taels as the basic unit. Smaller amounts were either made up in cash or entered separately for later settlement. By three or four in the afternoon, the certificates had generally all reached the association. A few calculations on the abacus showed what each bank should receive or pay. Suppose Fukang held a million taels in notes due from other banks, while only eight hundred and forty thousand of its own notes were presented: it had a surplus balance of a hundred and sixty thousand.
