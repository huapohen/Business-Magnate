HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Desolate Foreign Settlement

Chapter 7 — Part 1 of 6

CHAPTER SEVEN

The contracts were drafted. But a cocoon firm could not open without approval from the Ministry of Revenue, and Mi used this as a pretext to suggest waiting for the ministry licence before signing. Hu and Gu had no suspicion of his malicious intentions. To them the contracts were merely a formality: once the mill purchase was agreed, the money would be available whenever required. They therefore consented. With his Shanghai business concluded, Hu hurried back to Hangzhou through the oppressive summer heat. A marriage had been proposed for his third daughter, and his mother had accepted on her behalf: she was to marry the only son of “Wang the Philanthropist.” Wang, whose personal name was Caisheng, had been Hu’s friend for many years. In their youth both had been shop assistants, the men Hangzhou people jokingly called “counter monkeys,” though in different trades. Wang had begun as an apprentice in a large sauce-making establishment. When Hu renewed his acquaintance with Wang Youling and rose rapidly in the world, Wang Caisheng remained an assistant. After the Taiping rebellion was suppressed, however, he reappeared in the guise of a gentleman. Some said he had enriched himself by looting during the disorder; others that he had unearthed gold, silver, and jewels buried by the rebels. Whatever the source of his fortune, one fact was certain: at Hu’s invitation he helped with reconstruction, tending the wounded, saving the dying, and aiding displaced people. These good works earned him the title “Philanthropist.” In the second year after Hangzhou’s recovery, a son was born to him, and people called it a reward for his charity.

That was the yichou year, the fourth of Tongzhi. The boy, nicknamed A’niu, was now nineteen. Wang had long wanted a marriage connection with Hu, but Hu thought A’niu dull—indeed, as stupid as the ox suggested by his name—and had consistently refused. Unexpectedly, the young man had now passed into the county school as a licentiate, and the proposal was renewed. The matchmaker argued that his honest, kindly nature would ensure that Hu’s daughter was never mistreated. As an only son’s wife, she would also be cherished by her parents-in-law. Wang’s wealth fell far short of Hu’s, but his reputation for benevolence made up the difference; the families could be considered suitable equals. The sole deficiency had been A’niu’s lack of a degree. Now a licentiate, he had a promising future: as the saying went, “A licentiate is the seedling of a chief minister.” It was an excellent match. The argument was reasonable. Hu’s mother particularly valued the fact that, with no brothers-in-law’s wives in the household, her granddaughter would suffer no friction from sisters-in-law. She therefore accepted, merely writing to inform Hu and urge him home: the formal betrothal ceremony was set for the seventh day of the seventh month. On returning, he learned that the wedding too had been fixed, for the fifth day of the eleventh month. Wang’s aged mother was frail and might die at any time; she longed to see her grandson’s bride enter the household. If she died first, the wedding would have to wait three years, an excessive delay. The reasoning was entirely proper, and Hu’s mother agreed. Fortunately the dowry had long been prepared. Only some fashionable foreign goods remained to be added.

The scale of the celebration, however, awaited Hu’s return. Four people had to decide: Hu, his mother, his wife, and his concubine, Madam Luosi. All four had conflicting inclinations. The three women favoured restraint, yet Hu’s mother loved this youngest granddaughter best and feared slighting her. His wife wanted equal treatment: the girl should enjoy the same splendour as her two elder sisters. Madam Luosi thought of her own daughter. A restrained wedding now would set a precedent limiting the display when her daughter married. Hu naturally preferred the grandest possible occasion, but feared criticism in a depressed market. Two days of discussion produced no decision. At last he concluded, “The occasion must be respectable. The difference between a large and small celebration is limited anyway. There are still four months; we shall see how matters stand.” “The celebration is for other people to look at,” Madam Luosi observed. “The dowry brings real benefit. Give Third Miss a splendid dowry, and a smaller celebration will do. Publicly, we can explain that business is poor; privately, she will not feel slighted. Even the bridegroom’s family can have no objection.” This considered every side. Both Hu’s mother and his wife were pleased, while Hu felt that a magnificent dowry would preserve his dignity even without a lavish ceremony.

“Sister Luo is right. We must not stint the dowry. But the additions will have to be bought ready-made.” “Then someone must go to Shanghai,” Hu’s wife said after her mother-in-law, looking at Madam Luosi. Madam Luosi wanted to volunteer, but reconsidered and remained silent. There were too many tongues in the household. However hard she tried, someone would talk behind her back, perhaps alleging that she begrudged the expense because Third Miss was not her own daughter. Hu had expected her to respond. When she did not, he decided for her. “You know Shanghai. Go there, and visit the Seventh Lady while you are about it.” “Of course I should go for Third Miss’s wedding. But expensive jewellery is not necessarily pleasing. Naturally I would choose costly pieces, yet their designs might not suit her taste.” She smiled. “Why not let me take her and have her choose for herself at the foreign firms and jewellers?” “Quite improper!” the Old Madam exclaimed in broad Hangzhou speech. “An unmarried girl choosing her own dowry! People would laugh themselves sick if they heard of it.” “You go yourself,” Hu repeated.

Madam Luosi still would not commit herself. “I wonder whether Third Miss might enjoy a visit,” she murmured, as if to herself. “There is no need,” Hu’s wife said. “Surely you know what jewellery she likes.” Finally the Old Madam settled the matter: Madam Luosi would go alone. She would, of course, first consult the girl’s preferences and Hu’s wife’s wishes. Most important was a spending limit, which only Hu could set. “The dowry has already cost over a hundred thousand taels. Spend no more than another fifty thousand on additions. Money is tight in Shanghai, so goods must be cheap. Fifty thousand should buy at least seventy thousand’s worth.”

In Shanghai, Gu accompanied her to the German firm Biefa. Their first inquiries showed that the opposite was true. Domestic products were indeed being discounted for ready cash, but imported goods had risen in price. “The situation grows more tense every day, Mr Gu,” the manager explained. “France has a new premier, Jules Ferry, a very determined man. If China refuses to yield over Vietnam, he means to go to war. Ever since the foreign newspapers reported Admiral Courbet’s arrival in Vietnam, goods at every foreign firm have risen by ten to fifteen per cent. Some things cannot now be bought at any price.” “Why?” Madam Luosi asked. “If war begins, French warships will close China’s ports and foreign merchant ships will be unable to enter. Once supplies stop, sellers can ask whatever they please. The question will be whether an article is available, not whether it is dear. Every item sold now is one less for later, so everyone is putting the best goods aside.” “No wonder!” She pointed at the jewellery in the glass cabinets. “There is nothing here I like.” “Your taste is naturally exceptional, Madam Hu. We would not risk offending an old customer. Tell me what you require, and I shall open the safe for you.” Madam Luosi knew that foreigners in China, whatever their nationality, acted together. They too had agreements among traders. One firm would be much like another, so the maxim about comparing three shops would be little use. Better to negotiate as a major customer.

“To be frank, I am buying a dowry for our third daughter. If we agree, I shall give you the entire order—several tens of thousands of taels. Otherwise, Biefa is hardly Shanghai’s only large foreign firm.” The prospect demanded attention. “Third Miss’s dowry must be treated seriously. Please come inside. If you have a list, let me take it and assemble the goods for you to examine.” She had prepared a list but would not reveal her hand. “No list. If the goods are good and the prices reasonable, I shall buy more. First show me two pairs of diamond bracelets.” Chinese and Western fashions differed. To Chinese customers, diamond bracelets were the most precious jewellery. The request confirmed that she really was buying a dowry and that the order might indeed run to tens of thousands. The manager became still more attentive. He escorted her and Gu to the foreign director’s private sitting room and specially brought in a foreign saleswoman who spoke Chinese. Her name was Aidun; Madam Luosi called her “Miss Ai.”

“Where are you from, Miss Ai?” “I was born in Edinburgh,” she replied while preparing tea with milk. Madam Luosi did not know the place, so Gu explained that she was British. “Then your country is like ours,” Madam Luosi said. “Both are ruled by an old empress dowager.” Aidun’s Chinese covered everyday conversation only. Talk of an “empress dowager ruling” left her as bewildered as the name Edinburgh had left Madam Luosi. Gu had to explain again. “She means your Queen Victoria and our Empress Dowager Cixi.” Aidun was surprised. She had served many Chinese women—courtesans, and occasionally ladies of rank or respectable young women—but none had mentioned the British queen. She warmed to Madam Luosi and served her afternoon tea with particular care. The manager then brought three oblong boxes, all of black leather with gold lettering. Inside the first, a pair of brilliantly sparkling platinum bracelets set with diamonds rested on blue velvet. The workmanship was exquisite.

On closer inspection, however, the box looked new while the metal’s colour seemed otherwise. “These are used?” she asked. “Yes. They were favourite jewels of Napoleon’s empress.” “I care nothing for empresses. New things are better for a dowry.” “Both these other pairs are new.” One was set entirely with diamonds; the other combined diamonds with rubies and sapphires. The all-diamond pair was the more valuable. Each bracelet held four diamonds the size of soybeans, linked by smaller stones, and glittered even when held still. For splendour, however, the coloured stones excelled. “What are the prices?” “Thirty-five thousand for these; thirty-two thousand for the pair with coloured stones. I advise the all-diamond pair, Madam Hu. It costs three thousand more but is better value.” Unable to decide, she asked Aidun to put them on.

Aidun wore one kind on each wrist and held out her arms for inspection. After a long look, Madam Luosi turned to Gu. “What do you think, Brother-in-law?” “The all-diamond pair is better, certainly, but rather too plain in colour.” This exactly matched her own view, and she decided at once. “For a bride, with the Old Madam present, the effect should not be too subdued.” She turned to the manager. “I have chosen. Now we must discuss the price, or choosing is pointless. When did these bracelets arrive?” “More than a year ago.” “What price did you mark them at then?” “Thirty thousand.” “I do not believe it. You have added only two thousand—less than ten per cent.” “It is true.” He drew a label from beneath the velvet lining. “Mr Gu, please look.” The Arabic numerals did indeed read thirty thousand. Madam Luosi could read them too and responded quickly. “You yourself said the all-diamond pair was better value, so this one must be poor value. You must have marked an excessive price originally and felt too guilty to raise it by even ten per cent. Is that not so?” The manager smiled ruefully. “You are formidable, Madam Hu. I have no answer.”

She smiled. “However reasonable our arguments, too much disputing spoils goodwill. Let us do this: I shall certainly buy the bracelets from you. Show me the other goods first, and we shall settle their price last.” He agreed. They examined crystal plates and bowls, perfumes, and all manner of ingenious ornaments. Hoping to obtain a good price for the gem-set bracelets, the manager priced these other articles especially fairly. Once her choices were complete, they returned to the bracelets. “These goods total twelve thousand,” she said. “My husband told me not to spend more than forty thousand on additions to the dowry. What do you say? No bargaining now—yes or no.” “Madam Hu, how can I withstand such a crushing stroke?” “Business cannot be forced. If we cannot agree on the bracelets, I must look elsewhere. The twelve thousand is settled; I shall draw you a draft for that now.” Stunned, the manager signalled Aidun to serve more tea and refreshments, then quietly drew Gu aside. “I truly admire her methods,” he said with a bitter smile. “To accommodate her, I offered those later goods at cost. Intendant Sheng offered three thousand for one of the large crystal chandeliers, and we refused; I have charged her only two thousand five hundred. If she does not buy the bracelets, this transaction will cost me my job.” “She is willing to buy. You are unwilling to sell.”

“It is not that I am unwilling. The price is wrong.” “Profit is secondary here. Even without it, you gain. Consider the value of people saying that Biefa supplied the splendid dowry when Hu, the God of Wealth, married off his daughter.” “That reputation is precisely why I have been so accommodating. But I really cannot conclude the business at forty thousand.” “Would you lose money?” “Not exactly. But future prices—” “The future is the future; this is now. Frankly, trade is poor, and wealthy people are already fleeing the danger. You may not see another order like this later.” After a long silence, the manager said, “If I accept, I shall sacrifice my entire bonus.” Gu knew how foreign firms worked: salaries were modest, and employees depended on sales bonuses. The man seemed truthful. Madam Luosi had plainly bargained very hard—which also confirmed that she was obtaining excellent value. Some concession was needed lest they lose the opportunity.

“In that case, I shall help you,” Gu said, lowering his voice. “I shall take responsibility for having Madam Hu give you five hundred taels privately to compensate for your loss.” The manager wanted more. But Gu had presented this as a favour; refusing would look ungrateful, lose the sale, and offend a major customer. That was hardly sound business. He had no real choice. “Thank you, Mr Gu. The director happens to be here. I shall explain the position to him. Since you can decide for Madam Hu, there is no need to tell her of your promise just yet.” Gu understood. The manager feared that she might carelessly let something slip. To the foreign owners, accepting a private payment from a customer amounted to dishonesty. Discovery could mean not only dismissal but prosecution. Gu nodded emphatically to show that he fully understood. The manager excused himself to Madam Luosi and went to see the director. Soon he returned with a blue-eyed foreigner, square-jawed and wearing an upward-curving moustache. Gu recognized him at once as German.
