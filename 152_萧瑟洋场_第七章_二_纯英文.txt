HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Desolate Foreign Settlement

Chapter 7 — Part 2 of 6

The newcomer was indeed Biefa’s manager, Weilianshi. He spoke no English, while Gu knew no German, so the head clerk had to interpret. Introductions and courteous greetings followed. Weilianshi said that he had long admired Hu’s reputation and felt deeply honored that Biefa should supply the dowry for his beloved daughter’s marriage. Whether the price fell below cost was hardly worth considering. He would accept Madam Luosi’s offer and, in addition, have a silver salver specially made as a wedding gift. Delighted, she could not resist turning to Gu with a smile. “Such good fortune! I never expected it.” “Do not rejoice too soon, Sister Luo,” he answered. “I think there is something more.” “Mr Gu sees very clearly,” the clerk said. “Our manager has a proposal for Madam Hu. He would like to display the Third Miss’s dowry here for a month. Afterward, we would send a special escort to deliver it in Hangzhou.” Halfway through this explanation, Gu had given Madam Luosi a warning glance. She therefore listened quietly, neither agreeing nor refusing, and left him to answer. “How would you display it?”

“We would set aside half the shop and cordon it off with red silk ropes.” “Would there be an explanation?” “Naturally. It is something that brings distinction to everyone.” “Certainly. But we must discuss it. Is this a condition of the sale?” The clerk looked surprised. He had never imagined that the buyers could object. “Would displaying it cause some inconvenience, Mr Gu?” “Possibly. There is no need to explain why just now. Nor can we yet decide whether to agree. Please ask your manager only this: if we decline the display, does the sale fall through?” The clerk nodded and spoke with his employer in German for some time. “He says this is an additional request, not a condition of the sale. But we sincerely hope you will do us the honor.” “It is not my decision,” Gu explained quickly. “As you say, the display would bring credit to everyone, and I too would like to see it. But Mr Hu is a senior servant of the court. His public reputation matters. If we cannot meet your manager’s wishes, we must ask his understanding.” At the mention of an official’s reputation, the clerk understood and nodded repeatedly. “Of course. When might we hear your answer?”

After considering, Gu said, “Prepare a list of everything we saw today, with the prices, and come to my house tomorrow morning to discuss payment. I may be able to answer about the display then. If we must write to Hangzhou, however, it will take another half month.” “Certainly. I shall call on you myself tomorrow.” Madam Luosi was extremely pleased with the day’s visit to Biefa. Sitting in the armchair beside the Seventh Lady’s bed, she described it with lively words and gestures. When Gu mentioned privately promising the clerk five hundred taels in compensation, she not only accepted the expense but praised his handling of it. Naturally, the Seventh Lady was pleased too. “There is one other matter,” Madam Luosi said. “Let my brother-in-law explain.” “It needs discussion, not merely explanation.” Gu described the proposed display and asked their opinion. “I see no objection,” Madam Luosi said. “What do you think, Seventh Sister?” “It might be too ostentatious.” “Especially now,” Gu added, “with floods in Shandong and the general situation unsettled. People may criticize.” Madam Luosi fell silent. She glanced at the Seventh Lady, and her face darkened.

“Yingchun,” the Seventh Lady said, giving him a look, “telephone the doctor for me. Tell him my mixture is finished and ask for two more doses.” Understanding, Gu nodded and went out, leaving the women free to speak privately. “Seventh Sister,” Madam Luosi said without disguising her wish, “I truly want these additions to our Third Miss’s dowry displayed for everyone to see.” The Seventh Lady had not imagined that she cared so deeply and seriously about it. For a moment she could say nothing. Once Madam Luosi expressed an opinion, she always carried it through thoroughly. “If that German had not suggested it, the idea would never have occurred to me. The moment he spoke, I wanted it. Think of all the work that goes into marrying off a daughter. What is it for? To make a splendid occasion. When the dowry goes out, everyone should come to look. The more people, the greater the honor. After all the care and effort, to hear praise and envy on that one day makes one think: ‘Ah, this is what it means to live!’ Remember how we felt as girls, watching wealthy families marry their daughters. Think of Mr Hu’s feelings now. Would the German’s proposal not tempt him?”

The Seventh Lady’s thoughts began to follow the same course. In rich and distinguished families, the grandest celebrations were parents’ birthdays and daughters’ marriages. Yet after sixty came seventy, and after seventy, eighty or ninety: there would be further opportunities for splendid birthdays. A daughter’s wedding came only once. Parents had only that occasion on which to show their love in full, and could therefore add magnificence to magnificence without limit. As a child, the Seventh Lady had seen one great household’s dowry procession end with eight middle-aged men in new dark-blue robes. Each carried a red-lacquered tray holding a thick ledger bound in coarse blue cloth. What sort of dowry was that? On asking, she learned that the bride was receiving eight pawnshops. The eight men were their senior appraisers, and the books their general ledgers. Such an extraordinary dowry displayed wealth so impressively that the memory never faded. Now Biefa proposed to exhibit part of the Third Miss’s dowry in Shanghai, where people from every region gathered. News of it could carry stories of Hu’s lavish wedding preparations to Yunnan, Guizhou, Sichuan, and the remotest places. No amount of money could buy that publicity. Small wonder Madam Luosi was tempted. “What Mr Hu loves above all is prestige. If I let such an opportunity pass, I should feel I had failed him. Do you not agree?”

“Then why not ask him?” the Seventh Lady suggested. “We cannot. If we ask—” Madam Luosi paused. “Put yourself in his place.” A moment’s thought showed the difficulty. A man fond of outward show would never admit it. Asked directly, he would say, “Never mind, never mind,” in a tone neither eager nor actually opposed. Yet circumstances were different now; perhaps they could discuss the matter candidly with him.

Before the Seventh Lady settled that thought, Madam Luosi spoke again. “To tell the truth, buying these additions to the dowry is a thankless task. There is no fixed standard for prices or quality. No one will notice a bargain, but let me overpay for one thing and there will be plenty of talk behind my back. If Biefa displays what I have bought, that proves its worth, and no one will dare complain. I can account for myself to our old lady, the Third Miss’s mother, and everyone in the Hu household. I want them to know that I care for her even more than if she were my own daughter.” The last words moved the Seventh Lady. This mattered deeply to Madam Luosi’s reputation and standing in the family. The exhibition would prove both the excellence of her purchases and her generosity toward a daughter she loved as her own. Conversely, if such an opportunity for distinction were refused, no one would understand that the reason was to avoid ostentation harmful to Hu’s official reputation. They would say the goods were worthless and too embarrassing to show. The difference between these impressions was crucial. After pondering for some time, the Seventh Lady said, “Biefa’s displays are for foreigners. Few Chinese enter foreign shops, so it makes little difference to them whether the goods are displayed. These things would chiefly be shown to foreigners. In that case, I have an idea. See what you think.”

“Tell me.” “Let them exhibit the goods, but require every description to be in English. No Chinese characters. That way it will look less ostentatious.” After a moment Madam Luosi answered with an emphatic “Good,” seeming entirely willing to follow her lead. The Seventh Lady then called, “Little Sister!” For several months she had addressed Ruixiang that way, and Ruixiang had accepted it naturally, answering readily. With Madam Luosi present, however, she grew shy and did not even dare reply aloud. She merely hurried to the bedside to await instructions.

“Find out where the master is and ask him to come.” Ruixiang went. Madam Luosi lowered her voice. “I came for three things: the Third Miss’s dowry, a visit to you in your illness, and Ruixiang. Why have they still not begun married life together?” “I have urged him several times.” “Him” meant Gu, who had now appeared at the door. The Seventh Lady fell silent but gestured and gave Madam Luosi a look indicating that they would discuss it later. “Yingchun, I have thought of a way, and Sister Luo agrees.” She explained the plan. Gu thought it scarcely better than stopping one’s ears while stealing a bell. Still, it was preferable to Chinese labels. He nodded. “I shall tell Biefa’s clerk when he comes. But—”

He left the sentence unfinished, but the Seventh Lady understood. “Provided it stays out of the newspapers, it cannot attract too much attention. Are you not a friend of the ‘Rebel First Scholar’—the man you go drinking with in the pleasure quarters?” “Yes! You have reminded me. I shall speak to him. Is there anything else?” “Only that.” Gu turned to Madam Luosi. “I am sorry I cannot dine with you tonight, Sister Luo. I have an important appointment with Mi Benchang and must leave now. Oh, he knows you are here and wants to invite you to dinner. When would you be free?” “No, thank him for me. He lives in Shanghai without his family; it would be awkward for me to visit. Please decline firmly on my behalf.” Once Gu had gone, Madam Luosi hastened to ask about the “Rebel First Scholar.” “His surname is Wang, and his name Tao—the same character as in Hangzhou’s Taoguang,” the Seventh Lady explained. “When the rebels were powerful, they held examinations, and Wang Tao came first. Everyone in Shanghai calls him the ‘Rebel First Scholar.’” “What does that have to do with the newspapers?” “He works at Shenbao and has considerable influence there. If Yingchun asks him to keep the dowry display out of the paper, the family will not hear about it, and there will be no trouble.” “I see.” Madam Luosi glanced toward Ruixiang.

The Seventh Lady understood at once and sent Ruixiang to supervise the kitchen, freeing them to discuss her. “I have urged Yingchun several times. He always says we can talk later. Business is poor, he says, and he has no heart for such matters. Your visit is timely. Persuade him for me. If he still refuses, take it up with him.” “Take it up with him?” Madam Luosi was astonished. “How could I approach my brother-in-law on a matter like that?” “Why not? Ruixiang came from you. You ought to speak for her.” “Ah!” Madam Luosi laughed. “Once a thing passes through your mouth, even the unreasonable becomes reasonable.” “It is reasonable.” The Seventh Lady lowered her voice. “They are all right: one is in no hurry, and the other is probably impatient inwardly but says nothing. I am the one who suffers, feeling as though I owe Ruixiang something.” “With that reason, I certainly can take it up with him. He will have to choose a day.” “If we wait for him, he will find more excuses. Let us choose it ourselves. There must at least be a wedding cup of wine, and you must stay to drink it.” “Of course.” Madam Luosi considered. “Today is the twenty-eighth of the eighth month. This is a short month; the ninth begins the day after tomorrow. The Third Miss’s wedding is only two months away. I really am longing to get home.” “I know, I know. The almanac hangs behind the dressing-table mirror. Please bring it.” They consulted it and found the third day of the ninth month auspicious for everything. Madam Luosi’s eagerness to return to Hangzhou left no time to choose another date. They settled at once on that day for Gu and Ruixiang to begin their married life together.

“She must have some new clothes and a couple of pieces of jewelry,” Madam Luosi said. “Those will be my dowry gift. You need not trouble about them.” “Your gift is yours,” the Seventh Lady replied. “I have prepared something too, though perhaps not enough. Do not stand on ceremony.” Reaching beneath her pillow, she took out a Fukang passbook. “Take her shopping tomorrow. Whatever she likes, please buy it for her on my behalf.” Their friendship allowed neither polite hesitation nor refusal. Madam Luosi took the passbook and put it into her pocket without looking inside. “Our old lady worries about you so much. Will you be able to come to the wedding in the eleventh month?” “I should like to. But I may have difficulty getting about and cause you trouble.” “What trouble? We need only assign another couple of maids or older serving women to look after you. And Ruixiang will be there.” Long confined to bed, the Seventh Lady had often wished to go somewhere, and the invitation made her still more eager. Her chief concern was that a household already overwhelmed with wedding preparations might lack time to care for her properly. They would feel uneasy, and she herself might not be comfortable either. Despite Madam Luosi’s encouragement, she still felt the matter required thought.

“Madam,” Ruixiang said, coming in, “both dishes you mentioned yesterday have arrived. Are you hungry? If so, I shall serve dinner.” “Which dishes?” The previous evening Madam Luosi had fondly recalled several foods she used to enjoy and could not tell which two Ruixiang meant. “Did you not say you missed zaobotou, the wine-lees casserole, and dumplings stuffed with greens most of all?” “Exactly! But they must come from Sanpailou and Tao A’da’s.” “They do. I gave particular instructions to buy from those places. The casserole may be too rich for my mistress, but she can eat the vegetable dumplings. Shall I bring dinner in here?” “Yes, yes!” The Seventh Lady loved company. “I grew up in Shanghai and have always heard of Sanpailou’s vegetable dumplings, but never seen them. I must try them tonight.” “Several shops there make them. Widow Xu’s are the best.” “What is so good about them?” In Shanghai, the glutinous-rice dumplings eaten at the Lantern Festival were called yuanzi. Widow Xu’s, at Sanpailou, gave full value. Frugal customers would buy a bowl containing four meat dumplings, eat only the wrappers, and take the four balls of filling home to stew with cabbage and bean-thread noodles for another meal. Her greatest specialty, however, was the vegetable variety. “She says she has a secret, but once explained it is nothing remarkable,” Madam Luosi said. “I ate there several times and watched quietly until I understood. The secret is painstaking work. Only the finest leaves are chosen; yellow and old ones are discarded. Even the tender leaves have every vein stripped out. They are briefly blanched, chopped very fine, put into a ramie-cloth bag, and squeezed dry. Mixed thoroughly with fine salt and stone-ground sesame oil, they make the filling. Naturally, the wrappers use the best wet-milled rice flour.” “Then—” The Seventh Lady was just hungry enough to swallow involuntarily, making Madam Luosi laugh.
