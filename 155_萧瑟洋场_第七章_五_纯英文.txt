HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

The Desolate Foreign Settlement

Chapter 7 — Part 5 of 6

“She said, ‘Never mind, then. My husband says money is tight and Fukang might not have ready silver when we urgently need it, so a foreign bank would be safer. But after what you have said, I feel rather ashamed. I shall leave it with you.’ Madam Luosi, that taught me a trick. We must polish our golden signboard with foreign metal polish. What is that polish? Dealings with foreign banks. I mean to open an account with every one of them. If a customer like Intendant Zhou’s fifth concubine comes to withdraw money, I shall ask which foreign bank’s draft she wants. Whichever she names, she shall have it. Would that not make Fukang’s name more impressive still?” Madam Luosi found his argument persuasive and followed it closely. Now a question occurred to her. “A fine method, but would it not leave large sums lying idle in those banks?” Mi waved both hands. “No, no! That would be foolish.” “What is the clever way, then?” “There is another trick. Open an account at each bank and deposit two or three thousand taels. When a draft is issued, I arrange to send in enough funds before it is presented, and nothing goes wrong.” “Can you do it in time?”

“Certainly. That is where the telephone comes in. Lift the receiver, ring them up, and explain. Their staff will find a way to accommodate us.” Madam Luosi was learning a great deal. Hu had explained the essentials of Chinese banking several times, so she was no novice; foreign banks, however, were less familiar. Hearing Mi speak so confidently, she would not miss the chance to learn. How did the staff accommodate Fukang, and why should they do so? “There are many ways, but the principle is always the same: gain a little time until we have assembled and supplied the funds.” “What if you cannot?” “Exactly. However unlikely, the worst possibility must be allowed for. It has never happened, but we must be prepared. Then we depend on ordinary social connections. I know all the foreign banks’ compradors and regularly entertain their junior Chinese clerks too. If ever I cannot assemble the money, I need only send word and they will advance it. That is agreed beforehand. We pay the usual short-term interest, and the advance may last no more than three days.” He repeated emphatically, “But it has never happened.” “Which foreign banks do we deal with?” she asked. “All of them.”

Mi began counting them off. Shanghai’s best-known foreign bank was HSBC, whose English name was the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Company Limited. The oldest, however, was the Mercantile Bank, opened in the fourth year of Xianfeng. The Chartered Bank had come later but overtaken its rivals. Its full English name was the Chartered Bank of India, Australia and China. Its Hong Kong and Shanghai branches nevertheless had different Chinese names. Hong Kong used the phonetic “Zhada,” while Shanghai bankers considered that insufficiently resonant and uninformative. They called it Maijiali, after its manager. “It was established by a royal charter from the British queen. But it exists specifically to serve British trade in India, Australia, and China. Its main business is deposits, loans, and remittances—a purely commercial bank, unlike HSBC, which has something of an official air. Since His Excellency Zuo came to Liangjiang, Mr Hu no longer handles foreign-debt repayments, and Fukang’s connection with HSBC has weakened. I am now concentrating on the Chartered Bank. Please mention that to Mr Hu, Madam Luo.” “I shall.” Mi’s skill in managing his connections impressed her and changed her opinion. In fulfilling his duties and serving his employer, he could hardly avoid offending friends. Yet she had promised Gu that she would obtain satisfaction for him. To begin boldly and end feebly would seem a betrayal.

Her difficulty showed in her face. Quick to read an expression, Mi asked, “Is there something you hesitate to say? Please do not. After so many years with Mr Hu, I am almost one of the younger generation. You are my elder. If I have done anything wrong, tell me. To borrow a literary phrase, I shall correct any fault I have, and guard against those I have not.” His apparent sincerity persuaded her that a hint could do no harm. “You have done nothing wrong. Mr Hu entrusted Fukang to you; naturally you must protect it. But sometimes you must consider a friend’s affairs too. After all, everyone is in the same boat.” That revealed her purpose: she had come to call him to account for withholding the money due to Gu. He immediately admitted fault and apologized. “Yes. I went a little too far with Yingchun, thinking of Fukang as the lifeblood of Mr Hu’s business. But public business and private friendship are separate; my feelings for him have not changed. Tell me how I ought to make amends, and I shall obey.” “Making amends is too strong a phrase.” An idea struck her. “There is an opportunity now to preserve your friendship.” She glanced outside and left the thought unfinished. Mi understood after a moment: Gu’s taking a concubine. She had looked out to make sure Ruixiang could not hear. “I understand. I shall take charge and give him a proper celebration.”

An expensive gift alone would be unremarkable. Mi’s volunteering to organize the occasion, giving his own time and effort, would demonstrate real friendship. Madam Luosi was delighted, though she feared mere polite evasion and therefore pinned him down. “Remember, Mr Mi, that was your own offer.” “Do not worry. Leave everything to me. I shall make it splendid.” He added gravely, “But business and private matters must remain separate. There is no need to tell Yingchun anything of what I have explained today.” “I understand.” While entertaining her, Mi was turning over plans of his own. He too had ambitions. Looking at Hu’s dazzling success, he could not help thinking that a man ought to achieve as much himself. However trusted, he remained an employee. He should build a business of his own. The idea had first occurred five years earlier, but only at the end of the year before last had it taken shape.

His younger cousin, Chen Yisheng, had long worked with the coastal junk merchants in the trade between north and south. While accompanying a shipment north that year, he had been injured when a mast suddenly snapped and struck his leg. Left disabled, he received two thousand taels from his employer and was sent home to Ningbo to recuperate. Mi returned there for the New Year and spent much time with him. On New Year’s Eve, they talked through the night and reached a conclusion. Mi had considered his position repeatedly. If he told Hu that he wanted a business of his own, Hu would help him, but he would have to begin on a small scale and resign from Fukang. To leave the position of guest-master at a great monastery for the abbacy of a tiny thatched hermitage was hardly wise. The clever course, he believed, was to use his position at Fukang to deploy other people’s capital in his own enterprise. It must not be banking or silk and cocoons, however, since those would compete with his employer. There were two difficulties. First, what business would return its capital quickly? He could mobilize three to five hundred thousand taels, but only temporarily. Repeated short turns of the money might conceal his misuse of entrusted funds; a long commitment would expose him. Second, he could not appear openly. People would inquire into the source of his capital; worse, Hu might say, “Building a business takes concentration. You cannot remain my head clerk. Otherwise you will fall between two stools, harming both your affairs and mine.” Then the whole scheme would collapse. Chen solved both problems. “For a quick return of capital, nothing beats the north-south trade. Orders are placed beforehand and deposits collected; the balance is settled when the goods arrive. Southern goods go north, northern goods south—two transactions on one voyage. After two or three return trips, Brother Benchang, you will be a great merchant.” “If it is so good, why can I scarcely find anyone among my acquaintances in that line?”

“It is not that there are none; you simply do not know about them. The trade requires substantial capital, beyond most people’s reach. Those with enough money are often unwilling to bear the hardship. Without experience, knowledge of prices, and the ability to judge goods, even a man escorting his own cargo will be taken advantage of. Wealthy people therefore advance money for others to trade with. Unless disaster strikes, there is always a profit.” “Exactly. Suppose something happens at sea and the ship sinks, giving its cargo to the Dragon King. What then?” “That is the risk. But with insurance companies now, matters are quite secure.” “What did people do before insurance?” “Traded all the same. Not one voyage in ten necessarily ends in disaster. Even if one does, the profits from the others cover the loss.” Mi was greatly tempted. “A pity about your lame leg, Yisheng.” “My leg is lame.” Chen tapped his head. “My mind is not. Besides, the injury has healed; at worst, moving about is a little inconvenient. I am not confined to bed.” Mi considered. Chen already knew the trade and had long had wealthy backers. With him acting openly, nobody would know where the capital came from. Mi feared only that he might turn dishonest, and remained silent.

Chen, whose mother was Mi’s paternal aunt, very much wanted the partnership: a large fortune for one cousin and a smaller one for the other. Mi’s silence disappointed him, but he had a remedy. He enlisted his mother. She too understood Mi’s concern. On the fourth day of the New Year, she invited him over. “A’chang, you and Yisheng grew up together. Your mother died when you were two; you even drank my milk. You cousins are like brothers. Why will you not speak openly with him?” Mi could hardly acknowledge his suspicion. It would hurt their feelings and close off future cooperation. He invented another reason. “It is not that I will not tell him. The money is not mine. I must think carefully before we discuss it further.” “I understand. You fear the risks. There are only two: an unfortunate voyage, or Yisheng’s failing you. If you fear an accident on the voyage, there is nothing more to say. As for Yisheng, to fail you would be to fail me. Tonight, when we burn offerings to the God of Wealth, I shall have him swear an oath before the deity and make his intentions clear.” At the beginning of the zi hour that evening, it counted as the fifth day of the first month, the birthday of Zhao Xuantan, God of Wealth. Every household burned paper offerings. At his mother’s command, Chen swore a solemn oath during the burning. He and Mi then settled their agreement: one would provide capital, the other work; profits would be divided two shares to Mi and one to Chen. Neither would draw on the profits for a year, allowing them to accumulate capital of their own.

Chen returned to Shanghai and rented quarters at Shiliupu. Once Mi’s first fifty thousand taels arrived, he recruited his staff and took a shipload of southern goods to Yingkou on Liaodong Bay. Returning, he sailed from Yingkou to Tanggu at Tianjin, loaded northern goods, and brought them south. The round trip took exactly two months. On the fifty thousand taels, the profit after expenses was three thousand—a six-per-cent return. Mi, meanwhile, had drawn the funds in a customer’s name at only a quarter of one per cent a month, or half of one per cent for the two months. His hostility towards Gu sprang from his own misconduct: he feared that Gu would discover it and tell Hu, and therefore wanted to keep him away from Fukang. Now his thinking changed completely. Growing confidence convinced him that he could afford to show generosity. More deeply considered, Hu trusted Madam Luosi and Gu above all others. Winning those two over would make his position impregnable and give his enterprise still greater scope.

Within the course of that lunch, Mi decided upon the plan and began putting it into practice. Taking a bright young apprentice, he volunteered to accompany Madam Luosi and Ruixiang, first to Fang Jiuxia’s jewellery shop, owned by a fellow Ningbo native, then to the silk shops around the recreation ground. Mi was himself a prominent figure in Shanghai’s foreign settlement. His punctilious attendance upon the two women greatly enhanced Madam Luosi’s dignity and altered Ruixiang’s opinion of him. Whenever Gu and the Seventh Lady mentioned Mi, they spoke of his lean, fleshless face as a sign of a difficult man. Now she found him quite different. By sunset, everything was purchased. Mi would settle all the accounts. They carried the jewellery with them; the other goods were to be delivered to Fukang and paid for on receipt, with the apprentice attending to the details.

“It is getting late, Madam Luo. May I invite you and Miss Rui to a Western dinner in Hongkou? Today is the last day of the month, and the third of the ninth month is the auspicious date. We must prepare the celebration immediately. We can discuss it over dinner.” “Thank you. I appreciate the invitation, but must decline. The arrangements do need discussion, though. Let me first tell Yingchun of your kindness. Come to his house this evening, and we can settle everything face to face.” “Certainly. That will do very well.” Mi escorted the women home, but did not go inside.

To Gu, Madam Luosi naturally presented matters differently. She first explained that Mi kept money at HSBC to establish Fukang’s credit and reassure its customers. “That is not wrong in itself, but he should explain it to his friends. He admits that fault, and I gave him a proper talking-to.” “There was no need.” “Of course there was. Some people do not know they are wrong until told. Then they realize they were very wrong indeed and feel deeply troubled. Mi is such a man. To make amends, he has offered to organize the celebration on the third. He will come shortly to discuss it.” She added at once, “Do not stand on ceremony. To speak plainly, he is Mr Hu’s employee; you are Mr Hu’s close friend. There is nothing improper in asking him to do the work.” Gu could only clasp his hands in thanks. They had scarcely finished speaking when Mi arrived with men carrying Ruixiang’s new clothes and other purchases. Smiling broadly, he greeted Gu with repeated bows.

“Congratulations, Brother Yingchun! I shall be at your service on the third. With so little time, I dare waste none. Let me impose on your hospitality tonight. We can discuss the arrangements as we eat and settle everything. Tomorrow morning we begin; two days to prepare, and the day after tomorrow we shall have a splendid wedding feast.” Gu was moved but puzzled by such enthusiasm. Mi did not usually behave this way. Was he truly troubled by his conscience and determined to make amends? Whatever his thoughts, Gu responded courteously. “You are a busy man, Mi. I am embarrassed by all this trouble on my account. Frankly, I have little inclination for festivities now. I agreed only because my wife urged me and Madam Luo was so kind. Something respectable will do; I could not presume to be extravagant.” “Certainly—it must be respectable. You are a celebrated man in Shanghai, Brother Yingchun. The occasion must not be too plain, or people will criticize it behind your back.”
