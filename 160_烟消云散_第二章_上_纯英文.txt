CHAPTER TWO: AN UNFORESEEN CRISIS

Madam Luosi had already gone to bed when the maid Hong'er came to report a message from the inner gate: Xie Yunqing, the manager of Fukang Bank, wished to see her. “At this hour?” Her heart suddenly sank. Could Hu Xueyan have fallen gravely ill? She dared not pursue the thought. “Madam,” Hong'er prompted, “shall I ask him to come back tomorrow morning?” “No. Ask him what it is.” “He only said there was a telegram from Shanghai.” “But what is it about? Go and ask.” Then she reconsidered. If it were not urgent, he would not be seeking an audience now; if it was, she must not waste time. “Open the inner gate and invite Mr Xie in,” she said instead, adding, “Don't disturb the old lady.” As Hong'er left, the other maids helped her rise and dress properly, then accompanied her downstairs. She too had learned to master appearances and steady others by her bearing. Though anxious within, she walked with measured steps, her skirt scarcely stirring. Those who read character and fortune from a person's gait had always said that hers promised distinction. Proud of her composure, she would not allow her steps to become disordered.

Xie Yunqing was always punctilious. At the first glimpse of her approaching figure, he stood respectfully with his hands at his sides, his gaze lowered in an attitude of complete restraint. Only when a breath of the foreign perfume she used reached him did he raise his head and bow with clasped hands. “I am very sorry to disturb you so late.” “Please sit.” She glanced around, then addressed the maids by the door. “Your manners are getting worse. A guest arrives, and you don't even bring tea.” “Please don't trouble. I've received important news and dared not keep it from you, Fourth Madam.” “Then sit down and tell me.” She waved him to a seat and took the place of honour herself. Xie perched deferentially on his chair, his voice beginning to tremble. “A telegram has just arrived. There has been a run on the Shanghai bank. They put up the shutters at three this afternoon.” Madam Luosi's heart jolted. “There can be no mistake?” “None. The telegram also says that Mi Benchang cannot be found. He is said to have gone to Ningbo.” “Who sent it?” “Mr Gu.” A telegram from Gu Yingchun could not be mistaken. She maintained an outward calm, but her thoughts were in such turmoil that she could scarcely gather them. After a long pause, she asked, “Where is Mr Hu?” “Presumably on his way home.” “How could such a thing happen?” she murmured, almost to herself. “With all Mi Benchang's ability, how could he fail to hold things together and let it come to this?” Xie had no answer. Rubbing his hands, he could only say, “It's a very serious matter. Quite unimaginable.”

She suddenly shivered. Struggling to remain calm, she asked, “What about Beijing?” “Tianjin must have heard already. Beijing will learn of it a day later,” Xie said. “Pull one hair and the whole body moves. Tomorrow will be very hard to get through.” She felt an immense pressure descend on her shoulders, far heavier than any physical burden. She longed to escape it but dared not. If she could not bear this invisible weight, the entire family would suffer. Yet even the thought of lifting it drained her courage, before any question of whether her strength was sufficient. Then came the warning thought of harm to the family, and above all to Hu. She could only grit her teeth and support it with all her might. “Mr Hu is on the road. We dare not alarm the old lady, and the other madam cannot devise a plan. What do you suggest, Mr Xie?” He had come to ask her for a decision. He could only smile bitterly. There was an idea in his mind, but he dared not voice it. After a silence, she spoke again.

“In your judgement, there is bound to be a run tomorrow?” “Yes.” “How much silver would we need to meet it?” “That is hard to say. Here alone, Fukang has notes outstanding for more than one million four hundred thousand taels. The deposits are greater still.” “How much cash is in the bank?” “About four hundred thousand.” She considered the matter repeatedly. “With four hundred thousand in ready silver, I should think we can hold out for a day or two. By then Mr Hu will be back.” Xie thought that, judging by what had happened, even Hu might have no solution. Otherwise, why would the Shanghai branch have had to close its doors? But he could not say that plainly. He asked only, “And if we cannot hold out?”

Had she possessed a satisfactory answer to that question, he would never have needed to seek out his employer's wife in the middle of the night. “Tell me yourself, Mr Xie. What happens if we cannot?” “There will be trouble, and people will get hurt. Suppose those who arrive early, or can reach farther, take away the cash first, while the latecomers get nothing. Put yourself in their place, Fourth Madam. Wouldn't you be furious?” The question needed no answer. “Go on.” “The worst thing is to provoke a crowd's anger. Once that happens, even the authorities cannot suppress it. Besides smashing the bank to pieces, they may come here to make trouble. Where that would end is impossible to say.” She was horrified. Forcing herself to settle her thoughts, she went over the problem again. “They become angry because some receive their money and some don't. It is unfair. If no one receives anything, that is a kind of fairness too. What do you think?” “Fourth Madam,” Xie said calmly, “you have understood.” “Very well!” For the moment, she felt she could shoulder the burden. “We won't open tomorrow. But we must give the customers an explanation.” “Naturally. We shall say the closure is temporary and ask them not to be alarmed.” “That is the meaning, but the words must be tactful.” “I understand.”

“I hear you are often in touch with Provincial Treasurer De's household?” Xie continued. Treasurer De was Dexin, courtesy name Xiaofeng, a Bannerman serving as provincial treasurer of Zhejiang. His friendship with Hu was close, and the women of the two households often visited one another. Madam Luosi and one of his favourite concubines were sworn sisters. “Yes. What about it?” “Go to his residence first thing tomorrow, if you would. It would be best to see him himself and ask for help in person. With the authorities intervening to maintain order, we shall have a better chance of getting through this.” “Very well. I'll go. Is there anything else we should consider and do immediately?”

One question kept troubling her: should she tell the First Madam of this wholly unforeseen upheaval? Within the Hu family's inner gate, there were, as the saying went, three rulers in one state. Nominally, the old lady presided over everything, like Empress Dowager Cixi ruling from behind the curtain. The First Madam resembled Prince Gong, while Madam Luosi was like Shen Guifen, who had died two years earlier. A Hanlin scholar named Wu had once written a poem called “The Younger Sister-in-Law's Lament,” comparing Shen, who had been summoned from the governorship of Shanxi into the Grand Council, to a daughter returning to her parents' home. So beloved by her mother was this daughter that the daughter-in-law in charge of the household lost her authority: all domestic business was conducted by the daughter under her mother's instructions. If the empire was the Manchus' family property, then the Manchu Shen did resemble a daughter coming home for a visit, or returning as a widow, and taking over the management of her natal household.

The closest parallel in the Hu family was the old lady's affection for Madam Luosi, which resembled Cixi's trust in Shen. At the daily morning “audience,” when the day's important business was discussed, Madam Luosi usually raised a matter and the old lady approved it. Alternatively, the old lady would ask and Madam Luosi would propose a solution. Her suggestions were almost always followed. Only after a decision had been reached would the old lady turn to the First Madam and ask, “What do you think?” Sometimes even that was omitted. Truly difficult matters, however, were not put to the old lady at all, and bad news was particularly to be concealed. Madam Luosi decided what she could herself and asked Hu about what she could not. If he was absent and a decision carrying some responsibility or risk of blame had to be made, she would consult the First Madam, hoping not for a useful idea but for someone to share the responsibility. Tonight's news was too serious. It would terrify the old lady; even the First Madam might fall ill with anxiety. Yet to decide alone would be more responsibility than she could bear. Telling her, on the other hand, meant considering the consequences. Xie was right: to steady the situation, they must first keep their own composure. Rumours outside were one thing. A single admission from within the family that they could not hold on would bring complete and irretrievable ruin.

“Madam!” She started slightly and looked up. A'yun, her senior maid, stood in the doorway. She had replaced Ruixiang as Madam Luosi's most trusted confidante. Wearing a soft rose-purple satin lined jacket, she rubbed her sleepy eyes and suddenly looked astonished. “Haven't you slept?” “No. Pour me some tea.” A'yun brought it and said as she handed it over, “Hong'er told me Mr Xie came in the middle of the night—” “Don't ask questions.” Madam Luosi waved her away with some impatience. Just then, the watch gong sounded again and the morning bell began to ring. A'yun glanced back in alarm. “It's five o'clock. If you don't sleep now, it will soon be daylight. Madam Matchmaker is coming for her thirteenth chicken today. The old lady particularly asked you to be there. How will you manage unless you get a little sleep?” In Hangzhou's official households, a male matchmaker was respectfully called Master Matchmaker and a woman Madam Matchmaker. Arranging a marriage was known as “eating thirteen and a half chickens.” Under the procedures of the Six Rites, a matchmaker made as many as thirteen journeys between the prospective bride's and groom's homes. Each visit required a fine meal, with at least a chicken killed in the visitor's honour. The joke was that a greedy matchmaker would come for another meal just before the bridal chair departed, but in the rush could eat only half a chicken. Hence thirteen and a half. Today, the matchmaker for Hu's Third Miss was coming to discuss the final details. Her next visit would be for the half-chicken meal before the bridal chair arrived on the fifth day of the eleventh lunar month.

Madam Luosi did not answer her point. She only sighed. “Third Miss has a hard fate too.” Then she added, “Go to Mengxiang Tower and see whether the madam there is awake. If she is, say I want to see her.” “Now?” “Of course now!” She was growing angry. “What's the matter with you this morning? Can't you understand anything?” A'yun dared say no more and slipped away. Mengxiang Tower, where the First Madam lived, was some distance off. Madam Luosi had finished a cup of hot tea before A'yun returned, followed by A'lan, the First Madam's trusted maid. “Madam had just woken up. She called me to her bedside and asked what it was. I said I didn't know. She asked whether it was urgent. I said that, if you wanted to talk at this hour, I supposed it must be. So she sent A'lan with me to ask you.” Madam Luosi knew that the First Madam was habitually slow to act, but how could she still fail to distinguish what mattered? With a sigh, she called A'lan in. “Go back and tell her I must speak to her in person. I'll come at once.” When they reached Mengxiang Tower, the First Madam was up and smoking the first of her five daily water pipes. “You're very early,” she remarked. “And dressed already.”

“I haven't slept all night.” The First Madam blew out the burning paper taper and looked up. “Why?” Madam Luosi did not answer immediately. She glanced back. “A'lan, all of you go downstairs. Don't come up unless called.” A'lan hesitated, then led out the three maids who were tidying the bed and clothes, closing the door behind them. Madam Luosi waited until no sound remained on the stairs. “Xie Yunqing came to see me in the middle of the night. He had received a telegram from Shanghai. Fukang has put up its shutters.” For a moment, the First Madam did not understand. Surely closing the shutters at the end of the day was no reason to send a telegram? Then she suddenly grasped it. “You mean Fukang has failed?” “It happened yesterday afternoon. Mi Benchang cannot be found.” “Where is our husband?” “On the road.” “Then he must have left before it failed. It wouldn't have failed with him there.” Having said this, she relit her taper and went on smoking, the pipe bubbling steadily.

Madam Luosi was surprised. She had not expected such composure. Perhaps she really ought to ask her advice. “Madam, Xie Yunqing wants to know whether they should take down the shutters tomorrow.” She paused for a response. With “putting up the shutters” already understood, taking them down plainly meant opening for business. “Are you afraid that, once they're down, you won't be able to put them up again?” the First Madam asked. “Naturally.” “Then what do you think?” “I think that, rather than let other people force us to fail, we had better fail ourselves. No, no!” she corrected herself hastily. “Suspend business until our husband returns.” “There seems nothing else to do. When will he arrive?” “By my reckoning, tomorrow afternoon at the latest.” “Do you mean tomorrow, or today?” “Oh, I misspoke. Today.” “Today! Just this one day too late,” the First Madam said regretfully. She meant that, had Hu arrived a day earlier, he could surely have seen them safely through. Madam Luosi lacked that confidence. Only a wife married to him from the beginning could trust him so completely. But what use was that? In a crisis, she thought, you still have much to learn. Her competitive spirit revived, along with her usual decisiveness. “Madam, this burden now falls on both of us. We must discuss what to do together. Whatever the outcome, the responsibility will be shared.” “I understand. Put forward whatever ideas you have, just as usual.” “Just as usual” meant she could decide and act for herself. At such a moment, however, respect for the First Madam's position was necessary, so she continued in a tone of consultation.

“First, I think we must keep this completely from the old lady. We'll call the male and female stewards together shortly and give them instructions. Will you speak, or shall I?” “You speak.” “I'll do the speaking, but you must be present.” “I will.” “Madam Matchmaker is coming at noon, and the old lady wants me to attend too. I'm afraid you must entertain her on your own. I have to go to the provincial treasurer's residence.” “To see his second concubine?” “Not just her. I want to speak to Treasurer De himself. I shall have to wait there, and probably won't be back by noon. The old lady may ask.” “What shall I say?” “Isn't Treasurer De's eldest daughter going to Beijing for the selection of palace women? Say Mrs De has invited me to discuss it.” “Oh, I understand.” Madam Luosi rose. “Please get dressed. I'll call them together.”

Those summoned were the Hu family's seven stewards: four men and three women. The women mattered most, since the men seldom passed the inner gate except under special circumstances and were less likely to let the news slip. They assembled in a hall near that gate which the servants called the Common Hall. The male and female stewards used it for joint discussions, and Madam Luosi often addressed them there at festivals or when important instructions were needed. But this was the first meeting that required candles to be lit. Everyone felt an indefinable dread. The first cold spell of the eleventh lunar month had arrived, and even their fox-fur coats could not keep the dawn chill from their bones. Their teeth chattered. Guided by two rows of palace lanterns, the two mistresses, principal and subordinate, approached slowly and took their seats behind a large round table. They removed their black-fox muffs. A'lan and A'yun put gold hand warmers into their hands, then took gold water pipes from the younger maids and began to fill them. “No.” Madam Luosi waved A'yun away and glanced at the First Madam. “Go ahead,” the latter said.

Madam Luosi cleared her throat and spoke in a voice rather lower than usual. “Today is the second. Third Miss's wedding is on the fifth, three days from now. You must all work a little harder. Everything will proceed as usual.” Working harder was to be expected, but why the reassurance that everything would be as usual? The thought made Old Mother He's throat itch, and her chronic cough broke out violently. Everyone looked at her in annoyance, making her more nervous and the coughing worse. Madam Luosi, however, showed no irritation. “A'yun, pour Old Mother He some hot tea.” Another maid had already brought it and asked quietly, “Would you like me to help you somewhere else to rest?” “It will pass in a moment,” Madam Luosi intervened. Then, as the cough subsided, she asked, “Have you taken your tonic paste?” “Not yet.” Old Mother He smiled apologetically. “With Third Miss's wedding, everyone has been busy. I haven't had this year's prepared.” “You aren't busy; you're lazy. A'gao!” “Here.” “Send someone to have four batches of tonic paste made up for Old Mother He. Charge them to me.” A'gao, the steward responsible for outside errands and purchasing, answered, “Yes.” After Old Mother He had thanked her, Madam Luosi continued, “You are all old members of this household, and none of you is young. You should take something to build up your strength. When you have time, see Mr Cai at Qingyu Hall and ask for a prescription. Whatever quantity you need can be charged to the household.” Such kindness was common. Fusheng, the oldest and longest-serving, led the thanks as usual. Yet they remained puzzled. Had they been summoned at this cold hour simply to hear these few remarks? Of course not. Still, her unhurried composure had done much to ease their sense of impending disaster.

When she resumed, she first answered the question in their minds. “Why do I say everything will proceed as usual? Is there some reason it might not? In a way, yes. A telegram came from Shanghai yesterday. Conditions are poor, and Fukang must close for a couple of days.” She paused deliberately to study their reactions. They varied. Some were unmoved, whether because they had not understood or because they had no idea how grave it was. Others turned deathly pale, plainly believing that the family's decline had begun. The calmer ones waited solemnly for more. Only A'gao, the purchasing steward who dealt with the outside world, looked evasive, his eyes darting restlessly. She made a mental note of it. “Mr Xie came last night and asked what to do. I discussed it with Madam. The trouble has arisen at an awkward moment between outgoing and incoming funds, while our master happens to be travelling. Once he returns, all will be well. You have served him for years. You know he has his own ways of managing things, don't you?” “Yes,” Fusheng answered for them. “He has weathered countless storms in his life. This one won't defeat him either.” “It's simply bad timing. Think of us as a great ship whose captain happens to be on the opposite bank. We have to bring the ship across and take him aboard. Once he is at the helm, it will steady itself and sail on as before. For now, Madam and I are steering. We are confident of reaching the other side, but you must help her.” “Please give us your instructions,” Fusheng replied. “An old saying tells us to cross the river together in the same boat. However many people are aboard, they share one life. If they perish, all perish; if they survive, all survive. You must understand that.” Several voices assented. “In a storm, the worst danger is confusion among our own people. One wants to go east, another west; one wants to turn back, another to go on. Too many conflicting opinions bring disorder, and disorder overturns the boat. So everyone must stay steady.” She paused. “If anyone thinks we cannot reach the other bank and would rather swim back and escape ashore, say so.” Naturally, no one did. After a silence, Fusheng said, “Please go on, Madam Luosi.”

“Since everyone is willing to share the boat's fate, remember that harming others means harming yourselves. Listen carefully. First, this matter must not be discussed in any of the towers or halls, above all in the old lady's rooms.” “Yes!” “Second, as the saying goes, whoever brings tales is a troublemaker. Do not go about talking. If anyone asks, consider who it is. If it is someone with no business knowing, say you don't know. If it is a close friend with a real concern for the Hu family, you may reassure them sincerely: it's a temporary disturbance in the market, nothing serious.” Seeing nods and expressions of understanding, she felt easier and announced her third point, again beginning with a proverb. “A tall tree catches the wind. You may all have offended people at one time or another. Some will be delighted that Fukang is keeping its shutters up; others may take the chance to play tricks. Ignore strange behaviour and it defeats itself. If people are gossiping, pay no attention. If you discover someone plotting something, tell me quietly. Once the report is verified, I shall privately give the informant a substantial reward. A'yun!” The maid stepped forward from behind her. “Here.” “Whoever comes to the inner gate asking to see me, you are to meet them. Bring their words directly to me. If anything leaks out, you will answer for it. Do you understand?” Not only A'yun but everyone present understood. Information would be well rewarded, provided it was passed through Madam Luosi's trusted maid. The secret would be safe, and the message would reach her faithfully.

“Madam, do you have any instructions?” Madam Luosi asked, turning to the First Madam. She nodded, finished her pipe, and wiped her lips with a handkerchief. “Fusheng has seen more than anyone here. He was with our master before the Long-Haired Rebels rose. He has watched all his rises and falls. Tell us, Fusheng, how did our master make his way?” Fusheng coughed to clear his throat. “His greatest strength is that he never admits defeat. Twice, everyone thought he was certain to lose, but it was like a game of chess: he already had pieces lying in wait. Out of a hopeless position came a miraculous move. This time he must have a move prepared too, though we don't know what it is. Once he comes home, all will be well.” “You have heard him,” the First Madam said. “Third Miss's wedding is almost upon us. Go on preparing for it happily. If the sky falls, the tall ones will hold it up. Behave as though none of this had happened.”

Before noon, it seemed that all Hangzhou knew Fukang Bank was in trouble. Many people were “hawking the Court Gazette,” as Hangzhou people called running about spreading news. Some did so because nothing so extraordinary had happened since the fall of the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom. More were personally affected. Hu had so many enterprises that countless people depended on him directly or indirectly. Among the most anxious was Yueru, concubine of Tang Zishao, manager of Gongji Pawnshop. For some time, people around the Hu household had been making businesses out of supplying it. Madam Luosi disapproved, but Hu thought that benefits should stay among one's own people and that everyone was free to do business. There was nothing to condemn. Gradually it became a fashion, and Yueru was tempted to try. At Old Madam Hu's birthday, the banquets had drawn in almost every celebrated cook in Hangzhou. Yueru partnered with a cook named Sun, catered for one day, and made more than four hundred taels, to her great satisfaction. For Third Miss's wedding, the banquets were to be held at five locations. The Hu household's own cooks would serve the guests of highest standing; the other four were contracted out. A'gao was close to Tang, so Yueru knew him well. Through him she obtained one of the contracts, covering a hundred and twenty tables across the midday and evening sittings. Again she partnered with Sun, who supplied the labour while she advanced the capital. Now that Fukang was in trouble, the wedding could hardly be so grand. Yueru lived behind Gongji Pawnshop, only a few shopfronts from Fukang. She could clearly hear the clamour of people arriving to demand their money and shouting abuse outside its closed doors. While she was anxiously preparing to send for Sun, he heard the news himself and hurried over.

“Have you soaked the dried seafood yet?” she asked. “It went into the water yesterday. Otherwise, how could it be ready in time?” “Wonderful! Now we may have to eat all the shark's fin and sea cucumber ourselves.” “Has Third Miss's wedding been postponed?” “Even if it hasn't, it won't be much of an occasion now. And even if everything goes ahead as planned, who knows whether we'll get paid?” Sun stared. “Then what am I to tell the twenty cooks I've hired?” he asked miserably. Yueru felt angry but had to restrain herself. They had agreed that she would provide the capital, including those twenty men's wages. She could hardly blame him for worrying. “Mistress Tang, you hear more than we do. Have you heard what has gone wrong with Mr Hu?” “How should I know? I was having my hair done when I heard voices outside, first buzzing like flies, then roaring like a tide. I sent a maid to find out and learned that, for the first time since it opened, Fukang hadn't taken down its shutters for business. Later, people even began making trouble at Gongji Pawnshop. What have you heard?” “People say Mr Hu may never get up again after this fall. The higher you climb, the harder you fall. When the God of Wealth takes a tumble, his ingots roll everywhere and the little demons pick them clean. By the time he gets up, his hands are empty and he's the Barefoot God of Wealth.”

A god of wealth without even a pair of shoes: the misery of it was plain. Yueru sighed. “I can't understand how it came to this.” “He used to rely on Lord Zuo. Now Zuo is out of favour and Grand Secretary Li, Governor-General of Zhili, is in power. They say Mr Hu and Lord Li are at odds. If Hu falls, Li will kick him instead of helping him up.” “I don't really understand such things. Let's talk about our own business. I'm afraid this misfortune may make the Hu family reduce the wedding to a simple affair and cancel our banquets.” “Cancellation would be better. What I fear is that the wedding and banquets go ahead and we aren't paid.” “You underestimate Mr Hu,” she objected. “Even if the God of Wealth has fallen, surely he wouldn't fail to pay for our banquets!”

“True. He wouldn't refuse. But you may feel too embarrassed to ask. Think what a muddle everything will be in, Mistress Tang. Even if you can bring yourself to ask, you may not know whom to approach.” Yueru was silent for a long time. At last she asked, “Then what do you think we should do?” Sun swallowed and answered with difficulty. “First, we must find out whether the wedding will proceed as usual.” “I'm sure it will. I know Mr Hu's temperament.” “And if the wedding goes ahead, will the banquets?” “That goes without saying.” “No. It must be said. Whoever gives the order is the person we can look to for payment. You ought to find Second Master Gao at once and make it clear.” Second Master Gao was A'gao. This reminded Yueru that, even if she could not find him, she could at least go to the residence to ask for news.
