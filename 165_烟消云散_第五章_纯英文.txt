CHAPTER FIVE: THE LAST FLICKER

From the following day, Fukang opened for business as usual. The pawnshops, the pharmacy, the silk firms—every one of Hu Xueyan's enterprises appeared calm and undisturbed. Everyone looked forward eagerly to the wedding at the Hu residence on the fifth, and the festive atmosphere softened the impression left by Fukang's troubles. The bridal procession was to arrive at dusk, but Dongping Lane was crowded with spectators from early in the day. Lantern standards and decorated ceremonial pavilions of every kind stretched for two or three li. The attendants all wore blue robes and black jackets, while the bearers had brand-new blue silk padded coats edged in red. It was a magnificent display. At the Hu residence in Yuanbao Street, outward prosperity likewise seemed undiminished. Lanterns and decorations adorned the house inside and out, sedan chairs and horses came and went, and laughter filled the air. Yet a closer look revealed little groups of three or five whispering together everywhere. Whenever a member of the household passed, they all fell silent and stared vaguely into the distance. The sight inspired an uneasy feeling that was hard to account for.

The same thing happened in the garden, where the women guests were received. Most disturbing of all was the bride's absence. No one knew where the Third Young Lady was hiding. According to information whispered by the older women and maids, she had been weeping since morning and had never stopped. It was nothing unusual for a bride to cry before entering her wedding chair because she could not bear to leave her parents and sisters. To cry all day, however, was rare.

Guests familiar with the Hu household were not surprised. The Third Young Lady's own mother had died early. Her mother had shared Hu Xueyan's hardships during Hangzhou's second capture by the Taiping army. The kind-hearted Mrs Hu consequently treated her as her own and told less familiar women visitors that she was her natural daughter. The child had always been indulged. Soon after her birth, Hu had won Zuo Zongtang's appreciation and the family fortunes had risen steadily. Everyone said she brought good fortune, and she was especially cherished. Whatever she wanted, she received; nothing had ever disappointed her—except the marriage that would determine her whole life. Only after the engagement had she gradually learned that the bridegroom, A’niu, was as ox-like as his nickname suggested: rough and quite without tenderness. He fell immeasurably short of the handsome, talented young gentleman in distress who secretly pledged his love in a rear garden, as she had read in ballad books and heard in storytellers' tales. She had long felt wronged. Now, on the eve of her wedding, a run had broken out at Fukang. Inevitably, people would say she was unlucky. She had also heard that Philanthropist Wang wanted the marriage solely to attach himself to her family's wealth and influence. If her parents' household fell, her life with her husband's family would be difficult. Everyone could guess at her hidden suffering, but no one had anything to say that would comfort her. She could not confide her troubles either; crying was the only means of obtaining a little relief. Mrs Hu and Madam Luosi understood her feelings well enough, but neither her principal mother nor her father's concubine could find a single solid reassurance to offer. They themselves were forcing their spirits up to entertain the wedding guests, with an unspoken bitterness in their hearts. Each was longing for some good news to comfort herself. How could they rack their brains to console anyone else?

“Stop crying! Your eyes are already red and swollen. How will you face people?” Mrs Hu could only repeat this again and again. The girl's eyes were indeed swollen, and the maids kept bringing fresh towels to apply hot compresses. At last firecrackers thundered, voices rose all around, and the bridal chair stood at the door. Still the Third Young Lady wept. Despite repeated urging, she would not move. Mrs Hu, Madam Luosi, and the women relatives closest to them were at their wits' end. Madam Luosi finally found a way. Asking the others to step back, she drew her stool close to the girl at the dressing table and spoke softly. “Your father has brought you up to nineteen, given you fine food and clothes, and provided a splendid dowry to send you into marriage. If you have any conscience, you ought to do something for him in return.” This had an immediate effect. The Third Young Lady stopped sobbing. She said nothing, but her eyes asked how she could repay him. “Your father has striven to come out on top all his life. At a moment like this, above all, he must grit his teeth and hold on. Who would have thought it would be a case of ‘the father striving for honour while the child lets it all go with a fart’? With all this weeping, you are crying away his fighting spirit!” “Who says so?” The girl straightened, suddenly defiant. “That is more like it. Quick, bring a hot towel!” Madam Luosi called over her shoulder. “Coming!” a maid answered brightly.

“Third Young Lady, I have a passbook for the Shanghai HSBC, with ten thousand taels on deposit. Keep it hidden, and do not use it unless it becomes necessary. I do not suppose any such necessity will arise, but ‘a person may be a hero, yet money gives that hero courage.’ With this passbook, you will feel braver.” She slipped a paper-wrapped packet into the girl's hands and added, “The seal is on the face of a gold ring, engraved with the character Luo. Once you are inside the bridal chair, you had better put the ring on your finger.” The Third Young Lady nodded at every instruction. Her heart still ached, but now she could hold back her tears.

The wedding celebrations did not draw to a close until the bride and groom returned to her parents' home on the third day. During those three days, however, the situation changed again and a serious new disturbance broke out. It began in the capital, the empire's foremost city. On the sixth day of the eleventh month, the news from Shanghai reached Tianjin and passed from there to Beijing. Fukangfu was immediately besieged by depositors. Unable to meet their demands, Wang Weixian put up the shutters and slipped away. Local ruffians stirred up a mob, broke open the shutters at midnight, and looted the premises. By the time the censor responsible for patrolling the city arrived, the place was a wreck. Early the next morning, still more people came to demand cash. The prefect of Shuntian had to join the patrolling censor in issuing a proclamation to reassure the public. Beijing's four long-established banks—Hengxing, Henghe, Hengli, and Hengyuan—were all in the Dongsi Pailou district. Known as the Four Great Hengs, they had always enjoyed an outstanding reputation for reliability. Now, affected by Fukangfu's collapse, they too were packed with customers demanding silver. If the Four Great Hengs fell, the consequences for business were unthinkable. The local authorities therefore had to intervene. They ordered notes below a hundred taels to be paid in full; those from a hundred to a thousand taels were to receive fifty taels for the time being; those above a thousand were to receive a hundred. This barely kept the Four Great Hengs afloat. Smaller banks collapsed as soon as a run began, and commerce suffered severely. Meanwhile, banknotes depreciated and copper cash rose in value. Cheap silver and dear copper cash would normally benefit ordinary people, but falling note values and fluctuating prices harmed households with not even a night's grain in reserve before they could enjoy any advantage. The situation came to the court's attention. Hu knew that matters were taking a very dangerous turn.

By then Gu Yingchun had come specially from Shanghai to Hangzhou to share Hu's adversity. They had been friends for thirty years, but Gu was scrupulous about observing proper boundaries. He knew some of Hu's businesses intimately and very little about others. Ordinarily he avoided making inquiries that might invite suspicion. At this point he could no longer concern himself with appearances. “Little Uncle, it is not merely that paper cannot wrap up a fire. We cannot even afford to let a hole be poked through it. Everyone will peer through that hole to see what is inside, and it will be torn wider and wider.” He spoke with difficulty. “I think you should simply lift the paper yourself and let everyone see clearly. It would make matters easier to deal with.” “You mean I ought to admit failure and settle up?” “Have you never considered it?” “I have.” Hu's voice was faint. “More than once. I simply cannot make up my mind. Too many people are involved.” “Who?” “So many.” Hu paused. “For instance, some people thought well enough of me to entrust their money to me. If I fail now and repay them only a fraction, how could I justify it?” “Then let me ask you this, Little Uncle: are you certain you can bring the business back to life? Will holding on a little longer carry you through the crisis and enable you to repay every deposit in full?” Hu had no answer. Only when the strained silence had become almost suffocating did he speak.

“Business is too bad, and the foreigners are too formidable. I do not know how to recover. In the old days there were opportunities everywhere. If the banks made no money, the pawnshops did; if the pawnshops did not, silk would. Then there were foreign loans and purchases of arms. Eight jars and seven lids—you could keep moving the lids about without exposing anything. Now there are only four lids for eight jars. However quick your hands, you cannot cover them all. And people stand watching, ready to strike at whichever jar you cannot cover. It is hard, terribly hard!” “Little Uncle, at least you still have four lids. If you keep moving them about and make one mistake—or if someone beside you snatches one away—then…” Gu summoned all his strength to say it. “Then even if you hanged yourself, no one would pity you!” The words sent a chill through Hu. Delay would only make things worse. If he dragged matters out until the truth stood naked and people said he deserved to die, his lifelong reputation would be washed away. That was something he could never accept.

“Someone!” A maid came in. Hu sent her to fetch A’yun and told the latter to inform Madam Luosi that he would dine in the Hundred Lions Tower that evening. There would be four people in all. Besides Gu Yingchun, Mr Wu was to be invited at once. “We shall discuss the whole matter properly tonight and see what ought to be done,” Hu said. “For now, I must go and see a few people.” Brilliant lights, a roaring brazier, fine food and wine: the Hundred Lions Tower was as sumptuous and exquisite as ever. The four people at the table likewise appeared outwardly unchanged. If there was one difference, it was that Hu's old expansive confidence had vanished. As he grew subdued, the others lowered their voices too. “There are only the four of us tonight. Everyone must speak from the heart.” Hu's voice was a little hoarse. “These last two days I have been unable to do anything and have had more idle time than ever. Yesterday, sitting alone with nothing to occupy me, I picked up the Romance of the Three Kingdoms. In his thatched cottage Zhuge Liang recites, ‘Who first awakens from the great dream?’ I think Yingchun is the first to wake. Yingchun, tell Mr Wu what you said.” Gu spoke less emotionally now than he had at first. He had also thought of a further reason for reaching a decision quickly, before delay made matters worse. “With Fukangfu in trouble, the Four Great Hengs under siege, and business in the capital badly affected, I fear a censor may speak out. Once it reaches the Hall of Mental Cultivation, I doubt we shall be able to sit here discussing things at our leisure as we are doing tonight…” He left the sentence unfinished. Everyone was silent. It was not that they had nothing to say, but failure and liquidation were matters of such consequence that they needed careful thought.

“Fourth Sister,” Hu asked directly, “what do you think?” “Whether delay would help or harm, the first question is whether we can keep it up. Leaving everything outside aside, I already feel that I cannot go on deceiving the old lady. Pretending every day that nothing is wrong is exhausting. She has eyes, after all, and has begun to notice. She keeps asking whether something has happened. When the day comes that we can no longer hide it and the news strikes like a thunderbolt, will it frighten her out of her senses? I truly worry about that.” This was one of the very reasons Hu had been unable to decide. His attitude was changing, however. He was now wondering how to spare his mother too great a shock.

“I favour Mr Yingchun's proposal. A short pain is better than a long one,” Mr Wu said. “Since you have asked us to speak from the heart, there is something I dare no longer keep to myself. Some people say your name, Xueyan—Snow Rock—amounts to an iceberg. The day before yesterday, I had my grandson draw a character for divination…” “About my affairs?” “Yes.” Dipping a finger in tea, Mr Wu wrote on the rosewood tabletop as he explained. “He drew the character for ‘peak’ in the saying ‘After seeing the Five Sacred Peaks, one need see no other mountains.’ It is an ominous character. When the mountain of ice falls away, what remains is imprisonment.” Madam Luosi's face changed in alarm. Hu put a hand on her shoulder. “Do not be frightened. The iceberg has not fallen, so we are all right. Mr Wu must have more to say.” “Indeed. Character divination depends upon a sudden insight. Hearing Mr Yingchun just now, I felt this reading made still more sense. The ‘speech’ in the centre represents the censors, whose duty is to speak. For the moment a mountain weighs upon them, and there is no danger. Once your mountain of support falls, the censors emerge. Then there is a dog on the left and a dog on the right. One dog barks at the sun; the rest bark because they hear it. They all attack at once. How could anyone withstand them?” It sounded so coherent that both Hu and Gu felt they must take it seriously. Madam Luosi was more convinced still. “Mr Wu,” she asked anxiously, “does that mean there will be no trouble as long as the mountain behind us does not fall?” “There will certainly be some trouble. Look: the dog on the left has already risen, baring its teeth and claws to bite. But as long as the censors do not come forward, it need not matter. Throw the dog a meat bun and it will keep quiet. Then all will be well.”

“Exactly, exactly!” Hu said. “We have two things to do now. I must go and see Lord Zuo at once, and we must write immediately to Xu Xiaoyun, asking him to call on several of the more talkative censors in the capital and keep them in good humour.” This was the strategy of throwing a meat bun to the dog. Mr Wu thought a letter would be too slow; they must telegraph. “Yes.” Hu frowned. “But this cannot be sent in plain code. If it is, Sheng Xingsun will know at once.” “Surely Treasurer De has a cipher for communicating with the secretaries of the Grand Council?” “That is the Grand Council's official codebook. In an absolute emergency it might be used for two or three words on private business—someone critically ill, someone dead, that sort of thing. My affairs cannot be explained in a few words.” “If we can send even a few words, there is a way,” said Gu, who knew the workings of telegraphic correspondence well. “Ask Treasurer De to send Xu Xiaoyun a cipher telegram telling him how much to add to or subtract from the code numbers. We can then send our own message in cipher.” “Ah! That is an excellent idea. Yingchun, draft it for me.” Turning to Madam Luosi, Hu said, “Go and ask Treasurer De to send it in cipher immediately.”

She fetched a brush and inkstone herself. Gu took a sheet of paper and wrote without pausing: “Confidential. Brother Xu Xiaoyun, Secretary: Another telegram follows. Add twenty for the first fifty characters; thereafter subtract twenty. Xiaofeng.” This was an improvised cipher: twenty was to be added to the ordinary telegraph code for the first fifty characters and subtracted thereafter. It was a simple device to conceal the message in haste, and would be easy enough to break. By the time it was broken, however, events would have moved on and it would already have served its purpose of transmitting the information secretly. The phrase “another telegram follows” was particularly shrewd. The telegraph clerk would assume that Dexin himself was sending another message and would pay no special attention to Hu's. This misleading suggestion was a way of slipping past unnoticed.

Hu instructed Madam Luosi to go to Lianzhu at once and ask her to have Dexin send the confidential telegram. She was also to mention that he intended to leave specially for Jiangning the next day to see Zuo Zongtang, and ask Dexin to borrow a steamer from the naval commander stationed at Gongchen Bridge to tow his passenger boat. “Come straight back afterwards,” Hu added particularly. “I shall wait for you to pack my things.” He then summoned Secretary Yang, who handled his correspondence, dictated the substance of a telegram to Xu Yongyi, and asked him to prepare a draft immediately. He also sent for the chief steward to arrange a boat for the next day's departure. Having dealt with these matters, he began discussing with Gu and Mr Wu how to use Zuo's support to turn danger into safety. “Lord Zuo's help alone will not be enough,” Gu said. “We need him to bring in one other person. If they help together, the difficulty will be over. Only”—he frowned—“I am afraid Lord Zuo will not consent to bow to this man.” Both Hu and Mr Wu understood at once: he meant Li Hongzhang. If the governors-general of Liangjiang and Zhili, who also directed the southern and northern maritime administrations, would jointly support Hu, the public deposits could remain untouched. The large private depositors were all prominent officials at court; out of regard for those two men, they too would hesitate to press for repayment. Hu would then have little to worry about.

“That is a move that could bring life out of death,” Mr Wu said. “Whatever happens, Lord Zuo must be persuaded to humble himself this once. Mr Hu, this move really should be made early.” “To tell the truth!” Hu struck his forehead in frustration. “These last few days my mind has been a tangle of threads. I could think of nothing except keeping up appearances. When I could no longer manage that, I was so utterly exhausted that I simply gave up and left everything to fate. I thought of nothing. The fault is my own.” “It is not too late to mend the fold after losing a sheep,” Mr Wu replied. “If we decide upon this course, appearances must still be maintained. Explicit telegrams should go to every branch, telling them to find some way to hold on, whatever happens. It is like a battle: even if only one man remains, he must defend his position to the end.” “Quite right.” Hu nodded deeply. “Mr Wu, will you draft that telegram?”

Mr Wu could not refuse. The writing materials were ready on the table. He spread out a sheet and began composing as he wrote. Halfway through, Secretary Yang returned with his draft. Yang's language was concise and restrained, but it did not make the position sufficiently clear. Hu naturally praised it aloud, then said, “Leave it here, please, while I consider whether anything needs adding.” Yang had scarcely withdrawn when Madam Luosi returned with unexpected news. “Treasurer De says he is coming to see you. He has a number of things to discuss in person…” “Why did you not say I would go to him?” Hu interrupted. “Of course I did. I told him, but he insisted it would be better for him to come. Afterwards Lianzhu told me privately that there were too many eyes and ears at the treasurer's yamen; if you went there in the middle of the night, people would talk.” Hu was secretly alarmed and deeply distressed. It appeared that he was being watched. From now on, he would have to be careful about every movement. “Treasurer De is smoking just now. He will come once he has satisfied his craving,” Madam Luosi continued. “He has not sent the cipher message. He says he has another method and will explain it when he arrives.” “Oh. Did you tell him I was going to Nanjing?” “Naturally. I suspect that is why he wants to hurry over.”

“Good. It will be safer to discuss matters with him first.” In front of the guests, Hu took her ice-cold hand and said tenderly, “With all those muffs you have, you will not bring a single one.” She owned more than a dozen—sable, squirrel, black fox, every precious fur one could name—chosen to match her various fur-lined coats. But at a time like this, how could she spare a thought for clothes? His words brought back the sleeplessness and troubled meals of the past ten days or more. Tears nearly rose to her eyes, and she quickly turned away. “Wait, Luo Sijie,” Hu said. “Where shall we receive Treasurer De?” “In the Western drawing room. It is more comfortable and convenient.” “Yes. Have the Western fireplace lit.” “It shall be done.” She went downstairs to prepare for their visitor.

The Western drawing room had a fireplace, with two red velvet easy chairs before it and a small table beside each. On the host's side there was only a pot of Longjing tea. The guest's table held wine, dishes of fruit, pine-nut sweets, and flaky sesame cakes made with lard and date paste. Opium smokers liked sweet things, so these had been specially prepared for Dexin. “These cakes are rather good!” He put one in his mouth and reached for a second before he had finished chewing. Waiting outside to direct the servants, Madam Luosi quietly asked A’yun, “How many cakes are left?” “As many as you want.” “I mean the lard-and-date-paste cakes sent from Huzhou.” “Oh. I shall go and look.” “Yes. Whatever is left, wrap it all up and give it to Treasurer De's attendant when he leaves.” A’yun went to obey. Holding a small fine-porcelain teapot with a gold chain, Madam Luosi moved close to the wooden partition to listen to host and guest.

“It would not be suitable for me to send Xu Xiaoyun the cipher message you requested,” Dexin said. “Sheng Xingsun's men decode every Grand Council telegram as a matter of course. They would understand that addition-and-subtraction device at a glance. Instead, I shall telegraph a friend of mine in the capital and have him tell Xu that you need his help and that a telegram will follow.” “Then what cipher am I to use?” “The one in my own codebook. That friend of mine has a remarkably ingenious mind. They will not break the code he devised.” Hu thought that this would scarcely preserve secrecy either. Dexin would not lend him the codebook. Hu would have to hand over a draft, which would pass through one intermediary after another. With so many people involved, a leak would be hard to avoid and might make matters worse. He might as well tell Dexin plainly. “Mr Xiaofeng, I want Xu Xiaoyun to burn a little incense for me before those censors. The New Year is near. Let the seasonal gifts be generous and prompt, so that they can enjoy their blessings at home and leave other people's affairs alone. That would be a great help to me. If you put it to him, it would sound more respectable than if I did so myself. Would you be willing to do me this favour?” “Why should I not?” “Thank you, thank you! Shall the draft be prepared at your office, or should I have one ready?” Dexin had come with a settled principle in mind. Hu's interests did not, after all, matter as much as his own. Yet to protect his own immediate interests, he had to show at least some concern for Hu's future. In other words, he was willing to do anything for Hu's future in exchange for Hu's protection of his present position. He therefore agreed readily to the request to telegraph Xu, seeing in it an opportunity to demonstrate his loyalty as a friend.

After a moment's thought, Dexin said, “Ask a friend who writes well to join us. I shall explain how this draft should be composed.” Secretary Yang was naturally the strongest writer, but Hu thought Gu more suitable. Dexin might dictate something ill-judged. Yang would simply write it down; Gu would certainly object and ask him to reconsider. “My friend Gu Yingchun is here. You have met him, have you not?” “Ah, he is here!” Dexin sounded delighted. “Met him? He was of the greatest assistance when I went to Shanghai. Do invite him in.” Gu was summoned. Two years earlier, when Dexin had gone to Shanghai on official business, Gu had entertained him with the utmost care at Hu's request. Once the business was finished, he had taken him out incognito in pursuit of pleasure, without allowing a word to escape. Dexin had been thoroughly satisfied and had regarded Gu ever since as a capable man whom he would like to employ himself if the opportunity arose. He greeted him with delighted reminiscences and marked warmth. “Let us get to business,” Hu interposed when he could. “Yingchun, Treasurer De and I have agreed that he will approach Xu Xiaoyun for me. A third party can speak with greater freedom. The treasurer has consented; now we need a draft. He will explain what he wishes to say, and you can put it on paper. If there is anything you do not quite understand, ask him about it.” Gu naturally grasped the hint. Nodding, he said, “Let me find a sheet of paper.” “Are there not brush and ink over there?” “No.” Gu took a pencil from his pocket. “I need thicker paper, preferably Korean stationery.” “We have it!” Madam Luosi called from the doorway. In fact, no Korean paper could be found immediately, but she brought a thick sheet of Western paper. When Gu sat ready, pencil in hand, Dexin rose, paced a few steps with his hands behind his back, and began to dictate.

“The telegram must explain matters thoroughly. The first paragraph should describe the difficult times and the dreadful state of commerce. Countless Shanghai firms have closed. This is an exceptional calamity, not the fault of one man or one establishment.” Gu's pencil flew. Having noted the first paragraph's main points, he looked up. “Your instructions for the second paragraph, Treasurer?” “The second must describe Xueyan's financial strength and the foreign merchants' concerted manoeuvres over buying silk and cocoons. There is also another point. Do you think we should mention it?” Dexin continued, “The court has ordered the provincial authorities along the coast to prepare for war. Preparing for war is much the same as fighting one, and war requires money. The Liangjiang treasuries are empty. Lord Zuo has appealed to Xueyan for help, and Xueyan could not but do his utmost to assist. This has tightened his cash position still further and is one reason for the run.” “No, no!” Hu objected. “That would offend a great many people. Li of Hefei, above all, would dislike it.” “I too think it better left unsaid,” Gu agreed. “If Xu Xiaoyun passed that on to the censors, it would certainly create fresh complications, dragging Lord Zuo into the affair and putting him in a difficult position.” “Quite right. We shall omit it.” Dexin paused until Gu had finished writing, then began the third paragraph.

This was to say that Hu was fully prepared to meet his responsibilities, but that his credit had suffered and it was now exceptionally difficult to carry on. He stood at the dividing line between safety and disaster, success and failure. Whether he came through or collapsed completely depended upon the attitudes of others. If they appreciated that circumstances had left him no choice and trusted him to accept responsibility to the end, he would certainly not fail either his public or his private depositors. If, however, they took a shortsighted view and pressed daily to withdraw deposits and redeem notes—or, worse, deliberately made trouble and threw stones at a man already down a well—they might harm others without benefiting themselves. Hu would fall, and his depositors would recover very little. Both Hu and Gu thought this passage needed reconsidering, but Gu voiced the objections. “Throwing stones at a man down a well” might seem to point at Li Hongzhang. Saying that depositors would gain little by harming others would be even more likely to cause panic. Public funds could be protected by having property sealed, whereas private depositors lacked the government's power. Their only means of self-protection would be to rush ahead of everyone else and seize what they could. Would that not place Hu in danger of his own making? “True enough.” Dexin took the opportunity to show his goodwill. “I am only trying to be fair. If you find it unsuitable, we can phrase it however you wish.” “I think we should state only the positive side and leave the negative unsaid.” That meant asking people to understand Hu's difficulties and trust him to fulfil his obligations. Dexin naturally agreed and moved on to the fourth paragraph.

This reached the essential point. It need only state their wishes briefly and clearly; Xu Yongyi, occupying a key position in the capital, would understand thoroughly. But the next step required an arrangement by Hu, which ought to be settled first. “The New Year is close,” Dexin said, looking at him. “Are you still sending this year's charcoal gifts and seasonal presents?” “Of course,” Hu answered without hesitation, adding, “I am afraid I must send more than usual.” “How will you do it? Fukangfu cannot handle the matter this year. Whom will you entrust it to, and where will the money come from?” Only then did Hu realise how troublesome this was. His thoughts tangled, and he stared at Dexin, unable to answer. Gu could not refrain from speaking. “Since matters have come to this, and we are already asking Xu Xiaoyun's help, let us trouble the same man with everything.” “Yes, that is what I thought,” Dexin said. “If Xueyan agrees, we can discuss the steps.” “I agree.” “Good. Now, where is the money to come from? Finance is outside my province; you must work that out yourselves.” After a brief discussion, Hu and Gu decided to remit a sum through HSBC to Xu Yongyi for him to distribute. To avoid attracting attention, Gu would make the remittance in his own name. Naturally this had to be explained in the confidential telegram first.

The question was how much to send. Hu thought for a while. “I remember spending more than thirty thousand but less than forty thousand taels altogether last year. If we are to give more this year, we should remit sixty thousand. As for who should receive what, Wang Jingxian has the list. Xiaoyun can ask him for it.” Dexin nodded. “The telegram should say this: Although Xueyan is in difficulties, he remains deeply concerned for the year-end needs of the censors and the officials from his home province serving in the capital. He is arranging for so-and-so to remit sixty thousand taels to Beijing through HSBC. Ask Xu to obtain last year's list of charcoal and seasonal gifts from Wang Jingxian, increase the amounts as he judges appropriate, and convey Xueyan's respects. If they will treat the present Fukang crisis as though nothing has happened, neither hearing nor asking about it, time will certainly enable him to overcome his difficulties. That alone will be sufficient assistance. Those prejudiced against him, or ordinarily disinclined to court public attention, should receive particular care and reassurance.” The meaning was clear and the language reasonably suitable. Gu recorded it almost word for word, then revised the entire draft carefully and made a clean copy with a brush for Dexin and Hu to inspect. “Very good!” Dexin handed it to Hu. “Please read it closely once more.” “There is no need. I leave it in your hands.” Hu saluted with clasped hands. Dexin put away the draft. Gu excused himself and quietly withdrew, leaving host and guest to resume their confidential conversation.

“Xueyan, our friendship is no different from brotherhood. That is why I need not guard my words with you. Otherwise we might only make matters worse. Do you not agree?” Hu understood at once. He had long ago resolved that, whatever happened, he must act openly and honourably. A failed business could be started again. Failure as a man would leave no possibility of recovery and would sweep away the reputation of decades—a prospect he would rather die than face. After a moment's reflection, he answered with feeling. “Mr Xiaofeng, distance tests a horse's strength, and time reveals a person's heart. By coming tonight, you show that you will share my adversity. What you have just said proves that our years of friendship have not been in vain. I have brought this upon myself. At first I took the matter too lightly. Then my daughter's wedding intervened and I lost several days of the greatest value. From now on I cannot afford one more false step. Perhaps it is already too late; I can only do what lies within my power and leave the rest to Heaven. While matters are still in my hands, tell me whatever you wish. I shall certainly comply.” This was precisely what Dexin had hoped to hear. “Xueyan, let us hope for the best, but we cannot neglect to prepare for the worst. I have a list here for you to consider. If you can simply say there is no cause for concern, I shall hold off everyone on it for you.”

The sheet was three cun high and six or seven cun wide, densely covered in tiny regular characters. Hu felt irritated as soon as he took it. He tried to examine it, but his reading spectacles were not at hand. Moving it nearer and farther away did no good; he could not see clearly, and his head began to swim. For some time he had neither eaten with appetite nor slept peacefully. A feverish flush still made his face look ruddy, but in reality he was an exhausted shell holding himself upright by force of will. Now, agitated and distressed, he suddenly saw black and fell backwards. Fortunately the imported easy chair had a solid base and did not move. He did, however, knock over a cup on the adjacent table. The fine porcelain struck the floor and broke into several pieces. The sound was not loud, but it was enough to alarm Madam Luosi in the next room. “Oh! Oh!” she cried as she rushed in, so startled that she broke into Hangzhou dialect: “Jiage di, jiage di?” This was a local expression with a sound but no established written form, much like a northerner's startled “What has happened?” As she spoke, she went forward and pinched the groove between Hu's nose and upper lip. This groove was called the renzhong, and pinching it was said to be the most effective emergency measure when someone fainted. Hu, however, was merely weak, not unconscious. Although he had fallen back in the chair and seemed almost to have stopped breathing, his mind was perfectly clear. When Madam Luosi dug in with the long nails she had cultivated for years, the pain brought tears streaming down his face. He sprang up like a corpse suddenly coming to life, giving Dexin a tremendous fright.

Alarm soon gave way to delight. “He is all right, all right! It must have been his state of mind.” Madam Luosi now understood. “It is not only that. He cannot sleep or eat properly. He is far too weak.” She called A’yun in and ordered refreshments: bird's-nest porridge with pigeon's eggs. There was also a bowl of ginseng broth, prepared for Hu earlier. Because an honoured guest was present, she had thought it improper to serve it to the host alone and had kept it back. Now she had no choice but to make a show of generosity. “Bring another bowl and divide the ginseng broth into two portions. Offer one to the treasurer.” The broth was made from old wild ginseng from Jilin, bestowed on Old Madam Hu by Empress Dowager Cixi. It did indeed restore Hu's strength, and he soon revived. Picking up the list, he looked only at the total at the end: more than three hundred and twenty thousand taels.

“Mr Xiaofeng,” he said, “I am afraid I cannot assemble that much cash. I shall settle with you in readily saleable valuables.” These were the “fine and soft” valuables: “fine” meant jewels, and “soft” meant furs, paintings, and calligraphy. They would be difficult to value for payment in kind, but Dexin trusted Hu to understate rather than inflate their worth, and felt quite reassured. His spoken explanation was different. “Xueyan, I showed you this list only to remind you that these deposits came through my concubine and me. I had no intention of demanding them immediately. Now that matters have reached this point, I suppose you have drawn up an overall account? How much are you owed, how much do you owe, and can one cover the other?” The question left Hu confused and vexed. Yet Dexin had come late at night, at least outwardly to share a friend's adversity. Hu had to compose himself, think carefully, and give a reasonably candid answer. The need for a complete reckoning had, of course, been constantly on his mind. But every time he began, he found himself unable to continue. His thoughts had remained disconnected fragments. Now, patiently putting them in order, he was able to give a roughly coherent account.

“If we compare what is owed to me with what I owe, my reckoning leaves a comfortable surplus. The silk and cocoons held in Tianjin and Shanghai are worth nine million taels at market prices. There are twenty-nine pawnshops, some held in partnership. Taken together, my shares amount to twenty wholly owned shops. At a hundred thousand taels of capital in each, that makes two million. Hu Qingyu Hall must be worth at least half a million. As for the house we live in, that is hard to say.” “Leave your present home out of the calculation,” Dexin said. “What about antiques, paintings, and calligraphy?” Hu could only smile bitterly. Most were forgeries. Besides, his collecting had been an extravagance; he seldom bargained. Once an antique dealer had said, “Mr Hu, I am selling this to you honestly at cost. I am making nothing out of you.” Hu had been thoroughly displeased. Waving a hand, he replied, “If you do not make money out of me, whom are you to make it out of?” Once that story spread, dealers demanded outrageous prices. If Hu said, “Too expensive,” they would cheerfully admit it. “Meeting the God of Wealth means my good fortune has arrived.” Unless the asking price was truly absurd, he would generally write a note authorising payment at the accounts office. Naturally the accounts staff would demand their own commission. Dexin knew of these habits, so he pursued the subject no further. Counting on his fingers, he said, “Nine million and two million make eleven million. Add half a million: eleven and a half million altogether. How much do you owe?” “Including public funds, about eight million.” “Then you still have three and a half million left. You remain a very wealthy man.” “That is merely my hopeful arithmetic,” Hu said sadly. “If I could settle everything, keep a roof over my head and a few hundred mu of land, and live decently, I should be entirely content.”

“Why?” “The difficulty lies in the silk…” In recent years Hu's silk business had gone beyond ordinary commerce. He was fighting the foreign merchants to protect the livelihoods of several million people raising cocoons in Jiangsu and Zhejiang. It was like war: strength and weakness, attack and defence. The foreigners had joined forces, possessed ample resources, and attacked by every possible means. Hu fought alone and could only hold out, waiting for the situation to change. He resembled a city under siege. The foreign army lay before its walls, suffering the disadvantages of a distant campaign and therefore needing a quick victory. Hu, within the walls, benefited from waiting at ease for an exhausted enemy. If he could preserve order inside and hold firm until the besiegers tired of fruitless effort and withdrew in low spirits, he could open the gates, pursue them, and win a great victory. The run on the Shanghai Fukang was like a mutiny within the city. Yet war permitted deception. If he remained composed and prevented the enemy from discovering his true condition, there was still hope of turning danger into safety. This was why he kept up appearances and refused to admit any intention of selling off his stocks. “The moment I give way, I surrender. Once I surrender, they can do as they please with me. Goods worth nine million may fetch only a ‘reverse eight-tenths’ price…” “Xueyan, I do not follow,” Dexin interrupted. “What do you mean by a reverse eight-tenths price?” “I mean only two tenths would remain. Nine million taels' worth of goods would fetch just one million eight hundred thousand. This is the day the foreigners have been waiting for. Mr Xiaofeng, quite apart from profit and loss, I cannot swallow the humiliation. But”—tears gathered at the corners of his eyes—“it looks as though I may have no choice.” Dexin had never seen Hu weep, nor even heard of it. He too was deeply distressed, but could find no words of comfort.

“In a foreign country—in America, Britain, even Japan—the authorities would certainly support a man in my position,” Hu continued. “I keep thinking that it would not matter if I took a loss, provided the benefit did not go abroad. If the court would put up four million five hundred thousand taels, I would sell my entire stock at half price. Or if it would undertake to settle all my public and private liabilities, I would willingly hand over every business I own. But… ah!” He shook his head and would say no more. “That would certainly be an open and honourable way to cut through the tangle!” Dexin said eagerly. “Why not ask Lord Zuo to submit it to the throne?” “It would be no use.” Hu shook his head. “The court's priority is raising money for the war. Besides, Lord Yan controls the Ministry of Revenue. His calculations are too exact; he would never agree.” Lord Yan was Yan Jingming, Assistant Grand Secretary, renowned for financial ability. His method consisted of matching expenditure to income and practising thrift. He had always deeply disapproved of Hu's princely way of life and would certainly not offer assistance now. “Then,” Dexin asked, puzzled, “if you do not want Lord Zuo to intervene, why are you going to see him?” “It is not that I do not want his help. I merely feel it would be useless. Naturally, I must hear what he thinks. You know that Lord Zuo is the mountain behind me. That mountain must not fall.” He then related Mr Wu's interpretation of the divinatory character.

Dexin, it turned out, was also keenly interested in such matters. “Is Mr Wu here?” he asked at once. “I do not know whether he has left.” Hu rose to find Madam Luosi and ask, but she had heard their conversation and came in of her own accord. “Mr Wu is staying here tonight. I do not know whether he has gone to bed.” “Send someone to see.” “If he is asleep, leave him,” Dexin put in. “I should feel bad about disturbing him so late.” What this really meant was that Mr Wu should be woken even if he had retired. High officials always spoke in this fashion. Madam Luosi understood the convention. Aloud she agreed; once outside, she quietly told A’yun to send word to the guest rooms and ask Mr Wu to come immediately, whether he was asleep or not.
