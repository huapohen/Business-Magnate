Vanishing into Thin Air — Chapter Six, Part One

CHAPTER SIX: SEIZING THE DRAGON’S PEARL

Mr Wu had not yet gone to bed and came as soon as he was summoned. This was his first meeting with Dexin. After Hu Xueyan introduced them, the usual courtesies were exchanged. Dexin complimented him on his ingenious character divination, then proceeded to ask his advice. “You are too kind! A trifling skill, quite unworthy of serious company,” Mr Wu replied. “What would Your Excellency like to ask?” “I am pursuing a certain ambition and do not know whether there is any hope of success. Would you be kind enough to divine the outcome?” “Certainly. Please give me a character.” After a moment’s thought, Dexin said, “Let it be mou: ‘to scheme.’” Brush and ink were ready at hand. Mr Wu sat down, took a sheet of paper, and separated the character into its two components, yan, meaning “speech,” and mou, meaning “a certain person.” Then he laid down the brush to think. Dexin and Hu came over, each holding a water pipe, and stood quietly beside the table. “For what you are planning, Your Excellency, you need someone to speak on your behalf. You already have a person in mind but have not named him: hence ‘a certain person.’” Mr Wu smiled. “To be honest, I am only a dabbler at this. I know none of the professional diviners’ formulas. I simply consider the character itself. If I have begun correctly and continue taking it apart, perhaps I may stumble upon something pertinent.” “Yes, yes! Most perceptive,” Dexin said politely. “Then was my opening statement correct? If not, we must begin again. Please do not spare my feelings; you must tell the truth.” “I certainly shall. You have plunged straight to the dragon’s pearl.” Mr Wu studied his face until he felt sure the answer was sincere. “A fortunate guess!” he said with satisfaction, taking up the brush again. “‘Person’ beside ‘speech’ makes xin, ‘trust.’ The person standing beside the speech means that your spokesman must keep close at hand if he is to be effective.” Dexin nodded repeatedly, blinking as though weighing the words as he listened.

“Now look at the component mou, ‘a certain person.’ Add ‘woman,’ and it becomes mei, ‘a go-between.’ A woman must provide the connection.” “May I ask which party this woman connects with?” “A good question!” Mr Wu pointed to xin, “trust.” “There are two people here: one who speaks, and one who listens. The woman makes the connection with the one who speaks.” “You mean that the woman acting as go-between is not the person standing beside ‘speech’?” “Exactly.” “I understand. Now let me ask: when should I begin? Will I succeed? And if so, when?” “For that, we must consider the ‘wood’ beneath mou.” Mr Wu blotted out that lower component, leaving gan, “sweet,” and yan, “speech.” No explanation was necessary. Dexin understood that the person he relied on might promise everything, yet offer nothing more than soothing “sweet words.” “How would the ‘wood’ be lost?” he asked.

“Metal overcomes Wood,” Mr Wu answered. “If you began in the seventh or eighth month, the venture would already be lost.” “Why?” “The seventh month is shen, the eighth you. Both belong to Metal.” “This is the eleventh month,” Hu put in. “Isn’t the eleventh month zi?” “Yes.” Hu knew something of how the Five Elements generated and overcame one another. “Zi is Water, and Water generates Wood. Mr Xiaofeng, you must begin at once.” “There is no possible way to do it in time. Today is the sixteenth of the eleventh month. Less than half a month remains. How could I manage it?” “Besides,” Mr Wu continued, “though Water generates Wood, next month is chou, which belongs to Earth. Wood overcomes Earth: an unfavorable relation. It would be best to begin in the first month of the new year. The first month is yin, the second mao, both Wood. There will be a setback in the third month, but all will be well by the fourth or fifth. The fourth is si, the fifth wu, both Fire…” “Wood generates Fire,” Hu supplied. “Complete success!” “Precisely,” Mr Wu agreed. “Wonderful! I am entirely convinced.” Beaming, Dexin put down his water pipe. “I must offer you a fee, or the prediction will not come true.” He lifted his sleeveless outer garment, revealing a blue silk kerchief serving as a sash about his waist. Bannermen carried many small accessories on such sashes. Looking down, he detached a jade coin and presented it with both hands. “A trifling token. Keep it to amuse yourself.” Mr Wu found it was a flawless piece of mutton-fat white jade, finely carved with the four characters for “Qianlong circulating treasure.” It was not particularly valuable, but made a charming object to handle. He repeatedly clasped his hands in thanks. “This hardly counts,” Dexin added. “When my plan succeeds next summer, I shall thank you properly.”

After Mr Wu withdrew, Hu, despite his own heavy troubles, made a show of lively interest in his friend’s concerns and asked what he was planning. “What else but a post where I can take charge in my own right? I am working through Grand Secretary Bao, asking his younger brother to speak for me. You invited the brother south for a visit the year before last, remember? I offered to take him along the Fuchun River and asked you to join us, but you were too busy.” “Yes, I remember. Did you discuss it with him on that trip?” “No. I had only recently become provincial treasurer. I could hardly aspire beyond my station so soon. Our Second Master Bao took a fancy to a boatwoman on one of the Jiangshan boats and hinted that he wanted to install her in a secluded love nest. He also let me understand that he would supply the money himself: I need not spend a penny. But I had just taken office. How could I act as a procurer? I pretended not to understand. He has written to me recently and still hasn’t forgotten her. As for the woman, she wants to choose a suitable man to attach herself to. I’ve already sent someone to speak to her parents. For just two thousand taels, Second Master Bao can have his wish. I’ve been hesitating, but after listening to Mr Wu tonight, my mind is made up.” Hu could not help thinking little of such a way to seek a major provincial appointment. He also thought Dexin had chosen the wrong channel. As a friend, he ought to warn him. “Mr Xiaofeng, do you know how matters stand between Grand Secretary Bao and his brother?” “They don’t get on very well?” “Exactly. The grand secretary finds him a headache. I doubt his intercession would do much good.” “On the contrary. Our families are old friends, and I know their affairs. Bao can do nothing with his brother except placate him. If Second Master Bao nags him every day, he’ll have to listen simply to escape the nuisance.”

Hu could only smile at this. Then something occurred to him, and he added a smiling warning. “You must keep the business secret, Mr Xiaofeng. If the censors hear and submit an impeachment, there’ll be another Jiangshan boat scandal. It wouldn’t be worth it.” He was referring to a scandal of the previous year. Baoting, one of the “Four Hanlin Remonstrators,” had been appointed chief examiner in Fujian. Passing through Hangzhou on both journeys, he had taken a Jiangshan boat up the Fuchun River and into upper Fujian. On his return, he made one of the boatwomen his concubine. The censors prepared to attack him, but Baoting anticipated them and submitted a memorial impeaching himself, whereupon he lost office. Li Ciming, a celebrated man of letters in the capital, wrote a poem about it, including a particularly neat couplet: “Eight-Banner royal clansman, a scholar of grass; / Nine-Surname Jiangshan woman, a beauty with pockmarks.” Baoting belonged to the imperial clan and was indeed a man of letters, but the addition of “grass” was plainly satirical. The second line contained another allusion. The families living on Jiangshan boats were said to bear nine surnames, all descended from commanders who had served Chen Youliang at the end of the Yuan. After winning the empire, Zhu Yuanzhang punished them by forbidding them to live ashore: they could earn their living only on the water. The woman Baoting admired was what people called a “white-pockmarked woman.” Being so shortsighted that he could barely distinguish people and things even at close quarters, he had failed to notice. Hence Li Ciming’s joke about a pockmarked beauty. The lines became widely quoted, and the affair a laughingstock. Dexin naturally knew the story. Remembering the censors’ ferocity, he felt distinctly uneasy. Though he said there was nothing to worry about, privately he accepted Hu’s warning and resolved to be cautious.

Overnight, the situation changed drastically. Liu Bingzhang, governor of Zhejiang, received a confidential telegram from Li Hongzhang, Governor-General of Zhili and Minister for the Northern Ports. Six hundred thousand taels of Zhili flood-relief funds had been deposited in Fukangfu and were now stranded by its collapse. Li asked Liu to seal Hu Xueyan’s pawnshops as security for the public money. Liu immediately sent for Dexin, showed him the telegram, and asked his opinion. Dexin had anticipated just such an adverse development and long since considered his last resort. “In my humble view, our principal concern must be to avoid disturbing trade,” he replied. “If we act with ruthless speed, showing no consideration, we shall alarm the public. That cannot benefit the province. As for Hu himself, his spirit is honorable and straightforward. I suggest that I speak to him and persuade him to make his own arrangements.” “What do you mean by that?” “Let him draw up an inventory of his property and accounts of his public and private debts, and place them in Your Excellency’s hands, asking you to act for him.” Liu had imagined that “make his own arrangements” meant persuading Hu to take his own life. Discovering his mistake, he felt relieved. “Very well. I must trouble you with this. When may I expect an answer?” “Tomorrow morning, at the earliest.” That night Dexin called on Hu again. At their meeting his voice caught, and he actually squeezed out a few anxious tears. They said everything. Hu, however, was quite calm. “It would be wrong to say I cannot face the truth, Mr Xiaofeng, but equally wrong to say I am untroubled. I leave everything to your guidance.” Dexin explained his purpose. Hu agreed without argument, asking only for two days to settle his household affairs. Dexin pondered for some time. “I shall fight for those two days with Governor Liu, even if it costs me my official cap. But I hope you can hand him all the accounts when they are over.” “Agreed.”

After Dexin left, Hu and Madam Luosi shut themselves in and talked all night. The next day they took several steps. First, they sent confidential telegrams to the Fukang branches in Hankou, Zhenjiang, Fuzhou, Changsha, and Wuchang, instructing them to close immediately and begin liquidation. Next, they asked Gu Yingchun to hurry back to Shanghai and find foreign merchants with whom to negotiate the sale of the stored silk. Third, they gathered a quantity of ready silver and paid off the deposits of a small number of close relatives and friends. They also selected jewelry and antiques, valued them approximately, and used them to settle a group of deposits handled through Dexin. Most important of all, they drew up an inventory of their property. They worked quietly until midnight before reaching a stopping point. Utterly exhausted, Hu drank a glass of wine in which ginseng had been steeped and was about to go to bed when a special messenger brought a letter from Dexin. It read: “Supervising Censor Deng Chengxiu has memorialized, requesting that corrupt officials be ordered to contribute large sums as a penalty, to meet urgent needs. A supplementary memorial was attached. A copy follows for your information.” The enclosure read: “In a supplementary memorial he states: ‘I hear that Fukang Bank has closed, and that the sum deposited there by Wenyu, Assistant Grand Secretary and Minister of Punishments, amounts to more than seven hundred thousand taels. I request an imperial order to ascertain the exact amount and investigate its source.’ The Shuntian prefectural authorities are hereby ordered to investigate and report.” Neither letter nor enclosure brought good news, but Hu could not see how this might make his own position any worse. He put them aside and went to sleep.

He awoke with a clear head and naturally thought of Dexin’s news, and of Wenyu himself. A Manchu of the Plain Blue Banner, related by marriage to Prince Gong, Wenyu had served as acting Governor-General of Zhili as early as the eleventh year of Xianfeng. His fortune, however, had begun only after he became Fuzhou Garrison General in the seventh year of Tongzhi. The Qing armies had entered the passes and won the empire from the Ming, but even after the Southern Ming fell, the Prince of Lu remained in eastern Zhejiang, the Yongli Emperor in the southwest, and Zheng Chenggong overseas. There were also the Three Feudatories, princes of other surnames commanding powerful armies who might become a grave internal threat. Banner garrisons were therefore stationed at strategic centers in the provinces to guard against Han attempts to overthrow the Qing and restore the Ming. Their commanders were styled generals, with the place name attached: the commander at Xi’an was the Xi’an Garrison General, the one at Hangzhou the Hangzhou Garrison General.

Their authority and responsibilities varied with local conditions and circumstances. Fujian had first faced the fleets of Zheng Chenggong and his son, then Geng Jingzhong’s rebellion in support of Wu Sangui. As a strategic military region, it gave the Fuzhou Garrison General exceptional power. Elsewhere, generals commanded only Banner troops; the Fuzhou general alone also controlled Green Standard troops. He had a further duty: supervision of the Fujian Maritime Customs. Originally intended to inspect seagoing vessels and prevent illicit crossings or arms smuggling, this had gradually become a lucrative tax-collecting office. Especially after the Opium War, when the maritime restrictions were thrown wide open, British, French, American, and Japanese merchants established trading houses in Fuzhou. Customs revenue grew enormously, and its supervision became a famously profitable appointment. Wenyu became Fuzhou Garrison General in the seventh year of Tongzhi and concurrently acted as Governor-General of Fujian and Zhejiang from the tenth year. He remained until his transfer to the capital in the third year of Guangxu, spending nine years in Fuzhou. His official purse grew fat, and he deposited the money with Fukang. In the capital, he served successively as chief superintendent at Chongwen Gate and minister of the Imperial Household Department, both posts offering opportunities to make money. His deposits with Fukang totaled no less than a million taels, making him Hu’s largest customer.

An investigation into the source of this customer’s money had nothing to do with Hu himself. Yet it would make Fukang’s collapse an even greater sensation, which was very bad for him. There was nothing he could do, however. He put it aside and considered what would happen once he had handed over all the accounts. With the books gone, there would be no way for him to conduct the liquidation himself—would there? As this thought took shape, his spirits suddenly lifted. Incredible as it seemed, he felt the lightness of a man freed from debt. The reasoning was plain: surrendering all the accounts meant surrendering all the finances, and therefore all the debts as well. Liquidation would become the authorities’ responsibility. Of course, his conscience would still be burdened, but at this stage he could no longer attend to every consideration. He also retained one faint hope: that some outlet might be found for the great stocks of silk in Shanghai and Tianjin, enabling him to repay every debt. He had entrusted the matter to Gu, but Gu lacked sufficient influence. There would be hope only if Hu went to Shanghai himself and worked with Gu’s assistance. Seen in that light, handing over the accounts would let the authorities hold off the creditors for a time, giving him breathing space to concentrate all his efforts on the silk. Half his recent anxiety and dejection vanished. Slipping his feet into his shoes, he quietly went to Madam Luosi’s bedroom.

She too had worked late into the night and had slept for only a little over two hours. Hu lifted the fur bed curtains, and a warm fragrance met him. Madam Luosi was sound asleep, breathing softly. Unwilling to disturb her, he gently raised the silk-wadded quilt and lay down on his side. But she woke, turned inward, and said, “You really have no idea what trouble you’re in! At a time like this, you still have a mind to pester me.” Realizing her mistake, Hu could not help smiling. His changed mood even gave him some appetite for a joke. Taking hold of her shoulder, still rounded, soft, and warm, he said, “It’s like playing music while chewing bitter coptis: finding pleasure in suffering.” “Go away! Who wants to find pleasure with you?” Yet she turned back and took his hand. “I’ve just been thinking,” Hu said, coming to business. “When I see Governor Liu, I’ll ask him to take the whole burden on his shoulders. That will leave me free to go to Shanghai and try to find a solution. What do you think?” At this, Madam Luosi opened her eyes and sat up. She put on a fur jacket lying on the inner side of the bed, clasped her knees, and considered carefully. “Will he be willing to take it on for you?” “He’ll have to. Handing over the books means handing over the property. I’ll surrender it intact and ask him to deal with it as he sees fit.” “The property? Then we won’t even have anywhere to live.” “Of course we will. What I’ve come to discuss is where to draw the line.”

“Where is the line?” “It depends…on whether something is indispensable to living our everyday lives. If it is, we can keep it. Otherwise it is property, and must be listed and handed over.” “How can there be a fixed line? Some people are perfectly happy with plain tea and simple food; others can’t eat a meal without meat. How do you distinguish between them?” “Of course, there is considerable room for interpretation.” Madam Luosi considered this in silence. She had always imagined that only Hu’s businesses would be surrendered; among their private possessions, only gold ingots, bars, and leaf, together with ready silver, would be touched. Now it seemed little different from having their home confiscated. The thought made her heart ache, but she was strong-minded and clear-sighted. Holding back her tears, she tried to consider the practical questions. Yet she could find no starting point. Jewelry and fine possessions, ornaments, furniture, everyday utensils—each item seemed to require a separate assessment before it could be classified. As Hu had said, the scope for interpretation was enormous. “How could an estate like this be inventoried in an instant?” she asked. “I can see only two ways. Either tell Governor Liu that the private property is too complicated to list completely in such a short time, or give only broad totals with our own valuations: so many sets of hongmu furniture, so many linings of heavy fur, and an estimated total under each heading.” “The second seems better.” “But valuation is difficult too. Take this house we live in. What would you put on it?” “We can only enter the cost of construction. That will make the figures look better.” To improve the appearance of the accounts, they listed not merely the house at its building cost, but everything else at its purchase price.

They spent most of the following day at work again. When everything was ready, Hu went to Dexin to arrange an audience with Governor Liu Bingzhang. “Tonight would be best,” he said. “It’s hardly something to be proud of. The fewer people I meet, the better. Besides, I can wear informal clothes at night.” “I don’t think that is necessary. This is an entirely honorable proceeding, nothing to hide. Governor Liu comes from the Hanlin Academy and is particular about such conventions. Discussing it at night would make it look like a private bargain, which he certainly would not want. Let us fix the audience for tomorrow morning.” “Yes, very well.” Hu could only agree. “And there’s no need for informal clothes. That would make you look like a guilty man arriving at the yamen gate in a plain gown and little cap to confess. Still, Xueyan, you need not dress too splendidly.” The hint was that he should leave off the red finial and peacock feather. Hu understood. The next day, observing all the rules, he dressed simply according to the third-rank attire of a circuit intendant and set off by sedan, accompanied by his attendants, for the governor’s yamen in Youshengguan Lane.

Dexin had already sent someone ahead to receive him. As soon as Hu’s visiting card was presented, Liu Bingzhang admitted him to the western reception hall. Hu made the proper official salute, and Liu courteously invited him onto the kang. On ordinary visits they had sat together there, but today Hu repeatedly declined. Dexin would be coming shortly; if Hu occupied the kang, Dexin could only take the chair to the east, which would slight him. Hu therefore sat in the western chair, leaving the place of honor for Dexin. At such a time there was no need for small talk. Hu came straight to the point. “I never imagined I should reach this position, Your Excellency. I cannot settle my public or private debts and must ask your help.” “Please, there is no need to put it so strongly. These are difficult times. Our first concern must be to sustain trade and reassure the public. Whatever small service I can render you within that purpose, Mr Xueyan, is part of my duty.” Just then a voice outside announced, “His Excellency De!” Liu rose, while Hu went to the doorway to meet him. An attendant lifted the curtain. Dexin entered, first saluted Liu, then turned to Hu. “Mr Xueyan, please sit over here.” He took Hu’s former seat, leaving the place of honor to him. “No, no! That is your place, Mr Xiaofeng.” They exchanged courtesies for some time before Liu intervened. “No need to dispute over details. Sit on this side, Mr Xueyan. It will be easier to talk.” Hu finally took the eastern chair, nearer Liu, and asked Dexin opposite him, “I have brought the accounts. Shall I present them to His Excellency now?” “Yes, yes. I think you should.”

Hu rose, lifted the thick stack of account books placed beside him with both hands, and carried them to the kang. Dexin stood too and helped check them as they were handed over. There were six books in all. The first was the general ledger of Fukang Bank and all its branches. The second listed the managers and capital of the twenty-nine pawnshops. The third gave the locations and grades of all eleven thousand mu of land. The fourth inventoried the quantities and locations of the silk and cocoons in stock. The fourth listed miscellaneous property, including Hu Qingyu Hall. The fifth was an inventory of personal possessions, and the sixth a register of depositors. Notes issued by the various banks, however, appeared in the first book. Liu merely leafed through them and put them down. Once Hu and Dexin had resumed their seats, he asked, “What is the overall balance of these six books?” “On paper, there is far more coming to me than I owe. But what I owe is a definite sum; what is due to me is much less certain.” “‘What is due’ includes the goods,” Dexin explained. “Because of his struggle with the foreign merchants, Mr Xueyan may eventually have to sell his silk and cocoons by auction. How much of their cost he can recover is difficult to say.”

“What is an auction?” “A foreign practice,” Hu replied. “Those interested bid against one another. The bidding starts at a reserve price. So long as two people keep offering more, the price rises. When nobody bids further, the person presiding strikes a block of wood, like a magistrate’s desk block, and the sale is settled.” “Then the foreigners could agree among themselves and deliberately refrain from competing.” “They might not merely refuse to compete; they might refuse to bid at all. Then the reserve would have to be lowered.” “In that case, there is no way to estimate what your silk and cocoons are worth?” “Exactly.” “Difficult.” Liu turned to Dexin. “How do you think we should proceed, Mr Xiaofeng?” “Settle the public claims before the private ones, step by step.” “There seems no alternative. We do not yet know the court’s intentions. For now, I can only do my best to hold everything in safekeeping.” He added, “You won’t be leaving Hangzhou for the time being, will you?”

The question carried an implication that Hu’s movements were to be watched. After a moment’s thought, he decided to tell the truth. “Your Excellency, I should like to go to Shanghai. If I can prevent the silk and cocoons from being auctioned, it will benefit both the public and private interests involved.” “How long would you be away?” “At least half a month.” Liu nodded slightly, his eyes turning towards Dexin as if casually, though their expression was wary and inquiring. Then he looked back at Hu. “Suppose something arises during that half-month which requires me to speak to you in person. What then?” Hu had not considered this. He was momentarily nonplussed, unable to answer, but Dexin spoke for him. “If that happens, Your Excellency, please let me know.” “Very well!” Liu agreed readily. “Go about your business, Mr Xueyan. I only hope you can bring some order to matters within that half-month. So long as no public money is lost and private creditors make no trouble, why should I interfere?” “Yes, yes.” Hu rose, hands lowered, and bowed. “I am deeply indebted to Your Excellency’s kindness.” “Please, please.” As he spoke, Liu’s hand moved towards his teacup.
