HU XUEYAN
By Gao Yang

INSTALLMENT 167
Vanishing into Thin Air — Chapter Six, Part Two (conclusion)

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

The orderly at the doorway immediately raised the curtain and called out, “See the guest out!” Once Hu Xueyan had gone, Liu Bingzhang returned to his private office and took out the register of expectant county magistrates whom the Ministry of Personnel had assigned to Zhejiang. He examined it carefully. Besides each man’s name, age, native place, qualifications, and date of arrival in the province, the register contained two further kinds of information. One recorded previous assignments: an investigation undertaken in such-and-such a month and year, for instance, or the escorting of revenue silver to the capital. The other recorded his connections, in Liu’s own hand: a cousin of a certain grand secretary, or recommended by a particular minister in a letter of a certain date. Liu now had assignments for twenty-nine expectant magistrates and would make his choices according to four criteria.

The first was qualifications, with priority going to men who had entered through the regular examination route. A newly successful jinshi designated for immediate appointment, but still unable to obtain a vacancy or even an acting post, would naturally receive one of these assignments without further consideration. Liu found only three such men in the register and marked their names, leaving twenty-six places to fill. After the jinshi, who had passed both the provincial and metropolitan examinations, provincial graduates naturally enjoyed an advantage over men recommended for military merit or admitted by purchase. But this depended on the second criterion: their connections. A letter of recommendation from a senior figure at court would certainly place a man among the candidates, though the third criterion still had to be considered. Had he received an assignment recently, and had it been onerous or lucrative? A man recently given an onerous task might be considered in the interests of balancing things out; otherwise, he would have to wait. It took considerable effort to complete the list. Liu then sent orderlies to notify the men individually that they were to await an audience at the governor’s office the next morning. A copy of the full list went to Dexin for his reference.

The twenty-nine expectant department and county magistrates dared not neglect the summons. Early the next morning, with their formal visiting slips ready, they assembled in the waiting hall at the governor’s office. It happened to be a day for the regular official audience. Liu received the provincial treasurer and judicial commissioner, the salt and inspection circuit intendants, and the prefect and county magistrate of the provincial capital—the prefect of Hangzhou and the magistrate of Qiantang. It was noon before the expectant magistrates were admitted, with Dexin also present. County magistrates were ordinarily offered seats when they saw the governor, but there were too many men and too few chairs. The attendants brought in several long benches, and the twenty-nine “Your Honours” squeezed together. Even so, two men could find no room. One withdrew sulkily to the corridor to hear what happened; the other, more practised in ingratiating himself with his superiors, noticed that Liu was waiting for him to settle before beginning. Embarrassed to keep His Excellency waiting, he squatted down with his hands on his knees and his backside unsupported, as though he too were seated. “I have invited you gentlemen here because there is an assignment I wish each of you to undertake,” Liu said. “You have presumably read in the Shenbao that Circuit Intendant Hu’s Fukang Bank has failed, seriously disturbing the markets. Its deposits include a great deal of public money, which must be secured. Intendant Hu has voluntarily offered the twenty-nine pawnshops he owns for me to take into official custody as security for those funds. I now ask each of you to take charge of one.”

Everyone was surprised, but no one dared ask a question. Only the man suspended in his squat, whose legs had become unbearably painful, seized the chance to rise and stretch them under cover of speaking. “Your Excellency, no one has ever undertaken an assignment of this kind. I do not know how it is to be carried out.” “Oh?” Liu looked at him. “What is your surname, sir?” “Ma, Your Excellency.” “His name is Ma Fengshi, from Shaanxi. He arrived in the province only recently,” Dexin prompted quietly. Liu nodded. “Mr Ma is right. This is my first encounter with such an assignment too. But people are not born knowing everything. Have none of you considered that, when you eventually retire, you might open a pawnshop to support yourselves? Taking over a pawnshop is much the same as opening one. It amounts to verifying the capital and examining the accounts.” “May I ask further,” Ma said, “whether we are to seal the premises after verifying the capital and accounts?” “No, no. If the capital and accounts are found to be in order, hold the manager responsible for continuing the business as before. You need only obtain his written undertaking acknowledging the amount of capital held by the establishment, and your assignment will be complete.” So, though it was called sequestration, the premises were to be inspected rather than closed. Dexin then supervised the drawing of lots. Ma happened to draw Gongji Pawnshop, the principal establishment.

It was already about three in the afternoon, too late to carry out the inspection that day. Ma returned home with his official papers and sat alone, staring into space. A pawnshop meant both accounts and pledged articles. The accounts would be written in a flying scrawl harder to decipher than the cursive calligraphy of Zhang Xu or Huaisu; the pledges could include anything under the sun. How was he to inspect and secure it all single-handed, with no assistance? Gongji, being the principal shop, must be a large concern. If he failed to keep an eye on everything and fraudulent claims or concealed removals occurred during the takeover, his responsibility would be serious. His worried expression puzzled his wife. He had set off for the governor’s office so eagerly that morning, saying he had an assignment. It had looked as though they would be able to get through the New Year after all. Why had he returned in such a state?

She had to ask. Ma sighed. “They have given me a task I have never done before: taking the God of Wealth Hu’s Gongji Pawnshop into official custody. I am only to examine the accounts and verify the capital; the shop is to remain open. Think of it—I cannot even use an abacus. How am I to manage?” His wife saw it differently. “Since we came to Zhejiang to wait for an appointment, you have had only that one assignment escorting revenue silver. We have lived by pawning things, and those pawnbrokers have given us such sour looks!” she said delightedly. “Who would have thought you would get this assignment? Now I can get a little of my own back.” Ma broke into a smile. “A woman’s view, indeed! This task offers no benefit, only a chance of trouble.” “How could it bring trouble?” “Examining accounts and verifying capital! If there has been any tampering, the responsibility will fall on my head. I shall have to bear all the consequences.” “I do not understand what you mean.” She thought for a moment. “Why not ask Brother Yang’s advice?”

This reminded Ma of a possibility. “Brother Yang” was a clerk in the rites section of the Renhe county office and lived nearby. Ma and his wife were easygoing people who did not look down on his position, always addressing him warmly as Brother Yang. Flattered by their kindness, the clerk did what he could for Ma. His busiest time came each year when the education commissioner posted the results of the qualifying examination. When the sons of wealthy country families won their first degree, their families gave celebratory banquets and hoped to attract a few distinguished guests with examination honours to lend the occasion prestige. Clerk Yang would notify Ma, who put on his official robes and attended. Besides being well fed and supplied with wine, he received a red envelope from the host containing at least two taels. There were usually seven or eight such opportunities a year, so Ma owed Yang a good deal. At his wife’s suggestion his face brightened, and he sent his son to invite “Uncle Yang.” The clerk happened to have no engagement that day and came at once, asking what was the matter. “I have an assignment I do not know how to carry out. My wife had the good sense to suggest asking your advice, Brother Yang.” “Oh, Your Honour”—Yang observed the proper form of address—“what sort of assignment?” “Taking a pawnshop into official custody.” Yang was startled, then smiled. “Congratulations! Congratulations! Your Honour, you will have a fat New Year.”

Ma’s expression changed at once. Surprised and delighted, he asked, “Brother Yang, what do you mean?” “First let me ask: is it one of Mr Hu’s pawnshops?” “Yes.” “Which one?” “Gongji.” “Ha! Then your New Year will be fatter still.” Ma grew happier and more puzzled. Scratching his head, he said, “I can see the food, but I cannot get it into my mouth.” “What do you mean?” Understanding immediately dawned on Yang’s face. “Ah, Your Honour, you mean you do not know how to begin?” “Exactly.” Ma added at once, “If there is to be any fat, let us all share it. Brother Yang, you are Zhuge Liang, and I am Master Liu.” “You flatter me! Let me think. There is a friend who could certainly help.” “Can this friend of yours be found today? You see, I have to begin first thing tomorrow.” The friend Yang had in mind was Zhou Shaotang. Since his performance outside Fukang had relieved Hu’s predicament, Zhou’s reputation had grown considerably. Ma had heard of him and was very willing to seek his advice; he was only afraid there would not be time, since the takeover had to begin the following morning. “Do not worry.” Yang looked at the sky. “This is just the right time. He will be drinking at Longhe Tavern, by the entrance to Dajing Lane.” Dajing Lane lay at the foot of City God Hill and contained a very large well with sweet water. Longhe Tavern stood opposite it. Zhou went there for a drink every evening; even when invited to dinner elsewhere, he always put in an appearance first. If they went now, they could find out where he was even if he had already left.

They walked leisurely to Longhe. The lamps had just been lit, and business was getting under way. Hawkers and labourers drinking at the counter leaned there at their ease, each with a bowl. Many knew Clerk Yang and greeted him. Answering as he went, Yang made his way inside to an open hall containing more than a dozen square tables, about seven-tenths of them occupied. He stopped to look, saw no sign of Zhou, and caught a waiter to ask. “Mr Zhou has been and gone, but he will be back shortly. Would you like to wait or leave a message?” “I shall wait.” They chose a table in a quiet corner, ordered wine, and drank slowly. Zhou arrived as they were on their third bowl. “Shaotang! Shaotang!” Yang rose and drew him over. “We have been waiting ages. Let me introduce you. This is Magistrate Ma.” Zhou was adept in company and spent some time exchanging courtesies with Ma. Only when the wine had put them pleasantly at ease did Yang explain why they had come. Ma immediately raised his bowl in a toast. “I know nothing whatever about pawnshops. Now I have accepted this assignment, I have no idea how to carry it out. I must rely entirely on your guidance, Mr Zhou.” “You are very kind,” Zhou replied, while working things out in his mind. He and Gongji’s Tang Zishao were merely nodding acquaintances, but he knew Xie Yunqing of Fukang well and had seen a great deal more of him lately. From Xie he had learned quite a few secrets about neighbouring Gongji—and those, of course, were Tang’s secrets too.

Zhou had a very low opinion of Tang. His own friendship with Hu dated from their days of poverty, which gave it a particular depth. Now that Hu had suffered such a heavy blow, Tang ought to have remembered all the help he had received and shown enough conscience to stand by him. Instead, he was taking advantage of Hu’s distress, manipulating Gongji’s affairs and secretly embezzling its funds. Zhou bitterly resented it. Here was an opportunity to have Ma examine the accounts; how could he let it pass? “Your Honour, people say I talk big and never do anything properly. So I shall not say much about taking Gongji into custody. I have just one piece of advice. Understand it thoroughly, and I guarantee you will carry out the assignment splendidly.” He paused. “It is this: ‘Better to inspect the storeroom than read the books; better to examine the goods than verify the capital.’” Ma was taken aback. Zhou’s opening had been abrupt and sounded rather like a complaint. “Mr Zhou,” he stammered, “this is our first meeting. I have never said anything of the kind...” “Ah, a misunderstanding! I did not mean you, Your Honour, or Brother Yang. Someone happened to say that about me today, so I mentioned it in passing.” Zhou continued, “Did you not ask for guidance? Those two phrases point out the central thread. You must see it clearly and think it through.” His almost aggrieved opening had merely been a device to seize the other man’s attention. After this exchange, those twelve Chinese characters were firmly imprinted on Ma’s mind. Looking duly receptive, he said, “Mr Zhou, even on the surface those words contain a great deal of learning. Please go further and explain them plainly.”

“You are too kind,” Zhou said. “Do you understand pawnshop practices, Your Honour?” “Did I not say I knew nothing about them?” “Then it is no wonder...” “Old Zhou,” Yang interrupted impatiently, “stop leading us on a grand funeral procession round the city walls. His Honour has to take charge tomorrow. Tell him plainly what to watch for.” “Yes,” Ma added. “And how should I begin when I arrive?” Zhou reflected that Hu’s pawnshops were being taken into custody to secure the public money deposited with Fukang. Every additional sum preserved would reduce Hu’s liability. Recovering what Tang had embezzled from Gongji would therefore be the most direct and practical help he could offer Hu. He decided to intervene. “Have you an official warrant for this assignment?” “Yes. The instructions came from the governor, but the warrant was issued by the treasurer.” “That makes no difference. Both are your superiors. Have you collected any sealing strips?” “Yes.” “How many?” “Two.” “Why only two?” “I thought taking the premises into custody meant sealing the front and back doors, so I took only two. Afterwards I realised that could not be right. The governor said the shop was to continue allowing customers to redeem their pledges. If I sealed the doors, how could they come in?” “Nor could Gongji’s own people get in or out.” Zhou thought for a moment. “But that does not matter. Have an oblong stamp cut today, bearing the words: ‘By order of His Excellency: Commissioner Ma, Expectant County Magistrate, appointed to take Gongji Pawnshop into official custody.’ With that stamp, you can apply seals yourself.”

“Yes, yes.” Ma nodded thoughtfully. “I ought to have that authority.” “Of course you do.” “Mr Zhou, what should I do first tomorrow, and what should I do next? Please tell me.” “Well, that depends on what we find. It is difficult to say now.” Zhou glanced at Yang. The clerk, who had been listening calmly, knew his turn had come. “Your Honour, I think you should ask Shaotang to help you.” “Yes, indeed!” Ma answered eagerly. “I had just that in mind, but I did not know whether it would conform to official practice.” “Why should it not? If you received a substantive appointment and opened your own office, you would engage at least two secretaries, one for legal affairs and one for revenue. Asking Shaotang to help now is the same sort of thing.” “Quite so! An excellent comparison.” Ma raised his bowl with both hands. “Mr Zhou, please help me. I am ashamed to say I cannot yet offer any token of appreciation—only my heartfelt gratitude.” They then agreed on several steps, though in fact Zhou was issuing the instructions. Ma was to have the oblong stamp cut that night and reach Gongji before business began the next morning. His first action would be to post a notice: “Taken into official custody by order of His Excellency. Business suspended for one day.” They would then divide up the inspection, concentrating above all on the storeroom and the silver chest. “Then we shall examine the books. Why did I say it was better to inspect the storeroom than read the books, and to examine the goods than verify the capital? Because the books are lifeless things; you cannot see much from the figures alone. Inspect the storeroom first, then compare it with the accounts, and truth, falsehood, and malpractice will be clear at a glance.”

“Yes, yes. What sort of malpractice has this Tang been guilty of, Mr Zhou?” Ma asked. “I have only heard reports. We shall not know exactly until we go tomorrow. The first kind concerns pledges whose redemption period has expired. If the loan is small and the article valuable, there are two tricks he can play. One is substitution: replacing a heavy fur lining with one of lighter fur, for example. The other...” “A moment, Mr Zhou. How do we detect that first trick?” “Easily. One way is to read the accounts, but pawnshop books always describe good things as poor ones, so valuation is better. A pawnbroker’s skill lies in judging and valuing goods; he will not get it wrong. Heavy fur commands one price and lighter fur another. Compare similar articles and ask why, if the loan amounts are the same, one pledge is heavy fur and the other light. If he cannot explain it, something is wrong.” “I understand. And the other trick?” “He says the pledge has been redeemed, when in fact he has appropriated the valuable article after its term expired. That is not difficult to detect either. Ask him to produce the original pawn ticket cancelled on redemption. If he has it, the pledge was genuinely redeemed. If not, the customer never came to redeem it at all.”

Ma now had a general understanding of the abuses involved in handling expired pledges. But were there others? Zhou’s answer was emphatically yes, and his voice and expression showed considerable indignation. “This Tang is worse than a beast! To plunder his employer at a time like this—he has no conscience left.” Since Fukang’s troubles began, Tang had believed Hu’s collapse only a matter of time. Gongji could not survive it either. He might as well seize what he could while he was still free to do as he pleased. “His method is vicious, though once explained it is worth nothing. He brings in a broken brass watch, treats it as a gold one, and advances eight or ten taels on it. Your Honour, is that not outright robbery?” “Abominable!” Ma too looked righteously indignant. “Tampering with expired pledges might at least be called sharp practice: after all, the proprietor has recovered his principal and interest and merely loses an additional profit. But passing off false goods as genuine and cheap goods as valuable to defraud the proprietor is an offence that can be severely punished.” “And severely punished it should be.” Zhou gave a cold laugh. “He thinks himself clever. The fake goods would not be discovered until the pledges expired without being redeemed and the storeroom was checked. By then he would be back home in Huizhou. Even if you prosecuted him, he could deny it: the watch had originally been gold, and he had no idea how it had been switched. He never expected to run into you and me, Your Honour. Before the pledges expire, we shall get to the bottom of it. As the saying goes, ‘A man may make a thousand calculations; Heaven needs only one.’”

Yang spoke up. “Tang must have an accomplice. Pawnbrokers value their reputations. If a man became known for conspiring with Tang to cheat his employer, no one would engage him again; he would have to change his trade.” “A good question, Old Yang. Tang certainly has an accomplice, but the relationship is a special one: his sister’s son, whom he has advanced entirely through his own efforts.” “Ah, that explains it. If Tang is preparing to pack up and leave, he will naturally take the nephew with him.” “Exactly.” Zhou turned to Ma. “Tomorrow you must get at the truth through Tang’s nephew. Pay particular attention to the recent entries. Select several of the largest advances and compare the pledged goods with the accounts. If the goods do not match the entries, ask who handled the transactions. That is all you need do at first.” “You mean I should not pursue the matter on the spot?” “Exactly. If you ask then, Tang will have some plausible story ready. Alerting him before you are prepared is hardly wise.” “What would be wise, then?” “Take his nephew away and question him elsewhere. These youngsters cannot stand a fright; frighten them and they tell you everything.” Zhou added, “Better still, borrow two runners from the county office and take them with you. You will look more imposing, and it will make the work easier.” “Yes, that is easy. I know Magistrate Wang of Renhe well.” The more Ma heard, the keener he became. “And after we have the answers? Do we summon Tang for questioning?”

“You will not need to summon him. He will come to your house and ask to see you.” “What makes you so sure?” “Well...” Zhou hesitated. “Let me have a word with Old Yang first.” Drawing the clerk aside, he quietly asked about his relationship with Ma. Yang told him truthfully, and Zhou asked another question. “The New Year is approaching. His Honour will naturally want to find a little money to see him through it?” “Of course. You mean he should squeeze something out of Tang?” “Exactly. But both public and private interests must be considered. He can give Tang two conditions. First, Tang must redeem the pledged articles himself: that protects the public interest. Second, he must provide a few taels for the New Year. His Honour can negotiate the amount with Tang—or you can. If Tang refuses, report him and let him face prosecution.” Yang considered it, decided the scheme was workable, and smiled. “You really are a good friend to Mr Hu.” “One must not forget the friends of one’s poverty,” Zhou replied, dropping into a literary phrase. The quotation was not altogether appropriate, but neither could one say it made no sense.

As they discussed the matter further, a change came about naturally: the task of assisting Ma passed from Zhou to Yang. Zhou would still support them from behind the scenes. They agreed that he would sit drinking tea at Anli Tea House, opposite Fukang Bank. Gongji was only a step away, so they could contact him whenever necessary. Returning to the table together, Zhou explained, “Your Honour, you and Clerk Yang know each other well. Ask him to accompany you tomorrow; it will be easier to discuss anything that arises. I have told him the essential points, and this assignment will certainly go well.” He then rose, said goodbye, and left. By now lights shone from every house, and drinkers were streaming into the tavern. It was extremely noisy. If they spoke softly they could not be heard; if loudly, they risked revealing their secrets. Yang suggested going elsewhere for another drink. “Where?” “Come with me. But I must say this first: when we get there, I cannot address you by your title. Calling you ‘Your Honour’ would give you away.” “Never mind. Call me Old Ma.” “Better not use your real surname either. What is your honoured mother’s surname?” “Li.” “Then I shall call you Old Li. It is not far. Let us walk.”
