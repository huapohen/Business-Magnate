Vanishing into Thin Air — Chapter Seven, Part One

CHAPTER SEVEN: THE MASS SEQUESTRATION OF THE PAWNSHOPS

Clerk Yang paid the bill and led Ma Fengshi across two streets into a winding lane. At its end stood a house with both doors shut. An oil lamp beside the entrance cast a feeble light on a faded rose-red slip of paper bearing the words “Sun Residence.” “What is this place?” Ma asked uneasily. “Ma…” Yang checked himself. “Old Li, you must not tell Mrs Li about this place.” Ma said no more. Yang knocked three times quickly and three times slowly. As he finished, the door creaked open. A middle-aged woman, still attractive, held up a hand lantern. “I wondered who it was. You! I thought it was about time you came.” Her face filled with smiles. “And this gentleman is…?” “Mr Li,” Yang answered, then asked at once, “Any guests upstairs?” “No.” “Downstairs?” “Old Zhu from Qingyu Hall is drinking with some friends. They will be leaving soon.” “His employer is in trouble, yet he still has a mind for courtesan parties! Do not tell him I am here.” “As if I needed reminding!” The woman turned to “Mr Li.” “Come with me, please. Mind your step.” The three of them went up the stairs beside the main room. Upstairs were three rooms, one large and two small. In the central room, once their hostess had brightened the lamp, Yang introduced her. “Her surname is Sun. You can call her Auntie Sun.” Ma now understood: this was what Hangzhou people called a private brothel, and Auntie Sun was its madam. Smiling broadly, he said, “You must have a great many daughters, Auntie Sun?” “Indeed I do.” She turned to Yang. “Tea first, or wine?” “Both—and we shall want a meal as well.” He drew her into the outer room and returned some time later.

“Auntie Sun may be past her first youth, but she still has plenty of charm,” Ma remarked. “What? Have you taken a fancy to her? I shall be your matchmaker.” “Enough of that! Let us discuss our business first.” This suited Yang’s arrangements perfectly. He had instructed Auntie Sun to prepare good wine and a good meal, but to take her time, so he could settle tomorrow’s business with Ma before they drank freely. “Leave the rest of your business to me. First, how much do you owe at the year’s end?” Ma was taken aback. After a rough calculation, he said, “I shall need fifty or sixty taels to get through it.” “I understand. I shall go with you tomorrow. Once we reach Gongji Pawnshop, take your cue from my eyes.” What exactly did that mean? Ma pondered it for a while. “Brother Yang…” “Wait!” Yang cut him short. “You must not address me like that at Gongji tomorrow.” “I understand. When holding an office, observe its forms. I shall call you ‘Clerk Yang’ in my grandest official manner. Do not take offence.” “Of course not. We are only putting on a performance.” “You are the leading player. Do you know Tang Zishao?” “Naturally, though we are not close.” “Good. Tomorrow I shall play the stern red face and you the kindly white. If anything is wrong, I shall speak with official severity and you can smooth matters over. Tang will naturally turn to you. Whether something can be overlooked or must not be, I shall follow the tone you take.” “Exactly!” Yang said with satisfaction. “We shall give a fine performance of Returning to Court in Triumph.”

Footsteps sounded on the stairs. The curtain lifted, and Auntie Sun entered, followed by a maid carrying a large tray with four dishes and two sets of cups and chopsticks. “Why only two?” Yang asked. “I thought you had business to discuss and would not want to be disturbed.” “We have finished. Bring two more sets. Is Qiaozhen here?” “She has not come today. A’lan is here, though. I do not know whether Mr Li would care for her.” Noticing that Ma could not take his eyes off Auntie Sun, Yang decided to bring about a brief liaison between them. “We shall see later,” he said. “Have a couple of cups with us first.” More cups and chopsticks were brought. Auntie Sun served the guests attentively, and Ma kept praising the wine and food. Yang merely smiled. When he thought the time was right, he asked, “Old Zhu from Qingyu Hall has not left yet?” “Not yet.” “I shall go down and see him.” Rising, Yang told Auntie Sun, “Keep Mr Li company over a few more cups. He is a good friend of mine. You must treat him especially well.” He went downstairs and had an attendant announce him. Old Zhu came out, his face flushed scarlet. “Yang! Come in for a drink!” he said, taking his hand. “Would it be convenient?” “Certainly. Everyone is either a friend of yours or a friend of mine.” Inside, Yang knew three of the four men. On asking the fourth man’s name, he learned that he was Zhu’s colleague. Yang had joined the party partly to leave Ma an opportunity to make advances to Auntie Sun and partly to find out how Qingyu Hall was faring. What especially puzzled him was how Zhu could be enjoying himself so heartily when all Hu Xueyan’s businesses were in peril.

After the greetings and a round of drinks, someone mentioned the crisis at Fukang. It was the great news of the day throughout the country, discussed at almost every social gathering. Yang listened quietly until there was a pause. “Zhu, what exactly is your job at Qingyu Hall?” “I handle inspections.” “Inspections of what? The quality of the goods? You did not start in the medicinal-herb trade. You cannot tell whether the ingredients come from the proper sources.” “I may not know their quality, but I can read a weight! Once the experienced men pronounce the herbs genuine, they call me to supervise the weighing. My most important duty, though, is preventing goods from being stolen or spirited away.” “Have you ever caught anyone?” “Of course, but not many.” “You say not many. Perhaps goods have disappeared without your knowing.” “No.” Zhu paused. “To be frank, even if you urged the men to steal, they would refuse. Think of it: their rice bowls may not be gold or silver, but they are at least iron, unbreakable for a lifetime. Besides wages, there are bonuses. When summer epidemics bring customers queuing for medicines, there is an extra allowance. If an adult or child at home falls ill, medicines—pills, powders, pastes or pellets—are free, however costly. They do pay for tonic pastes and medicinal wines, but at less than cost. Steal a root or two of ginseng out of greed, and all those benefits vanish. Would that be worth it?”

“You have a point. But this time the whole thing may be uprooted!” “You mean Mr Hu’s businesses may not survive? I cannot speak for the others, but Qingyu Hall is certain to remain.” “Why?” “It has protection,” Zhu replied calmly. “When the trouble at Fukang began, our manager told us that Qingyu Hall was the best of all Mr Hu’s enterprises. It earns money and has earned him a reputation too. Suppose they say there is a shortage of public funds and seal Qingyu Hall to cover the debt. They can confiscate the stock and equipment, but they cannot take away its signboard. Qingyu Hall is only the short name. The full name is Hu Qingyu Hall. If the owner is no longer a Hu, how can they keep using that sign? So the authorities are certain not to seal it. They will let Mr Hu remain the owner. We must all work especially hard, dispense genuine medicines and treat customers courteously. Then our rice bowls will be secure. There is no need to worry.”

Yang was deeply moved by what he heard. With enterprises on so vast a scale, Hu must have trained more capable men than anyone could count. But if, at the beginning, he sought only assistants who would make money for him by whatever means, the result would be a Mi Benchang or a Tang Zishao. As the water rose, so did the boat: an apprentice became an employee, an employee became a manager. Then the manager would think, “How did you become the proprietor? When I was an apprentice, I emptied your chamber pot and rinsed your water pipe with hot water. I raised you up step by step. How long must I remain an employee? I want to be a proprietor myself.” Once that thought took hold, he ceased to be a true manager. First came private trading on the side. If a profitable transaction presented itself, he took it for himself. Someone might offer a consignment he knew would make money; he would find fault with it in every way, then openly or indirectly suggest another place where a deal might be struck. That place would be his own secret business. Next, he would watch for talent, privately cultivating promising employees and apprentices so that, when he could establish himself, he would have his staff ready. Finally came drawing away customers. There were many methods, but one thing was certain: to win them over, he would never speak well of his present employer. Otherwise, why would they change firms? A selfish manager who decided to set up on his own would inevitably profit at his employer’s expense. Even if the employer had trained him from the start, he would feel no ingratitude. He had made money for the man and believed that debt was already paid.

The reason Qingyu Hall’s manager could be so sincere and understanding, doing everything to preserve its reputation and one part of Hu’s business, was not difficult to imagine. Hu had founded the medicine shop because the troops on the western campaign required enormous quantities of prepared medicines and herbs. Buying them elsewhere was expensive and did not always ensure timely delivery. Responsible for the supply administration behind the army, he had to calculate carefully. A large shop of his own would save money, save trouble and make supply easier. He had not intended to earn a profit. Later, genuine ingredients and effective preparations brought flourishing trade and substantial earnings. But apart from profits reinvested to expand the business, the surplus paid for free medicines and treatment for the poor, and for large donations of prepared remedies during floods, droughts, famines and epidemics. Hu had never taken a single cash from Qingyu Hall for his own use.

Because his original purpose was entirely public-spirited, he had sought a different sort of manager. Honesty came first. Just inside the shop, above the place where customers waited and rested, hung a black-lacquered couplet in gold: “Though none may see your medicines prepared, Heaven knows the intentions in your heart.” A dishonest medicine seller, especially one selling ready-made preparations, could harm people by using poor ingredients or insufficient quantities. Compassion came next. A physician must have the heart to sacrifice his own flesh for another; a medicine seller should be the same. Only by constantly thinking of the sick could he constantly attend to quality. Finally, the man must be capable, or honesty and compassion might merely make him easy to deceive. Such a manager, free from concern for the proprietor’s personal profit, would devote himself wholeheartedly to the business. An unselfish employer would not awaken selfishness in him; generous treatment removed any need for it. Examined closely, Qingyu Hall’s escape from Fukang’s troubles exemplified the enduring truth that good causes bear good fruit. Yang felt a spontaneous admiration for its manager, whose name he did not even know. Over their drinks he asked Zhu about the man’s history. He learned that his surname was Ye and that a relative of Hu’s named Liu had originally found him. What he heard of Ye’s character and ability broadly confirmed his deductions, making Yang still more pleased with himself.

“Your manager has done right by Mr Hu and by himself,” Yang said. “Not like that black-hearted Tang Zishao at Gongji. I think he will soon find himself facing charges.” “What?” Zhu asked. “Where did you hear that?” Only then did Yang realise that wine had loosened his tongue. Naturally, he would say no more. After some evasive replies, he returned upstairs. Ma and Auntie Sun were still drinking and talking. Their manner remained decorous, but they were plainly getting on very well. Yang joked, “Old Li, do not go home tonight.” “Whom are you speaking to?” Auntie Sun asked, glancing across. Before Yang could answer, Ma laughed. There was something behind that laugh, so Yang held his tongue. “He is Magistrate Ma. Why did you call him Mr Li?” Auntie Sun demanded. “Why tell a lie?” “I am sorry,” Ma apologised to Yang. “She said I did not look like a businessman and asked where I had acquired my official manner, so I told her the truth.” “The truth? What truth? Anything besides who you are?” “Nothing else.” Somewhat reassured, Yang turned to Auntie Sun. “You must understand the importance of this. Do not tell people Magistrate Ma has been here for pleasure.”

“What is there to hide? Even a circuit intendant has come here to drink.” “Do not argue. If you want me to keep bringing friends, listen to me.” Yang continued, “We must go now. Magistrate Ma has official business tomorrow. We shall come another day.” “When?” Auntie Sun asked. “Tomorrow?” “Tomorrow may still be impossible,” Ma answered for himself. “I shall come as soon as my business is finished.” “The rectangular stamp has not been made,” Yang said. “We shall find nobody to do it tonight. I shall go to an engraver first thing tomorrow. It will be noon before everything is ready. We had better settle on taking the shop into official custody at lunchtime.” “Very well. I leave everything to you and shall wait for word at home.” They rose and left together. On the way, Yang could not help reproaching Ma, but Ma apologised so persistently that he could hardly say more.

The next day was one on which attendance had to be marked. As usual, Yang went to sit in the rites section, so that if the county magistrate had questions about dealings with the education official, he would be on hand to answer the roll. An engraver was readily available there. Yang summoned one and handed him a slip bearing Ma’s temporary title. In less than the time it took to eat a meal, the stamp was cut and brought back. Finding nothing else to do, Yang went home to prepare to accompany Ma to Gongji. The moment he entered the main room, he saw a ham and four boxes of different sizes piled on the central table. By the door stood a fifty-jin jar of Huadiao wine. Someone had sent a gift. “A’mao’s mother!” he called. A’mao was his son’s childhood name; he was calling his wife. She came at once and spoke before he could. “Here is a card. The man is Tang from Gongji Pawnshop. We have no dealings with him, so I dared not touch the gifts.” She handed him the card. It was indeed Tang Zishao’s. After a moment’s thought, Yang asked, “What did he say?” “That he would come back shortly. He seemed hesitant, as though he had something to say but would not say it.” “I understand. We cannot accept these gifts.” Yang sat drinking tea and thinking. Tang’s purpose was obvious. What puzzled him was how the man had learned so quickly that Yang would accompany Ma to take Gongji into official custody. Had he already visited Ma and been told to come here? If so, Yang ought to see Ma first and find out what they had discussed before deciding how to act.

While he was considering this, someone knocked. Yang went to answer the door himself. He and Tang were nodding acquaintances from social gatherings. Sure enough, Tang stood outside, and Yang had to make a show of surprise. “You did not expect me, did you, Mr Yang?” Tang asked with a broad smile. “Indeed I did not. Please come in.” Leading him into the main room, Yang pointed at the table. “Master Tang, I cannot accept a reward I have not earned. I certainly shall not accept these gifts.” Tang seemed to have expected this response and took it lightly. “A small matter. We can discuss it in due course.” His composure made Yang wary. “Please sit.” He did not call for tea, but sat stiffly upright in the lower seat, signalling that he did not intend a long conversation. “Mr Yang, I hear you are to accompany Magistrate Ma to take Gongji into official custody?” Faced with this direct question, Yang was unwilling to admit it outright. “Who told you, Master Tang?” “The news reached me indirectly.” If it was indirect news, he had not met Ma. There were only two possible sources: Zhou Shaotang or Old Zhu from Qingyu Hall. On reflection, the latter seemed more likely. “Did you hear it through Qingyu Hall?” That was itself an admission of the matter. Tang nodded. “Yes.”

“So you came only to find out about that?” “There are other things I should like to ask you, of course. When do you intend to come? I should like to be ready to receive you.” “You are too courteous. You must ask Magistrate Ma about that.” Finding the conversation unpromising, Tang could not disclose his real purpose. Yet he would not take back the gifts, which were by no means trifling. Yang could do nothing about it. He now felt uncomfortably indebted, and feared it would be harder to take a firm line when assisting Ma at Gongji. As Tang rose to leave, he suddenly asked, “Mr Yang, may I ask whether you know Zhou Shaotang well?” Yang considered, then answered simply, “Well.” “And how well does he know Magistrate Ma?” This question suggested extensive connections and many sources of information. Perhaps somebody had already told Tang that Yang, Ma and Zhou had met in the wine shop the previous day. To answer “I am not sure” would then be a deliberate lie. If, after receiving his gifts, Yang would not answer even that small question honestly, Tang would surely sneer inwardly. Whatever happened later, it was hardly worth earning the contempt of a man like Tang now. Without quite intending to, Yang told the truth. “Not particularly well.” Tang appeared satisfied. Smiling, he said, “I have taken up your time. Once the official business is finished, I should like to invite you and Magistrate Ma for a proper gathering.”

Yang was at Ma’s house, preparing to set out for Gongji, when his maid hurried over to ask him to come home. “Madam has something urgent to discuss,” she said. While Yang hesitated, Ma spoke. “Go back first. Come straight over when you have finished. I shall wait here.” Fortunately, the houses were close. Yang decided to return briefly, only to find, to his great surprise, that Zhou was waiting for him. Clearly Zhou was the one who wanted to talk; the excuse about his wife meant he did not wish Ma to know of the visit. “Things have changed,” Zhou said. He paused. “I shall tell you frankly: Tang Zishao has been to see me.” “Oh? When?” “Just now. He found me at Fukang. There is something in what he says. If Gongji’s affairs cause a commotion, there will be another scandal, which will harm Mr Hu. And if complications arise during the proceedings to take the shop into official custody, the authorities may deal with him more severely. Both points are sound, I think. We must not smash the vessel while trying to strike the rat. That is why I came to consult you.” After a moment’s thought, Yang replied, “He came to see me first and brought gifts. The position is clear: something is wrong at Gongji, and probably something quite serious. Now you speak of sparing the vessel. Do you mean we should let him off and drop the affair?” “Would that not be letting him off too cheaply? He understands his position and has agreed to disgorge the money.” “How?” “That depends on you.” Zhou wanted Yang to accompany Ma to Gongji and examine the storeroom and accounts carefully, uncovering every irregularity. A written record would be best, but they need take no action. Yang should bring the facts back, and Zhou would negotiate with Tang himself. Yang thought this made Zhou master of the entire affair, with himself and Ma merely Zhou’s employees. Still, provided there was something in it for them, being employees did not matter.

There was no need for him to raise the point. Zhou supplied the assurance himself. “This arrangement only saves Tang from a lawsuit. If he thinks he can profit from it as well, he is dreaming. We shall follow our original plan: help Mr Hu on the one hand, and find a few taels for the three of us—you, me and Ma—to see us through the New Year on the other.” “It does not matter so much about me,” Yang said. “Ma seldom gets an assignment, and this one carries responsibility. We ought not to stint his share.” “Exactly. Tell him he need not worry.” “He trusts you to handle it. Still, it would be best to give him a ‘measurement.’” In business slang, a measurement meant a sum of money. “We do not yet know how many ‘feet of water’ there are,” Zhou replied. “How can I give him a measurement?” The depth of the water meant the total amount. How much had Tang embezzled, and how much could be recovered? They could not yet estimate it. Zhou was right not to promise a definite sum, but they could at least agree on the shares. When Yang suggested this, Zhou immediately said, “The largest share must naturally go to Mr Hu. Divide it into ten parts: six for him, two for Ma, and one each for you and me. How does that sound?” If ten thousand taels could be recovered from Tang, Yang thought, Ma would receive two thousand and he himself one thousand. That would make a very comfortable New Year. He nodded happily. “Good. Let us divide it that way.”

Since contact had already been made, Ma needed no display of official importance when he arrived at Gongji with Yang. They simply posted the prepared notice suspending business for three days, waited for the customers to leave and shut the main doors. Then they began sealing the storeroom and examining the accounts. Tang was composed at first. Seeing Ma’s mild manner and hearing Yang’s courteous words, he thought his approach through Zhou had succeeded. When he saw that the storeroom was to be sealed, his expression became less natural. On hearing that the accounts would be examined, he could no longer keep calm. “Mr Yang, a word.” Drawing Yang aside, he asked quietly, “Did you meet Zhou Shaotang at noon today?” “Yes.” “What did he say?” Yang was surprised, but his mind worked quickly. He realised that Zhou had played a ruthless move. Without betraying his feelings, he asked, “What did you tell him?” Tang had met Zhou through Xie Yunqing. He had hinted that Zhou should ask Yang and Ma not to be too exacting in their inspection of Gongji, and had promised Zhou three thousand taels to settle everything. He had also asked Xie to guarantee that, once the affair was over, every cash of the three thousand would be paid. Xie had agreed. What Tang did not know was that Zhou intended to help Hu, not to pursue his own gain. He felt indignant on Hu’s behalf, so this was not a matter of money. His ready agreement had been an empty dumpling—a promise with nothing inside. Tang had still not grasped this. He merely assumed that Zhou had kept too much for himself and Yang, dissatisfied with his share, was making difficulties. There was nothing for it but to open his purse wider.

“Mr Yang, we are all men who know the ways of society. Everything can be discussed. Do not make matters too embarrassing.” In his anxiety, Tang no longer chose his words carefully. “The New Year is coming. We all have debts to pay; I understand that. Besides the original amount, I shall give another two thousand taels. If you can settle matters with Magistrate Ma, I shall have nothing to say.” What original amount? Yang could guess, but still needed to find out. “How much was it originally?” Once he had learned the details of Tang’s dealings with Zhou, he felt deeply uncomfortable. Yang was comparatively kind-hearted. Tang looked so pitiful that he could not bear to string him along with an empty promise as Zhou had done. Yet accepting his terms was equally out of the question. “Mr Yang,” Tang said almost pleadingly, “do it for the sake of making a friend. I know your word carries enormous weight with Magistrate Ma. One word from you and he will show mercy and let me through.” Friendship gave Yang something to say. “Friendship is one thing, official business another. So long as Magistrate Ma can answer for his conduct of the assignment, I shall certainly consider our friendship. Master Tang, I promise you one thing: you will not be humiliated today. But I hope you will not obstruct official business. We can all discuss what to do after the inspection and sealing.” Tang recognised the needle hidden in the cotton. If he failed to take the hint and refused to let Ma examine the accounts, he would be obstructing official business. Ma could have him detained in the county office’s holding room. Fortunately, there was still the promise of later discussion. A sensible man did not invite immediate suffering. He would satisfy Yang first and consider the rest afterwards. “Since you put it that way, Mr Yang, I must listen. I shall follow every instruction.”

Tang now committed himself. He supplied every ledger they requested and answered every question, cooperating so fully that the examination proceeded smoothly. There were simply too many account books. That afternoon they checked only a third; finishing would take at least the whole of the following day. When Yang returned home, his wife told him, “Zhou Shaotang came. He said he would wait for you where you were drinking yesterday.” “Oh? Did he mean only me?” “Who else?” “Did he ask Ma to go too?” “He did not say.” “Very well. I shall go at once.” Yang took a written record of the findings with him to the meeting.

“How could Mr Hu escape misfortune?” Zhou said, pointing at the record. “Add up what is on this sheet alone, and the money swallowed already exceeds thirty or forty thousand taels.” “What do you intend to do?” “Make him disgorge it, of course. The amount is so large that I think I must speak to Mr Hu first.” Speaking for Ma, Yang said, “Would that be quite proper in official business?” “Why not?” Zhou countered. Yang could not explain exactly why it was improper. He merely felt resentful that Ma, officially commissioned to take charge of Gongji, should have to wait for Zhou and Hu to decide how he was to discharge his assignment. Zhou was exercising too much control. “Official business is what it is. You are an old hand at it—what is there that you do not understand?” Zhou spoke soothingly. “In any case, I shall make sure Ma can account satisfactorily for his assignment, and that the private benefit pleases him too. As for your share, it certainly will not be less than his. That goes without saying.” Naturally, Zhou’s own benefit would be no smaller than either man’s. Tang appeared to be in serious trouble. Recalling his helpless expression and desperate pleas, Yang grew troubled. “What is it?” Zhou asked. “Is something troubling you?” “How could it not? You gave him an empty dumpling, but he does not know it. He thinks everything has been settled. Now it looks as though we are deliberately making trouble for him. He has been to my house once and will certainly come again. How am I to deal with him?” “That is easy. Put it all on me.” In fact, this was the only possible answer, and Yang had already intended to use it in the end. Now that Zhou had offered the assurance himself, he accepted it wholeheartedly and ceased to worry further.
