INSTALLMENT 169
Vanishing into Thin Air — Chapter Seven, Part Two

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

The next day the inspection continued as before: polite words, unsparing eyes. Tang Zishao’s irregularities were pursued one by one to the end. His own manner, however, had changed. He seemed scarcely concerned with what Ma Fengshi and Clerk Yang were doing, merely sitting to one side and smoking his water pipe. Sometimes he rolled a paper spill between his fingers again and again until it broke; only then did he look momentarily lost, revealing how deeply his mind was at work. Shortly after noon began, Gongji served refreshments and invited Ma to rest. Tang took the opportunity to draw Yang aside. “Mr Yang, can you finish the inspection today?” “We mean to try.” “And afterward? Didn’t you say we could discuss things?” “Certainly. You had better find Zhou Shaotang. Once you have settled matters with him, I’ll answer for His Honour Ma.” Tang hesitated for some time. “I thought we had settled things already. Who could have known His Honour would come and insist on examining everything from the beginning?” His tone suggested that Yang and Zhou were working together, deliberately passing him back and forth without helping. Yang thought the misunderstanding natural enough; he might as well give him a hint. “My dear fellow, don’t indulge in wishful thinking. Before we’ve even examined the place, there’s nothing to discuss, let alone settle. Go and see him tonight. I guarantee you’ll get a result.” Tang suddenly understood. They wanted to see how much he had taken from Gongji before naming their price. Expecting to settle everything for a few thousand taels was an absurd dream. When the tree fell, the monkeys scattered. Better to take Yueru and flee far away. At worst, he would never work as a pawnbroker again; he should still have enough to live comfortably for the rest of his life. The decision formed in an instant, leaving him even less concerned with Yang and Ma, though outwardly he remained respectful. When the day’s examination ended, he invited them to dinner. Ma firmly declined, while Yang again hinted that he should find Zhou early and “discuss matters.”

Tang repeatedly agreed, without the least intention of doing so. His urgent business was to get home and consult Yueru. After briefly describing what had happened, he revealed his decision. “Of the Thirty-Six Stratagems, running away is the best. Start preparing now. We should have everything ready in three to five days, then slip away.” Yueru stared. “Where to? I won’t go to Huizhou.” Tang’s first wife remained in his native Huizhou. Yueru would rather die than go there to take a subordinate place and do the household drudgery herself. She had made that clear more than once, and he knew it perfectly. “How could I send you to suffer in Huizhou? Even if you wanted to go, I couldn’t bear it. I have three places in mind: Shanghai, Beijing, or Yangzhou, where I have two sets of relatives.” Provided Huizhou was excluded, the choice could be discussed at leisure. Better still, though, would be not to leave at all. Yueru had lived here all her thirty years and had never traveled far. She feared that elsewhere the climate and water would disagree with her and she would not settle.

“We can’t stay, unless we’re prepared to lose everything.” “Is it as bad as that?” “Of course. This Zhou has a black heart and a ruthless hand. They have copied all the accounts. Once he adds them up item by item, it will take fifty thousand taels to get past him.” “Can’t you deny it?” “With the evidence in their hands?” “What if you ignore him?” “Try it! Ma is an expectant magistrate acting under his superior’s orders. He has enormous powers. One word from him, and I’m in the lockup at Renhe. You can bring me my meals there.” Yueru sighed. “Then it will have to be Shanghai. But even there, we may not be safe.” “We certainly will!” Tang declared confidently. “The foreign settlement is practically foreign territory. Even the circuit intendant can’t send runners in to arrest people, let alone the Shanghai magistrate. People come there from everywhere, every sort imaginable. So long as you have money, you can swagger about every day, ride in carriages, visit Zhang Garden, eat Western meals, and watch Beijing opera. Nobody will meddle in your affairs.” This picture of Shanghai’s pleasures thoroughly tempted Yueru. All her sorrow at leaving vanished. After careful thought, she said, “Fortunately, the cash is in HSBC. We can take our valuables with us and should be packed within three days. The property we can’t move will have to stay for now. But might something happen during those three days?”

“We must play for time. Yang told me to see Zhou tonight. He’s bound to name a price. He’ll ask the earth, and I’ll bargain him down. Naturally, we won’t agree. I’ll invite him to dinner tomorrow evening, and you must make a special effort…” “That trick again!” Yueru shouted. “What do you take me for?” “The compassionate Guanyin herself, saving those in distress. This Zhou fed me an empty dumpling. You must avenge me.” “Avenge you?” She gave a cold laugh. “I’ll have nothing to do with it. You may end up losing the lady as well as the troops, and I’ll have let another man take advantage of me for nothing. Why should I?” “Don’t be foolish. Can’t you give him an empty dumpling too? Drag it out for two or three days, and we’ll be in Shanghai. How will he take advantage of you then?” “Suppose he forces himself on me?” “Can’t you shout? I’ll come and rescue you.” “Then it becomes a blackmail set-up. And what you do to him one day, he’ll repay the next. It looks as though I’ll be bringing you prison meals after all.” Tang fell silent. Yueru was not his first wife, and she had already once slept with another man. Since lifelong fidelity was no longer in question, he thought, it would hardly matter if Zhou enjoyed her favors too. But he could not say so crudely and could only hint. “If you don’t want to bring meals to prison—if, in truth, you can’t bear to see me imprisoned—then everything depends on your showing a little compassion.” Yueru said nothing either. She merely called the serving woman who did the cooking and told her to kill a chicken and buy extra food for the following day.

Zhou went eagerly to Yuanbao Street to see Hu Xueyan, only to meet a rebuff as soon as he explained his purpose. “To be frank, Mr Zhou,” the doorkeeper said, “illness is an excuse; the master is refusing visitors. Just think—what mood can he be in to receive anyone? I’ll announce you, certainly, but if he won’t see you, please don’t take offense.” “I have serious business to discuss with him.” “Business?” The man shook his head vigorously. “Then you definitely won’t see him. Mention the bank, pawnshops, or silk business, and his head begins to ache.” “Then say I’ve come to visit him.” “That’s all I can say. Still…” As he started off, the doorkeeper muttered, “I doubt it will do any good.” Zhou’s enthusiasm cooled. He reflected silently that he had done his part in wishing to help; if Hu would not see him, nothing could be done. Tang must not be let off, of course. He could think of some means of gripping the man by the throat and making him disgorge what he had swallowed. The New Year was coming. Distributing padded clothes and rice would do good and earn merit in the unseen world. Absorbed in these thoughts, he lost track of time, until a sudden “Mr Zhou!” made him look up. The doorkeeper stood before him. “The master asks you in.” “Oh!” Zhou collected himself. “So he will see me after all?” “I didn’t know you were his friend of forty years!” the man said with an apologetic smile. “Please don’t be offended.” “Not at all. Lead the way.”

At the garden entrance the doorkeeper handed him over to a senior maid accompanied by an older serving woman, who led him straight to the Hundred Lions Tower. “Mind your step, Mr Zhou!” A beautiful middle-aged woman greeted him upstairs. He had met her once before and hastily clasped his hands. “Madam Luosi! You are too kind!” “Mr Hu is waiting inside.” She lifted the curtain herself. This was Zhou’s first visit to these rooms. Looking in, he was dazzled: lamps to the east, lamps to the west; Hu Xueyan to the east, Hu Xueyan to the west. There might be any number of lamps, but Hu could not multiply himself. He warned himself that there were many mirrors here: he must not make a fool of himself like Granny Liu entering Yihong Court. He stopped just inside the doorway to get his bearings. “Shaotang! Sit over here.” Following the voice, he found Hu in a red-velvet armchair, wearing a short padded jacket buttoned down the front, with a checked woolen blanket around his lower body. A satin band about a cun wide was tied around his forehead, presumably for a headache. “Sit here.” Hu patted the embroidered stool beside him and pointed to his head with a smile. “Don’t I look like a woman resting after childbirth?” If he could still joke, Zhou thought with relief, perhaps things were not as bad as he had imagined.

He began casually describing the inspection of Gongji, recounting the whole affair without omitting the smallest detail. Finally he explained how the money was to be divided once recovered from Tang. “Let it go,” Hu said. “There’s no need to pursue it so seriously.” Zhou stared. Only after a long silence could he say, “It seems I’ve been meddling unnecessarily.” “Shaotang, that makes me sound a halfwit. Let me tell you the truth. I’m not willingly playing the gullible fool, unable to distinguish good from bad or right from wrong. I’m thinking of someone else.” “Who?” Zhou naturally asked. “Tang’s concubine.” “Ah!” Zhou understood at once. He had long known of Hu’s amorous adventure and now had a chance to confirm it. “I heard Tang laid a beauty trap and you fell into it?” “It wasn’t exactly that. I lost my head for a moment. There’s no need to discuss it now.” Hu went on, “Yesterday I told my concubines: ‘I’m giving you a way out. Any of you who wish to leave may take everything in your rooms, and I’ll give you five thousand taels besides.’ Yueru, after all, once shared my affection. Now that I have this opportunity, I’d like to let Tang off. But it is Ma Fengshi’s official business, and you have gone to such trouble. All I can do is thank you. I wouldn’t presume to interfere too much with how it is handled.” “So that is what you have in mind. I misjudged you. I only wanted to do something for you. If you remember an old lover and want it handled this way, naturally I’ll follow your wishes. As for how much or how little, I must judge by circumstances. And I shall tell them.”

“No, no! Don’t explain it.” Hu suddenly smiled mysteriously. “Shaotang, do you remember A’xiang, the ‘Tofu Beauty’ at Shita’ertou?” Zhou paused, retrieving her image from the dust of memory. Shita’ertou was a place name. A’xiang, the daughter of a tofu-shop owner there, had been famous for her beauty. Zhou had first become her lover; later Hu had become his so-called “brother sharing the same boots,” and Zhou had stopped visiting altogether. Looking back on that youthful dream of love, he felt nothing now. He only wondered why Hu had suddenly brought it up. “I’ve always felt bad about that. A rabbit shouldn’t eat the grass beside its own burrow. I shouldn’t have poached on you. Now it would be best to repay you in kind.” Lowering his voice, Hu said, “Yueru is what Yangzhou people call a ‘slender horse.’ Why don’t you try riding her?” Zhou felt a flicker of interest, though he repeatedly protested, “You must be joking!” Hu said nothing. His smile slowly faded, while his eyes kept blinking: plainly another thought was forming. “Then, Shaotang, would you like to hear something that is certainly no joke?” “Yes.”

“You know the stories about General Nian, surely?” General Nian was Nian Gengyao. Hangzhou people told fantastic tales of his being “demoted eighteen ranks in a single night.” He had spent roughly six months in the city, first reduced from duke of the first class to Hangzhou Garrison General, then to an unattached officer—baitalabulehafan in Manchu, cavalry commandant in Chinese, of the regular fourth rank—and assigned as commander of the guard at Yongjin Gate beside West Lake. There were so many stories about him in Hangzhou that Zhou asked, “Which one?” “The story of General Nian giving away his concubine.” This was one of the most remarkable of the many Nian Gengyao legends. It was said that he sat every day at Yongjin Gate. Country people passing through, awed by his formidable reputation, either avoided him or hurried past with lowered heads. Only a poor scholar, who came and went morning and evening, always greeted him with a respectful bow. Several months passed. Then an imperial order arrived for Nian’s arrest and removal to the capital. Early the next morning, he stopped the scholar. “I can see that you’re an honest, kindhearted man. I doubt I shall survive this journey to the capital. I have a concubine I should like to give you. Please look after her, and do not refuse.” The poor scholar dared not entertain such an extravagant thought. He repeatedly declined, while Nian repeatedly urged him. Finally he admitted the truth: his house was bare, and he could not afford another mouth to feed. “So that is the difficulty! You needn’t worry. I’ll send her tomorrow. Where do you live?” Only after much questioning did the scholar reveal his address. At dusk the next day, a small sedan arrived, accompanied by a modest “dowry.” A beautiful woman in the bloom of her twenties stepped out: Nian’s favorite concubine. The scholar was overwhelmed with joy at this unexpected romantic good fortune, yet did not know how they would live. Without a word, the woman unlocked the two drawers of a table she had brought. They were full of jewels—enough for a lifetime.

“I am much like General Nian now,” Hu said. “Half my concubines left yesterday. Some insist on staying with me. One has not decided whether to go; I think she fears having nobody to depend on for the rest of her life. She is a very honest woman, and I want to find someone trustworthy for her. Shaotang, take her home with you.” “You’re joking!” Zhou replied without thinking. “There can be no question of it.” “Why not? Haven’t you heard the storytellers? People in the old days often gave away concubines or horses. I’m offering her to you; you aren’t stealing my beloved. What is there to fear?” Zhou fell silent. He wanted to refuse but could find no reason. At last he could only say, “I must discuss it with my wife.”

Early the next morning, while Zhou was still in bed, Clerk Yang knocked at his door. Zhou rose to receive him, apologized for having been out the previous evening, and asked why he had come. “Tang Zishao! He came to see me last night and asked me to accompany him to your place. He seems quite obedient. I told him yesterday to see you in the evening, and he really came.” “Where is he now?” “I said I’d arrange a meeting with you and let him know, then sent him home. Where shall you meet?” “Somewhere reasonably secluded.” “My house, then. I’ll fetch him. Come over when you’ve washed and had something to eat.” Zhou nodded. After seeing Yang out, he washed and considered his plans. Remembering Hu’s words the previous day, he could not help feeling tempted. He wanted to see what sort of “slender horse” Yueru might be. Tang was already at Yang’s house when Zhou arrived. He hastily stood and, behaving like the host rather than a guest, attended to the newcomer with great solicitude. “Brother Shaotang,” Yang said as he rose, “you two talk. I have a small matter to attend to and will be back shortly. Stay for a simple lunch.” He was leaving them to speak privately; mentioning lunch further suggested that they need not hurry and could discuss everything at length.

In fact, little time was needed, for Tang had his plan ready and had no intention of denying anything. When Zhou produced Yang’s copied list and calculated that the total embezzled exceeded eighty-three thousand taels, Tang dropped to his knees. “Mr Zhou, please save me!” “Please, please!” Zhou hastily pulled him up. “Mr Tang, whether or not I can save you, you must first propose some course for me to consider.” “Let me tell the truth. I took money from Mr Hu’s capital little by little because I had no choice. My luck has been bad these past few years. I lost money in business, and my concubine loves gambling and has lost a great deal too. Now that Mr Hu is in trouble, if I had any means of doing so, I ought to have replaced the money long ago.” “So you’re an iron rooster, unwilling to part with a single feather?” “No, no! I still have a few antiques and jades. I know you are an expert. Perhaps you could come and see what they’re worth?” Tang paused, then added, “There isn’t much cash, but I’ll gather whatever I can.” “How much?” “I can’t say at once. First we must value the objects and see what is lacking; then I’ll try to find a way.” This was a story Tang had invented to appeal to Zhou’s tastes. Zhou’s interest in jade was well known in the collectors’ tea gatherings. Taking Tang at his word, he readily agreed. “Very well. When shall I come?” “Would this evening suit you? My concubine cooks very well. I’ll ask her to show what she can do, and you can judge her skill.” The invitation stirred both Zhou’s lust and his appetite. Still, he could not ignore Yang and Ma. “You shouldn’t invite only me.” “I understand. It would be awkward for me to invite His Honour Ma, but I’ll ask Clerk Yang as well.”

Yang had deliberately withdrawn and had nothing to attend to. Realizing that their conversation had ended, he immediately came out to urge them to stay. “Lunch is almost ready. Have it before you go.” “Thank you, thank you!” Tang clasped his hands repeatedly. “I have something to do. I’ll trouble you another day. By the way, I’ve invited Zhou to a simple dinner at my house tonight. Please join us.” The phrase “by the way” showed how little sincerity there was in the invitation. Yang also knew they had unfinished business to discuss and had no wish to come between them. Claiming an engagement that evening, he declined. After seeing Tang off, he kept Zhou for a confidential conversation over wine. Zhou described his visit to Hu the day before, omitting the suggestion that he ride Yueru, the “slender horse.” This was not deliberate concealment; he had simply made no decision. Even if the sight of her tempted him, he would first have to judge the circumstances. “Mr Hu has real generosity of spirit,” Yang said. “Even now he remembers old affections. A man as kindhearted as that may yet rise again.” “Hardly. His powerful patron is no longer any use, and his own spirit is broken. How can he rise again?” Zhou paused. “Enough of that. To business: how much should we make Tang disgorge?” “You decide.” Having designs on Yueru, Zhou left himself room to maneuver. “Half at most; twenty or thirty thousand at least. We three will divide it equally.”

Tang’s house was easy to find. One had only to go into the lane behind Gongji Pawnshop and ask where Pawnbroker Tang lived; someone would point it out. Tang answered the door himself. “It’s very cold today. Come upstairs.” A brazier was burning there, and fresh strips of white paper covered the cracks in the wooden partitions. Though the wind outside was strong, the room was as warm as spring. Zhou could not bear to keep his fox-fur robe on. At his host’s suggestion, he removed it and was perfectly comfortable in his lined jacket of woolen satin. “Would you care for a fragrant pipeful?” Tang asked, indicating the smoking tray. “You go ahead. I have no craving, though I like reclining beside a smoking tray.” “Then make yourself comfortable.” Tang told a maid to light the opium lamp, brought out a large brocade-covered box, and placed it below the tray. “Have a look at a few jades first.” They lay facing each other. Tang smoked while Zhou opened the box to examine its contents. It was divided by removable trays. On the top lay three Han jades, each roughly the same size: a little over one cun long and six or seven fen wide. Zhou could recognize only the last four seal-script characters carved on them. “These are gangmao amulets,” he said, pointing. “They must have these four characters: ‘None dare confront me.’” “Oh?” Tang asked deliberately. “What are gangmao used for?” “Warding off evil.” “Gang means strong—that I understand. Naturally, something that wards off evil must be strong. But I don’t understand mao.” “Mao, together with ‘gold’ and ‘knife,’ gives the surname Liu. The Han was the Liu dynasty. Another explanation says the choice must be made in the first month of the year; hence the name gangmao.” “You really are an expert.” “Anyone who collects Han jade must know such things.” Zhou took up the second piece and examined it by the opium lamp.

“What do you think of these three gangmao?” “They’re all quite good. But…” When Zhou stopped, Tang looked up. “But they aren’t worth much?” “I wouldn’t say that.” Zhou went no further. Tang understood: against a debt of tens of thousands, objects like these would fall far short. There was no need to ask more. Lifting the partition, he said, “I doubt you have seen anything like this before.” Zhou picked it up. It was indeed new to him: a large piece of Han jade with a rich, mottled patina, about eight cun square, carved with a circular ring. Lines radiated outward from the center, each ending in a number, from one to ninety. There were ninety lines in all, exceptionally fine, deep, and evenly cut. “What is it? It looks like a compass.” “Yes, rather like one. It’s a sundial.” “A sundial?” Zhou examined it repeatedly. “The jade is certainly Han. It looks as though it was excavated recently.” “A discerning eye!” Tang held up his thumb. “Only three or four years ago, in Guihua City.” Intrigued, Zhou asked, “What is it worth?” “It’s only recently come to light, and nothing like it had been seen before, so there’s no established price. The owner originally wanted a loan of a thousand taels. I offered five hundred, and we finally settled on seven hundred. In the hands of someone who appreciates it, it could fetch a good price.” Zhou murmured without committing himself and lifted the second partition. Beneath it lay seven or eight jade seals of various sizes. He had just picked one up when footsteps sounded on the stairs. Turning onto his side, he listened.

“Ask the master where dinner is to be served.” The voice came from the outer room, clear and composed. Its tone suggested that Yueru was past the first bloom of womanhood and approaching the age of mature charm. Giving orders to a maid from only a few steps away—did that mean she would not appear herself? A slight disappointment rose in Zhou. When the maid came to inquire, Tang ordered dinner served upstairs, again because it was warmer. Then the curtain lifted, and Zhou’s eyes brightened. The woman who entered was about thirty, tall and graceful, with an oval face and exceptionally bright almond-shaped eyes. As her glance swept towards him, some force seemed to spring him from the smoking couch. Looking at her gentle smile, he found his own face wreathed in smiles. “This is my concubine, Yueru,” Tang said, indicating her with his opium needle as he prepared another pipe. “Yueru, this is Mr Zhou. Make your greeting.” “Ah, Madam!” Zhou clasped his hands and bowed first. “You are too kind!” She curtsied. “Mr Zhou, I feel I’ve seen you somewhere.” “Naturally you have,” Tang said. “Remember the platform outside Fukang, and the few words that silenced everyone demanding their money? That was Mr Zhou.”

“Ah, now I remember!” Her eyes sparkled with delighted surprise. “I went with our neighbors to watch. We talked about you for two or three days afterward. Your eloquence is beyond praise. But that is the lesser thing. Everyone says your loyalty is truly rare. Mr Hu was the God of Wealth; who knows how many people flattered him then? Once misfortune struck, he became a God of Pestilence, and everyone fled at the sight of him. Only you could not bear it and came forward to speak fairly. Now that I see you, I can tell you’re a blessed, kindhearted gentleman who does good, earns merit, and shows mercy wherever he can.” Her words soothed Zhou as though his heart had been gently ironed. Naturally, he recognized the hint conveyed by “shows mercy wherever he can.” “You are too kind. I too have long heard how capable and virtuous Mistress Tang is—a worthy helpmate to our Old Tang.” Hearing this change of address, Tang knew Zhou was certain to fall into the trap. “Cunning as a devil, yet you’ll drink the water a woman has washed her feet in,” he thought smugly. Tossing aside his pipe, he sprang up. “Time for wine!”
