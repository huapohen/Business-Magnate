HU XUEYAN

INSTALLMENT 170
Vanishing into Thin Air — Chapter Seven, Part Three (conclusion)

This installment is part of an ongoing translation. Paragraphs correspond to those in the bilingual edition.

The square table had been set. Naturally Zhou Shaotang was given the seat of honour, but only Tang Zishao sat beside him. The dishes were not particularly elaborate, yet they were delicious. Zhou liked foods preserved in wine or wine lees, and especially enjoyed the whitefish steamed with lees, the country-style cured pork, and the drunken crabs. On learning that the pork and crabs had been prepared by Yueru herself rather than bought, he praised them still more warmly. Zhou was an animated talker and in excellent spirits; Tang was deliberately flattering him. They drank freely, talked eagerly, and got on very well. Their subjects were naturally light and entertaining. “Old Tang,” Zhou asked, turning to his host’s trade, “all the pawnbrokers in the country seem to be Huizhou men, just as all the remittance banks belong to Shanxi men. I have heard that even those come from only two or three prefectures, such as Taigu and Pingyao. Why is that?” “Mr Zhou, if anyone else asked, I should pretend not to know and put him off with a few words. But since it is you, I must tell you the history. It is not a very creditable story, though.” “Oh? How so?”

“During the Jiajing reign of the Ming, there was a man from our Huizhou called Wang Zhi. Have you heard of him?” “I know only that there was a Zou Yinglong in the Jiajing reign—the man who attacked Yan Song. I know nothing about Wang Zhi.” “Then I shall tell you. Wang Zhi was a traitor.” “A traitor? You mean he conspired with foreigners, like Qin Hui?” “Exactly. But Wang Zhi’s allies were not the Jin troops. They were Japanese, what people then called the wokou pirates. When they landed on the Jiangsu and Zhejiang coast, they knew nothing of the country. Wang Zhi and his followers guided them as they raped and plundered their way inland. The pirates were a base lot: when looting, they took everything. Some things they could not carry away; others would be of little use in Japan even if they could. So Wang Zhi had an idea. He opened a pawnshop that would accept anything. Ordinary people bringing in their pledges were merely a cover. In plain language, he was disposing of stolen goods for the Japanese.” “No wonder your writing looks like a sorcerer’s charms, and you speak in special jargon. There was a reason for it all,” Zhou said. “Since it was illegal, you naturally had to use men from your own district.” “Still, one must remember how poor Huizhou is. What the district produces cannot support its own people. They have to go elsewhere for a living, calling in friends and helping fellow townsmen. They have little choice.”

“You Huizhou men certainly know how to do business. Ask the great salt merchants of Yangzhou where their families came from, and more than half say Huizhou. But if Wang Zhi was both a traitor and a receiver of the Japanese pirates’ stolen goods, was he not afraid of being arrested?” “There was a reason for that too. Hu Zongxian, the inspecting censor who later became governor, was also a Huizhou man. One need not call it protection; he had only to be a little lenient towards a fellow townsman, and the matter passed. However serious a case may be, if there is friendship, something can always be arranged.” Tang raised his cup. “Come, Mr Zhou, let us drink.” The last two sentences, accompanied by the toast, plainly carried another meaning. Zhou pretended not to notice. He drained his cup and changed the subject. “You say this Hu Zongxian was an inspecting censor. I doubt he had the power to protect Wang Zhi. Was his authority really so great?” “You have only to look at Wang Jinlong in The Trial Before Three Judges.” “Wang Jinlong is played as a young man, apparently just beginning his career. How could he have such authority? A play is only a play.” Tang knew much more about this than Zhou. “Ming jinshi were different from those today. Nowadays, unless a jinshi enters the Hanlin Academy or becomes an official in one of the Six Ministries, he is sent out as no more than a county magistrate of the ‘tiger class.’ But a Ming jinshi appointed inspecting censor received an imperial sword of authority. He was as good as the emperor himself on a tour of inspection. His power was tremendous. Let me tell you a story, and you will understand.”

There was said to have been a rich man in the Ming with two daughters. The elder married a military officer, the younger a poor scholar. The father was somewhat snobbish, and the younger son-in-law suffered many slights. Eventually the scholar passed the provincial and metropolitan examinations and was appointed inspecting censor for Henan. His fellow son-in-law happened to be brigade commander at Nanyang in that province. A censor held the seventh rank, a brigade commander the second. But an inspecting censor toured on the emperor’s behalf, which gave him a different standing. When he reached Nanyang, the commander came before he was out of bed, during the fifth watch next morning, to request permission to parade the troops for inspection. Remembering how differently their father-in-law had treated them, the scholar was moved to compose a quatrain on his pillow: “Before the yellow-grass slope, ten thousand armoured men; behind green gauze curtains, a solitary scholar. Now you should know the worth of verse and prose: lying in bed, I hear the commander announce the fifth watch.” With a poem to prove the story, Zhou could hardly disbelieve it. He also drew a further conclusion. “Then the men in red and blue robes on either side in The Trial Before Three Judges must be the provincial treasurer and judicial commissioner. Am I right?” “Exactly.” “If those two sit beside him as fellow judges, the man in the centre ought to have the standing of a governor?” “Hu Zongxian went from inspecting censor for Zhejiang to governor of Zhejiang.” “That explains it.” Zhou sighed. “If only Mr Hu had encountered that namesake of his!” He meant that, had Hu Xueyan found a governor willing to protect him as Hu Zongxian had protected his fellow townsman Wang Zhi, his pawnshops would not have been taken into official custody. Tang understood but did not care to respond.

“Mr Zhou,” Tang said suddenly, “Gongji has quite a few unredeemed pledges whose terms have expired. Would you like to come and look?” Zhou did not want to take such a petty advantage, but neither did he wish to refuse outright. “Is there anything unusual, something one does not often see elsewhere?” “Yes, plenty.” Tang thought for a moment. “The New Year is approaching. There is a set of lanterns I advise you to buy. Hang them up on the fifteenth of the first month, and I guarantee they will be a splendid sight.” Zhou was puzzled. Lantern Festival lanterns were made of bamboo strips and coloured paper; novelty was their chief attraction. How could they be accepted as pledges? “These cannot be paper lanterns, I suppose?” “Of course not. How could they be pawned otherwise? They are silk lanterns, in a limited number of designs, mostly hexagonal, like palace lanterns. The silk is painted with figures, beautiful women, and all sorts of stories. The frames fold. After use, you clean them, fold and wrap them, and bring them out again next year. They are common around Haining. Have you never seen them?” “No.” “You will understand when you do. They are not revolving paper lanterns for amusing children. A respectable family gives a New Year dinner for distinguished guests and hangs them in the hall and corridors. With a cup in hand, one can study the paintings by different artists at leisure. One can also hang riddles from them for the guests to solve. It is a quiet, cultivated amusement. A man of your present standing ought to acquire a set.” Zhou’s income had been good in recent years, and he was eager to improve his social position. Since his dealings with the authorities over Fukang, he was by now virtually counted among the gentry. Tang’s suggestion appealed to him. “How much would such a set cost?”

“You could spend any amount,” Tang replied. “There are enormous differences in quality. It all depends on the paintings—whether they are by famous artists. Even a famous artist may not consent to paint lanterns; that is what makes them valuable. Take Fei Xiaolou, the foremost painter of beauties today. Ask him to paint lanterns, and he will refuse.” “Then who painted the set of expired pledges you have?” “An exceedingly famous man. Countless great officials of the Kangxi reign commissioned him to paint portraits of them at leisure. He came from Yangzhou. His surname was Yu, like Yu the Great, and his full name Yu Zhiding. He also held office as an usher in the Court of State Ceremonial. By regulation that post was for bannermen; I do not know how he came to hold it...” “Old Tang,” Zhou interrupted, “let us leave his office aside and talk about his paintings.” Tang returned to the point. Yu’s set comprised twenty-four silk lanterns in six designs, illustrating six stories: Xi Shi and the fall of Wu, Wenjun tending the wine shop, Zhaojun going beyond the frontier, Wenji returning to the Han, Fufei leaving her pillow, and Mei and Yang competing for favour. Mei was Consort Mei, and Yang was Yang Yuhuan. Thus the six stories contained seven beauties. “There are many counterfeit Yu Zhiding paintings, but these lanterns are certainly genuine. Their history is different. In the Kangxi reign there was a great imperial favourite called Gao Jiangcun, originally from Hangzhou. Later he lived in Pinghu County, Jiaxing. By the Jiaqing reign his descendants had fallen into poverty. Gao commissioned Yu Zhiding to paint this set, so there can be no doubt about it. Mr Zhou, I shall have it sent to your house tomorrow.” “No, no!” Zhou waved his hands. “Let me see it first; we can decide afterwards.”

Tang was about to continue when a maid came in. “Someone has come from Gongji. The chief counter appraiser has been taken suddenly ill. They ask you to go at once.” Pawnshop clerks held either internal or counter posts. The head of the latter was called the chief counter appraiser and ordinarily sat at the extreme left of the counter facing the entrance. Any valuable article brought in had to pass through his hands for examination and valuation. It was therefore an important position, and worry immediately appeared on Tang’s face. Zhou could no longer remain comfortably seated. “Old Tang, if you have urgent business, please go. I was about to take my leave.” “No, no! I shall go and look, then return. Our business is important too.” He called for Yueru. When the maid brought her, he told her to keep their guest company, saluted Zhou with clasped hands, and went downstairs. In this situation Zhou naturally remembered the story of Hu falling into a beauty trap, and became a little wary. But Yueru was entirely at ease. Serving food and pouring wine, she asked about Zhou’s family, moving from his wife to his children as one subject led naturally to another. She talked readily, with the unceremonious familiarity of an old acquaintance discussing household affairs. To Zhou, she seemed an able and virtuous housewife, making the stories about her and Hu almost impossible to believe. Inevitably Hu’s failure came up, and she showed deep loyalty to her former master, as though his fortunes were her own. Zhou was tempted to raise Gongji’s affairs, but in the end said nothing.

“Madam,” the maid reported again, “the master has sent word that the chief appraiser is very ill. He must remain there a little longer to see how things develop. Mr Zhou is asked not to leave; there is still important business to discuss.” “Very well. Warm another pot of wine.” “I have had enough wine,” Zhou said. “There is no need for more. If there is congee, I should like a bowl.” “We have fragrant rice congee ready. You can still have a little more wine.” “Then let us stop after one pot.” After the wine they had congee, then tea. Still there was no word of Tang’s return, and Zhou began to feel uncertain. “Mr Zhou.” Yueru emerged from the inner room, her powder and rouge freshly applied. Quite openly she said, “Let me prepare you a pipe.” “I have no habit.” “Just enjoy one for amusement.” She lit the opium lamp herself, lay down, and lifted some opium on the needle to heat it.

The maid brought a small pot of piping-hot tea and a plate of pine-nut sweets, set them on the smoking tray, and withdrew without a word. “Your pipe is ready,” Yueru called. “Come over.” Almost against his will, Zhou lay down opposite her. They shared a long pillow, and he immediately caught the fragrance of her osmanthus hair oil. Once desire took hold, it became a torment. Beginning with that scent, everything that met his eyes or nostrils was an irresistible provocation. “Zhou Shaotang, Zhou Shaotang!” he told himself. “You have been about the world for decades. You are no youngster of twenty or thirty. Can you still be so green?” The warning steadied him a little, but soon he was bewildered again and had to repeat it. Before that single pipe reached his lips, he had struggled with himself three or four times. Yueru finally prepared a yellow, elongated, loosely rolled pellet, placed it over the pipe bowl’s opening, and gently pierced it with the needle. Turning the pipe, she brought the mouthpiece to his lips. “Try it.” To Zhou, this offered a means of resisting the thief within his heart. Though he was not addicted, he could appreciate opium’s attractions. Concentrating on its rich, mellow flavour, he temporarily forgot everything about Yueru. A few draws finished the pipe and left his mouth dry and bitter. But following it immediately with hot tea made addiction easy, so he dared not drink any and took a pine-nut sweet instead.

“Mr Zhou,” Yueru began, “you and my husband have known each other well for a long time, have you not?” “Not well, originally. But he is a man of standing, and of course I had long heard his name.” “He tells me there is something for which he needs your help. I do not know what it is.” Zhou was surprised. Did she really not know, or was she asking a question whose answer she knew already? After a moment he asked, “Has Old Tang not discussed it with you?” “No. He only said that we must sell the hundred-odd mu of land by West Lake as soon as possible, or he would find it difficult to face you.” “Why should he find it difficult?” “He says that asking your help with nothing but empty words would be no use.” She suddenly sighed. “Ah, that husband of mine! I have often told him: if you have a shortfall, tell Mr Hu frankly. With his temperament, however serious the matter, he will always let you through if you are honest. But my husband felt he had wronged Mr Hu by creating the deficit. Eight jars and seven lids—however he shifted them, he could not cover everything. He has lost face in the end all the same. Why go through it?” “Oh?” Zhou listened closely. “What sort of deficit did he create?” Yueru poured out a long tale of misery. Tang, she said, was shrewd on the surface but foolish underneath. He had suffered concealed losses in business partnerships and had been forced to tamper with Gongji’s funds. At the saddest points she seemed ready to weep, and even Zhou’s heart ached for her.

“You say Old Tang suffered concealed losses and could not speak of his troubles. What losses? What troubles?” “There is no harm in telling you. Mr Hu has a friend with an unusual surname—Gu. Do you know him?” “I have heard of him. He handles Mr Hu’s foreign business.” “Exactly. This Mr Gu invited my husband to join him in property development. The original calculations looked excellent. Then the foreigners went to war and the market changed. When people fled the Longhairs, they went to Shanghai; however savage the rebels were, they dared not attack the concessions. But once the foreigners were going to war, it was the people of Shanghai who had to flee. The completed houses would not sell, and they lost several hundred thousand taels. Think what that meant! And the worst of it was that he could not tell Mr Hu.” For one thing, Tang had originally undertaken to devote all his energies to managing Hu’s pawnshops and conduct no private trade of his own. Although this promise had gradually been diluted, his other ventures had remained confined to businesses connected with Hu, such as buying cocoons and selling silk. He was, in effect, taking subsidiary shares. Property development was a new business altogether. “There was another reason,” Yueru continued. “Mr Gu is Mr Hu’s close friend. If we said we had lost money in property with him, Mr Hu would be displeased. Why? He would think: you did not tell me when you joined him, but now you have lost money you come to me. Do you expect me to make it good? Besides, there were things about why the partnership lost money that could hardly be mentioned. Speaking of them would do no good. Mr Hu might think we were deliberately speaking ill of his friend, and there would be a misunderstanding.” “Why?” Zhou asked. “Was something not quite honest?”

Yueru said nothing. She had reached a critical stage in preparing another pipe. Her nimble fingers heated and rolled the opium busily, her attention entirely absorbed. Not until she had filled the pipe and Zhou had finished it, saying, “That really is enough; I have no habit,” did she lay down the needle and answer. “Think, Mr Zhou. My husband was in Hangzhou. He knew little of Shanghai prices and had no reliable market information. Afraid that Mr Hu might find out, he did not even dare make inquiries. If that sort of business could make money, the sun would surely rise in the west.” Her words plainly suggested that Gu had been embezzling. Zhou half believed and half doubted her, with no means of getting to the truth. His mind turned instead to Tang’s prolonged absence. Was he too arranging a beauty trap? Here was another serious uncertainty. “Where is Old Tang? Surely he ought to be back by now?” “Yes, indeed.” Yueru called her maid. “Hurry to Gongji and ask why the master has still not returned. Tell him Mr Zhou wants to go home.” The maid agreed and left. Yueru rose from the smoking couch, took a Tianjin pear from a large platter, and began slowly peeling it with a sharp foreign knife with an ivory handle. Zhou remained on the couch, considering how to negotiate when Tang returned.

He was deep in thought when he suddenly heard a cry. Yueru was gripping her left thumb with her right hand. The knife and a nearly peeled pear lay on the table; plainly she had cut herself. “Is it bad?” Zhou hurried over. “It is nothing.” She rose and went straight to the dressing table, giving instructions as she went. “There are clean handkerchiefs in the drawer. Tear me a strip.” She used pa’er, the Hangzhou word for a handkerchief. Zhou opened the drawer and found several in various colours of spun silk, folded into neat squares. A white one lay underneath. Turning the others aside, he discovered a thin book. “There is a book here too,” he remarked casually. He was about to turn its pages when Yueru cried urgently, “Do not look! Do not look!” Startled, he drew his hand back. Her cheeks were flushed, and she looked slightly embarrassed. After a moment he understood what sort of book it must be. Smiling, he took the white handkerchief, made a snip with scissors, and tore off a long strip about a cun wide. Holding it in one hand, he opened the powder jar with the other and took a pinch between two fingers. “Let go of your hand.” When she did, he put the powder on the cut, quickly bandaged it, and found a thread to tie the dressing. “Does it hurt?” he asked, still holding her hand. “Not much. It is lucky you were here, or I should have bled all over the floor.” She gave a slight tug to withdraw her hand. When he would not release it, she stopped resisting.

“Sister-in-law, what white hands you have.” “Really? How do they compare with your wife’s?” “There is no comparison.” “Is your wife very young?” “She was born in the Year of the Sheep. And you?” “The Ox.” “Then she is much older than you.” Holding her hand, Zhou led her back to the square table in the middle of the room, released it, and sat down opposite her. “The pear is only half peeled...” “I shall finish it. It is unusually large; we can share it.” “One should not divide a pear,” Yueru said. “Take your time and eat it yourself. Pears clear phlegm and internal heat. They are excellent for opium smokers in winter.” “It does not really matter whether you and I divide a pear. As long as you and Old Tang do not part, all will be well.” The word for pear, li, sounded like the word for parting. Both understood the play on words and used it to test the other. Yueru seized her chance. “Whether Old Tang and I part depends entirely on whether you will do a good deed and earn merit, Mr Zhou.” “You exaggerate. How could I have such power?” “There is no need for modesty. I have heard that whether Old Tang faces prosecution depends entirely on your willingness to help. If you help, we can remain together. If not, I fear we shall certainly be parted.”

Only now did Zhou realise that, even if Yueru had taken no part in Tang’s doings, she must know all about them. Tang had arranged for her to negotiate with him. He therefore had to decide whether to discuss the matter with her. If not, it was time to leave. Yet the thought of leaving immediately filled him with reluctance, and he found himself considering how to begin. He would first learn a little more before deciding. “Sister-in-law, do you know how large Old Tang’s deficit is?” “Thirty or forty thousand taels, I suppose?” “More.” “Oh? How much?” “At least twice that.” She stared at him, stunned. Who knew what she summoned up as the most sorrowful experience of her life? Somehow she squeezed out a sudden flood of tears. Zhou could not bear it. “Do not be anxious. We can discuss it calmly. If I can help, I certainly shall. How much ready silver can Old Tang raise now?” “Ready silver?” She thought. “Probably only two or three thousand taels. Apart from that, there is only my jewellery.” “What is it worth?” “Two or three thousand at most.” “Two lots of two or three thousand make five or six thousand. What about your land by West Lake?” “That is worth over ten thousand, but we cannot find a buyer immediately.” “West Lake land is in great demand. Ten days or half a month should bring a buyer.” “Would ten days or half a month be in time?” The question astonished Zhou. It showed that she understood official procedure. “In time” referred to Magistrate Ma’s report. Having accepted the treasurer’s commission, he had to report back by the appointed deadline. If matters could not be settled and he reported the facts, Tang would immediately face imprisonment. Evidently Tang had studied the case thoroughly, considered every consequence, and coached her in what to say and how to respond. Zhou must take the greatest care not to fall into their trap.

After thinking for a moment, he asked, “What if it is in time? And what if it is not?” “If it is, so much the better. If not, would you discuss it with Magistrate Ma and ask him to hold the matter back, without reporting it yet?” “That may be difficult.” By now Zhou had made up his mind. Discovering that Tang and Yueru intended to use the same methods on him as they had on Hu had aroused his desire for revenge. He would take some advantage of the situation first. “Sister-in-law,” he said abruptly, “the boat will straighten itself when it reaches the bridge. Do not think too much. If the sky falls, there are tall people to hold it up. When Old Tang returns, we shall find a way. I shall certainly help you. But what do I get for helping?” “Mr Zhou, how petty that sounds!” She glanced at him. “Must good friends offer you a benefit before you will help?” “That is not what I mean. To help a friend properly, the wish must come from the heart. One must keep thinking of it. Otherwise one remembers while the friend is there and forgets afterwards. That is human nature, not pettiness.” “Then what benefit do you want?” “Only that you should be kind to me. Remembering your kindness will naturally remind me of what you have asked.” He reached out and pointed to her thumb. “Does it still hurt?” “It stopped hurting long ago.” “Let me see.” He took her hand and slowly slipped his other hand up her sleeve. She merely smiled.

“Is it nice?” she suddenly asked. “Is what nice?” “My arm. Does it feel pleasant?” “Very pleasant.” “That is the kindness I offer. When you remember it, do not forget what I have asked.” “I shall not. Only, it is a pity.” “What is?” “There is so little of it.” “How much kindness do you want?” Rising and turning on her feet, she simply sat on his lap. Zhou naturally began caressing and fondling her freely. His mind, however, remained clear. He watched the door and listened for sounds on the stairs, telling himself that, as long as he neither undressed nor went to bed with her, it would not matter even if Tang walked in. Keeping within that limit was another matter. He felt as though he were burning. Again and again the urge to go further grew stronger. Just when he could scarcely control himself, he thought of a way out. Pushing Yueru aside, he seized a cup of cold tea from a small table by the window and gulped it down without stopping. That made him feel somewhat easier.

But he was unwilling to stop there. Breathing hard, he said, “Sister-in-law, no wonder Mr Hu was bewitched by you.” “Nonsense!” She widened her eyes. “You listen to people’s malicious gossip.” “There is no smoke without fire. There must have been something to start it.” “Something just like this. You like me, so I let you touch and kiss me. What else could there be? Do you really think me so cheap?” “How could I dare?” Zhou sat down again and drew her back onto his lap. This time he felt more in control. “Very well. Since you say that if I like you, I may touch and kiss you, I shall do as you say.” He slipped an arm around her and kissed her mouth. Yueru was quite compliant and made no attempt to resist. After letting him kiss her for a while, she leaned her head back. “Have I given you enough kindness?” “Plenty.” “And you?” “What about me?” “What you promised.” “I shall certainly not forget.” “And if you do? Swear before the Lamp-Light Bodhisattva to accept punishment if you forget.” Zhou took oaths invoking divine punishment seriously. After a brief calculation, he said, “Sister-in-law, I promise to help you by having Magistrate Ma hold back your case for a while. But a delay does not mean letting it disappear. Do not misunderstand. This is official business. Even if Magistrate Ma were my own son, I could not order him how to deal with it, nor would he obey.”

“I understand that. But what do you intend to ask him to do?” “Allow a reduction.” “How much?” “What do you say?” “If it were up to me, I should want a big affair made small and a small affair made nothing. If you will do that, I shall give you more kindness.” Zhou was tempted. Smiling, he asked, “What sort?” Yueru said nothing, her lively eyes constantly moving. Knowing she had thought of another trick, he kept a cool watch. Suddenly footsteps on the stairs broke the silence. They were plainly a man’s: Tang had returned. “Mr Zhou,” Yueru said with perfect seriousness, “when my husband comes in, do not make any suggestive remarks.” She rose to meet him. Her warning gave Zhou a peculiar feeling. This was clearly a scheme the couple had arranged together. Why, then, must she pretend to be proper before her husband? And why was she worried that Zhou might say something suggestive in his presence? These were questions worth pondering, but there was no time. Tang was already there, and courtesy required Zhou to rise and greet him with a smile.

“My apologies!” Tang hurried forward, saluting with clasped hands. “I am truly ashamed to have kept you waiting so long.” “Not at all!” Zhou deliberately made an insinuation. “Sister-in-law and I have got on very well. She even cut her hand peeling a pear for me.” “Yes!” Tang turned to Yueru. “I noticed as soon as I came in. How did you cut it? Is it serious?” “It is nothing. How is Mr Zhao?” Mr Zhao was Gongji’s chief counter appraiser, the man who had fallen suddenly ill. “Out of danger for the moment. Thanks to that fine ginseng Mr Hu gave me, a decoction of ginseng alone finally brought him round.” Tang continued, “Quickly prepare a couple of pipes. I must satisfy my craving first. Come, Mr Zhou, let us lie down and talk.” Host and guest reclined on either side of the smoking tray. Yueru drew up a small stool between them, lit the lamp, and prepared the opium. Tang described Mr Zhao’s illness. Zhou had little interest in listening and answered vaguely, watching Yueru’s expression closely without discovering anything. After the two pipes had been smoked, she spoke. “I have been telling Mr Zhou about your troubles. You had no choice about the deficit. He has been very helpful. First he will ask Magistrate Ma to hold back the official proceedings while we hurry to raise a sum to settle the affair.” “Yes, once a matter has come out, it must be settled. If Mr Zhou will help, we have found our saviour.” “He says the deficit is large and can only be settled at a reduction. He also says our West Lake land should sell in ten days or half a month. Since it is inconvenient for you to appear yourself, we must ask him to find a buyer. Naturally, we shall pay him the usual intermediary’s commission.”

After considerable discussion, Tang made a commitment: within ten days he would raise twenty-four thousand taels. The principal source would be the sale of his West Lake land, followed by Yueru’s jewellery and his antiques. If those were insufficient, he would sell whatever else he had until the sum was complete. It was now Zhou’s turn to speak. He had been considering one difficulty throughout: once Ma reported that the takeover had proceeded satisfactorily, Tang would be entirely discharged from responsibility. What if he then repudiated the agreement? Zhou could, of course, demand an acknowledgement of debt. But whereas the authorities enforced the principle that a murderer must pay with his life, the two parties could reach a private settlement over a debt. As the saying went, “It is not the fierce creditor one fears, but the penniless debtor.” If Tang deliberately refused payment, demands might prove fruitless and merely create a public scandal. People would ask: “How did Tang come to owe you twenty-four thousand taels? You are no more than nodding acquaintances. Would you really lend him so large a sum?” Zhou would have no answer. The truth would inevitably emerge, and he would acquire all the stink of mutton without having tasted a morsel. It was not worth it. His silence lasted so long that Tang and Yueru gradually guessed his concern. Tang decided to state his position first. “Mr Zhou, you must be thinking that empty words give you nothing to put before Magistrate Ma. Is that it?” Since he had guessed, Zhou need not deny it. “Exactly. I am the intermediary. I must answer to both sides.”

“Then let me have Yueru sort out her jewellery first. Please take the Han jades you saw earlier too, and sell them for us. As for the West Lake land, I shall ask you to find a buyer and give you the red deed.” A deed on which the transfer tax had been paid and the official seal impressed was called a “red deed.” But it was only the original evidence of the previous acquisition. Obtaining it prevented certain later complications; fundamentally, a fresh contract was still required for a sale. Without a deed of sale, the red deed alone could not authorise a transaction. Moreover, Tang could report it stolen and have it invalidated, reducing the original to wastepaper. Quick-witted as he was, Tang saw that Zhou understood too much to be deceived and promptly added, “Naturally, I shall mortgage it to you, with Old Yang as intermediary.” Zhou was struck by an idea. After considering, he said, “Let us do this: tomorrow morning I shall come to Gongji with Old Yang, and we can work out an arrangement.” “Very good. I shall await you both.” “It is late. If we go on talking, dawn will be here.” Zhou rose. “Thank you for everything. Until tomorrow.” “Take this box of jades with you.” “No, no!” Waving both hands, Zhou firmly refused. He then turned to Yueru. “Sister-in-law, thank you indeed. This meal has suited my stomach better than a shark’s-fin banquet.” “You are too kind. Come whenever you have time. I have a few more special dishes I should like you to try.” “Excellent! I shall certainly trouble you again.”
