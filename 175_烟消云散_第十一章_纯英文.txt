CHAPTER ELEVEN: THE PEOPLE GONE, THE TOWERS EMPTY

The two sat side by side and talked in low voices for a long time before they finished. Hu put on a hooded winter cap, pulling its brim very low, and took a quick-witted servant called A’fu with him. They opened a seldom-used side gate in the garden and stepped into a long lane. Few people were about, and those who were kept their heads down and hurried along: the cold was unusually severe, with a strong northwesterly wind. Nobody noticed that the God of Wealth, ordinarily surrounded by attendants whenever he went out, was trudging forlornly through the streets with only a single servant. “A’fu, do you know where Mr Zhou lives?” “Of course. At Longshezui.” “Yes, Longshezui. Go ahead and tell him I am coming.” “Yes, sir. What if he is not at home?” “He will not have gone out in weather like this. But if he has, leave word that when he returns he should come and find me at the restaurant beside Yaowang Temple on City God Hill.” A’fu strode ahead. Hu followed slowly on foot. He had barely entered Longshezui when he saw A’fu coming back. Catching sight of his master, the servant hurried towards him. “Well? Is he out?” “No!” A’fu pointed behind him. “There he is!” Zhou had come out specially to meet him. Hu waved a hand and gave him a warning look. Understanding that he feared a loud greeting would attract passers-by, Zhou asked quietly, “What brings you here?”

Hu could find no answer. He smiled. “You did not expect me, did you?” “Certainly not.” Noticing that people were already looking, Hu quickened his pace and went ahead of Zhou. Only at the mouth of the lane did he stop and look up. “It is twenty years since I visited your house. I remember: the fifth house, facing north.” “We moved to the other side. The fourth house, facing south.” “Of course! You bought the house opposite. This will be my first visit there.” “The house has bad feng shui.” Why bad? Hu had no chance to ask, for they had arrived. He had last seen Mrs Zhou more than twenty years before. What he remembered most clearly was her substantial figure. She had grown still stouter: a pair of tiny bound feet supported a body as large as a buffalo, making movement difficult. Yet she was overwhelmed that Hu should condescend to visit, and attended to him with a face full of smiles.

“Please, please!” Watching her sway unsteadily as she brought tea herself, Hu genuinely feared she might fall. “Sister-in-Law Zhou, do not trouble yourself. You are too heavy to risk a tumble. It would be no laughing matter.” “Exactly! Do sit down,” Zhou said. “Mr Hu comes here because he treats us as his own people. All this ceremony only makes him an outsider. Let A’chun and A’qiu do whatever needs doing.” Since Hu had helped him advance, Zhou’s circumstances had steadily improved. Fond of display, he kept four servants for the elderly couple alone. A’chun and A’qiu had been bought ten years earlier and were now nearly twenty. “Better to obey than insist on ceremony.” Mrs Zhou sat down, panting, and asked after his family. “Is the old lady still strong?” “Very well, thank you.” “And Mrs Hu?” “Well enough.” She seemed about to ask after Madam Luosi and the other concubines. Zhou already knew what had happened in Hu’s household that morning. Afraid she would keep questioning him and make matters awkward, he hastily intervened. “Mr Hu will dine with us. There is no time to prepare everything ourselves. We shall have to order some dishes.”

“Shaotang, listen. Do not put yourself to any trouble. To tell the truth, I have had my fill of rare delicacies. Especially now, I could not eat them however well you prepared them. I came because I remembered our old days together, when we could eat heartily, sleep soundly and live without a care, like immortals. I do not feel that I am a guest here. If you treat me ceremoniously, it defeats the purpose of my visit. Do you understand?” “I did not before, but now you have explained, naturally I do.” Zhou thought for a moment. “What a pity Fat Zhang is dead. Otherwise we could invite him for mulang tofu and listen to his dirty jokes. Even if it were snowing hard outside, we would not feel cold.” The mention of Fat Zhang saddened Hu and sharpened his longing for the past. “Which of our old friends are still around? I should love to gather them for a talk.” “That will have to wait for another day. But I can think of one man. Shall we invite him for a drink?” “Who?” “Mr Wu.”

After a moment, Hu gladly agreed. “Yes, yes. And I have thought of another: Zheng Junsheng.” Zheng was a celebrated performer of an’kang, the Hangzhou name for tanhuang: five seated performers sang and played the male, female, painted-face, older-male and comic roles, with the comic performer, known as the Little Painted Face, taking precedence. That was Zheng’s role. In the old days he had often pooled money with Hu and Zhou for wine. But as Hu rose swiftly in the world, the gulf between their positions had made Zheng feel inferior, and he had withdrawn. He never even accepted engagements at Hu’s household celebrations. This had always troubled Hu, and today he wanted to make amends. Understanding his feelings, Zhou nodded. “I still see him. Let me send for him.” He called Guisheng, a servant of ten years’ standing. “Go to Mr Zheng, the an’kang performer. Say I need to speak to him about something important. Then invite Mr Wu to drink with us. And when you are at Zheng’s, be sure not to mention that I have a guest.” He feared Zheng would refuse to come if he knew Hu was there.

After giving these instructions, Zhou excused himself and went to the back to discuss the meal briefly with his wife. Then he returned to sit with Hu by the brazier in the main hall. “Have you read Shenbao today?” his guest asked first. “Glanced through it. My verdict is simple: embroidered stories and utter nonsense. Pay no attention.” “I do not care. You see it as abuse; I see it as praise.” “As long as you can take it lightly. There is a saying: a hundred-legged insect does not fall stiff even when dead. If you can take things as they come, you still have several comfortable years ahead.” “Taking things lightly is only a way of deceiving oneself. For more than a month I have often had a strange thought: where could I find a medicine that would make me forget the past? Of course, I must not forget my own birth date and hour, my parents or my brothers. It would be best if I could cut out a stretch of my life.” “Which stretch?” “From the day I met Wang Youling until now. Cut that out and return to the days when we were together. That would be like living among immortals.” Zhou did not share his feelings. To describe a return to their former hardships as an immortal’s life seemed a considerable exaggeration. He merely smiled and said nothing.

“Shaotang, in my present circumstances, what could I do over the next couple of years to find some pleasure in being alive?” Zhou considered for a long time, quite earnestly, but could only shake his head with a bitter smile. “Honestly, I cannot think of anything. I can only advise you to take things less to heart.” “I have thought of something myself.” “Oh?” Zhou sincerely wished to share Hu’s fortunes, good or bad. Their unequal positions had put that beyond his reach, but the feeling was there. He urged him eagerly, “Quick, tell me!” “Do not hurry me. First let me tell you something that happened.” He related his unexpected encounter at Laotonghe, then explained what it had made him think. “Every summer I have given away tea and medicine; every winter, congee and padded coats. I have provided coffins and supported an infant foundling home. I did all these good works, yet honestly, they left my heart unmoved. The saying that doing good brings the greatest happiness never occurred to me. Why do you think that was?” “Probably because you considered it your duty. Like eating every day: a matter of routine, with no question of happiness or unhappiness.” “Exactly. A man who has made a fortune ought to do these things. That meant money was using me, instead of my using money. So I never understood the true value of being rich…” “Ah, I see!” Zhou interrupted. “You must first want to do something that lack of money prevents. When you have the money and can fulfil your wish, you appreciate its value.” “That is only half the answer. Even with money available, you must have the opportunity. That depends on the time—and on the person.”

“What do you mean, on the person?” “Suppose you want to help a friend but cannot afford it. Suddenly you win a lottery prize, just when your friend is in trouble. Could there be a better opportunity? Yet when you take him the money, he refuses. That is what I mean.” “Why would he refuse? He needs it, and you are a good friend. There is no reason.” “Refusal is refusal. Reason has nothing to do with it.” Zhou shook his head repeatedly. “Then he must have something wrong with him—an eccentric who understands nothing of human kindness.” “What if it were you?” “I should certainly accept.” “Good!” Hu pointed at him with a smile. “Those are your own words. Do not go back on them when the time comes.” Zhou stared. “Go back on what? Mr Hu, you have completely baffled me.” Hu only smiled and asked, “Does Mr Wu not live quite nearby?” Mr Wu had originally lived outside Luosi Gate. When Madam Luosi entered Hu’s household, Zhou had helped with the wedding arrangements and met him. They had taken to each other and become close friends. Madam Luosi regarded Wu as one of her own family and urged him to move inside the city, where she could consult him more readily. He asked Zhou to find him a house and bought one in Shisanwan Lane, famous for its many turns. Their homes were close, they visited frequently, and Mrs Wu and Mrs Zhou had even become sworn sisters. Zhou’s suggestion of inviting Wu had reminded Hu of something he could discuss with him, which prompted his question.

“He will be here soon.” Sure enough, Wu arrived before long. Surprised to find Hu there, he greeted him warmly, but it was not convenient to talk at length. “Shaotang, may I borrow your study? I should like a few words with Mr Wu.” “Oh, Mr Hu, do not make fun of me! How can my little bookkeeping room be called a study?” “Where there are books, there is a study.” “There is one book: the imperial almanac.” Despite Zhou’s self-mockery, his study was not badly furnished. He told A’chun to bring a brazier and prepare tea, then shut the door himself so that the two could speak privately at ease. “Mr Wu, do you know what has happened in my household?” “What is it? I know nothing.” Wu looked rather anxious. “I have sent them all away.” “Ah.” After a moment Wu asked, “How many?” “Ten altogether.” Hu’s garden was famous for its Twelve Chambers. Ten sent away meant two remaining. One must be Madam Luosi; who was the other?

Before Wu could put the question, Hu changed the subject to Zhou Shaotang. “Shaotang has lost his only son. Of the three offences against filial duty, leaving no heir is the worst. Has he thought of taking a concubine?” Wondering why Hu should ask, Wu answered truthfully. “I asked him. He said he could not find a suitable woman just then.” “Are those two maids not grown up now?” “He likes neither of them. His wife wanted him to take A’chun into his chamber and urged him twice, but he refused.” “What sort of woman does he want?” “It is hard to say. But he seems rather like Yuan Zicai.” “Yuan Zicai? In what way?” “Yuan preferred women a little older, not immature young things.” Wu quoted a line: “A mature woman’s charm surpasses youthful bloom.” He knew Zhou very well. Even before their son died, Mrs Zhou, so kindhearted that she was almost indiscriminate, had urged her husband to take a concubine to look after his daily needs, since her own bulk made it difficult for her. She meant him to choose either A’chun or A’qiu, but he refused outright for several reasons. “His argument is not unreasonable,” Wu said. “He considers both practical matters and appearances. He says he is ordinarily so sharp-tongued that if he takes a concubine, people will certainly abuse him to his face and behind his back, saying prosperity has gone to his head. If he chooses some unsuitable woman he cannot present in company, they will laugh all the more. Since he must bear that reputation anyway, he should at least enjoy a real beauty. Only then is it worthwhile. If she is truly an admirable woman, the people who mock and abuse him will eventually envy and respect him instead. That would mean something.”

“Then what exactly does he require?” “First, looks, naturally. ‘A lovely wife and a beautiful concubine’—the saying settles it. Why take a concubine who is not beautiful? Second, a good temper: she must not bully Mrs Zhou.” Hu nodded approvingly. “Good! Shaotang has a conscience.” “Now things have changed again. The purpose of taking a concubine is to continue the family line, so a third requirement must be added: she should have the look of a woman who will bear sons.” “Then tell me what you think of someone: my seventh, the one surnamed Zhu.” Wu stared at him for a long moment. “Mr Hu, you mean to give your seventh concubine to Old Zhou?” “Why not? Did General Nian not do the same?” “Not only General Nian. Giving away concubines was common in former times. But anyone from your household will be accustomed to a great deal. Mr Hu, you should consider this carefully.” “What do you think is wrong with it?” “First, would she feel wronged? Second, with the food and comforts she is used to, could she adapt to life with Old Zhou?” “She could. To tell you the truth, I not only had Luo Sijie ask her; I spoke to her myself this morning. She will not feel wronged. Besides, she is a doctor’s daughter, educated and able to read. Her understanding is different from other women’s. With Shaotang, she would be like Luo Sijie with me. She knows we are thinking of her welfare.”

“Very well. But what about Old Zhou? Have you discussed it with him?” “Of course.” “What did he say?” Hu smiled. “However close the friendship, in a matter like this there must always be some polite refusal.” “I am not entirely convinced. Old Zhou may be accommodating on the outside, but he has firm principles within. If he thinks something wrong, he will never do it.” “Why should he refuse? She meets all three of your requirements. If he feels awkward about taking a friend’s concubine, that depends on the people around him. Encourage him and he will accept; disapprove and he will not be able to agree. You and Zheng Junsheng must give him a little encouragement today. There is something else I want to ask of you, and only you can do it.” “Please tell me.” “Settle matters with Mrs Zhou first.” Wu patted his chest. “Leave that to me. A gentleman helps bring others’ good intentions to fulfilment. I shall go at once.” “Good. But speak to her privately. It would be best not to tell Shaotang beforehand, or let anyone else know. Please be very careful.” “Understood. I shall give you a signal afterwards.”

They went in opposite directions, Hu towards the front and Wu towards the back. As Hu reached the hall, Zheng Junsheng was coming in. Looking from the light into the shade, Zheng could not see clearly; only after climbing the steps and hearing Hu greet him did he recognize him. “So Mr Hu is here!” His comic role in an’kang had trained him to improvise and turn anything into a joke. After a moment’s surprise he smiled. “No wonder the magpies called to me this morning. Meeting the God of Wealth must mean my luck is coming.” Hu was about to say that the God of Wealth had lost his luck. But would that not imply that Zheng too was unlucky, encountering a man in misfortune? He smiled and changed his answer. “Only the God of Wealth has gone barefoot.” “Barefoot or not, the God of Wealth is still the God of Wealth.” “An old friend indeed, still flattering me.” Hu felt that this title ought not to be bestowed on him for nothing. He began considering how he might show his appreciation. The host interrupted his thoughts. “Please sit down, both of you. How good of Mr Hu to remember his old friends! Let us sit and talk.” “First tell me,” Zheng asked, “what business did you want to discuss?” “Nothing except asking you here to keep Mr Hu company.” “Oh? Was there any particular reason for choosing me?” “He is thinking of old times and wanted to see friends who used to be with him every day.” “Who else is coming?” “There was no time today. I invited only you and Old Wu.” Zhou suddenly remembered. “Where has Old Wu gone?” “Here I am!” Wu emerged from behind the screen. “I was talking to Sister-in-Law in the back.” “Have you settled it?” Hu asked. “Yes.” Those few words conveyed their message. Zhou noticed nothing, and Zheng had no idea that anything lay beneath them. Hu likewise showed no outward sign, merely continuing his private calculations.

“Master,” A’chun came to ask, “the dishes are ready. Shall we serve?” “Everyone is here. Go ahead.” The table was opened out and the places laid. There were many dishes, some ordered from Baofan’er and some cooked at home. The principal dish was fish-head tofu, called mulang tofu in Hangzhou, mulang being an allusive expression for a head. There were also two simple dishes: a mixture of sliced pork, bean-curd skin and greens, called “meat and vegetables,” and “three-shrimp tofu,” cooked in a pale sauce with shrimp oil, shrimp roe and a few shelled shrimps. These were dishes Hu and Zhou had often ordered when pooling their money with friends for an evening meal in their poor days. Since Hu longed so deeply for the past, Zhou had ordered them specially to revive those memories. The home-cooked dishes were far more elaborate. An enormous yipin pot held a fat chicken cooked with ham, with twenty pigeon eggs added. There was also a whitefish steamed with wine lees. Those two dishes and the fish-head tofu alone filled the square table. “Please, Mr Hu, take the seat of honour.” “No, no. Today Mr Wu should sit first, Junsheng next, and I third.” “That cannot be right,” Wu said. “Junsheng and I are regular visitors here. You seldom come. The place of honour is yours.” “Please sit first. I have my reasons and shall explain them later. If they are wrong, you may make me drink a penalty cup. Will that do?” Wu caught a hint and nodded. “Better to obey than insist on ceremony. Junsheng, let us sit.” Once they were seated, wine was poured. The warmed Huadiao gave off a rich fermented fragrance. Fond of his drink, Zheng exclaimed, “Excellent wine!” He drained a cup and recited with a smile: “Spring is no season for studying books; summer’s heat is best for sleep. By the time autumn comes, winter is here—a cup of old wine makes an immortal of me.”

They all laughed. “Junsheng, you must perform something for me today,” Hu said. “Only say what you want to hear. A pity I did not bring a sanxian; I shall have to sing without accompaniment.” “Your speciality is Ma the Rake, and there is more talking than singing in that. The sanxian will hardly matter.” “I know where to borrow one. No difficulty!” Zhou raised his cup high. “Come, come. Wine and food should both be taken hot.” Some sipped; others drained their cups. Zheng finished his and displayed the empty bottom. “To tell the truth, I never expected to be drinking here with Mr Hu today.” It could sound as though he were mocking Hu’s fall. To dispel any possible misunderstanding, Zhou quickly said, “Nor did I expect the honour of his visit. Such a rare gathering deserves a proper drinking session. Nobody goes home sober.” “Since old friends have so seldom met, there is something I want to say from the heart.” Hu paused, looked round and let his gaze settle on Zheng. “Junsheng, how have things been with you lately?” Unsure what lay behind the question, Zheng thought, then answered, “Much as always. Never enough to fill me, never so little as to starve.” “What would it take to fill you?” Nobody had ever asked him that, and he had never considered it himself. He hesitated. Then he remembered an idea he had once entertained and immediately dismissed as a foolish dream. He spoke it aloud. “If only I could start a troupe of my own.”

“Oh? How would you go about it?” “With money, I could start at once.” “Tell me.” By now Zhou and Wu both understood Hu’s intention. They urged Zheng on with their eyes. Zheng understood too: Hu meant to help finance him. Yet he hesitated. He had always refused to ask Hu for assistance. To do so now, after Hu’s business had failed, would hardly be reasonable. “Go on!” Zhou urged. “You said it yourself: Mr Hu may be barefoot, but he is still the God of Wealth. Helping you with a thousand taels or so to start a troupe would be no effort at all.” “To refuse would be disrespectful; to accept leaves me ashamed. And I feel a little regret besides. Why? I ought to have spoken to Mr Hu long ago. Then it would have been more than a thousand taels. But”—Zheng raised his voice to make his turn—“had I asked earlier, he might have given me ten thousand. That would have been flowers added to brocade. A thousand now is charcoal brought through snow, and the kindness is greater.” Zhou stared, then laughed. “Two expressions everybody knows—flowers on brocade and charcoal in snow. You have found a novel way of using them.” “Yet, when one thinks about it, he has not used them wrongly,” Wu said. “Mr Hu is himself out in the snow and still brings charcoal to others. That makes it all the more remarkable. Come, let us drink, and wish Junsheng’s troupe a splendid future.” “Thank you for those auspicious words.” Zheng drained his cup and said excitedly, “Either this troupe will come to nothing, or, if it succeeds, you will see: every performer will be first-rate.” “Exactly what I think. Either do not undertake something, or do it properly. Junsheng, go ahead without hesitation. Do not worry about money. I can still raise three or five thousand taels.”

Zheng nodded, blinking rapidly, as though some other calculation occupied him. Just then A’qiu came and whispered in Zhou’s ear, “Madam would like to see you.” “What about?” “I do not know. She only asks you to spare a moment. She has something to say.” “Very well.” Zhou stood. “Excuse me. I shall be back shortly.” Once he had gone, Zheng seemed about to speak, then stopped. After hesitating, he finally began, first giving Wu a look that asked him to listen carefully. “Mr Hu, I have an idea. You provide the capital, and I shall start a troupe. Even allowing generously for everything, two thousand taels would be the most it could cost. But give me five thousand. I shall keep the other three thousand in reserve.” He cast Wu another glance, inviting him to join in. Wu was observant and understood the ways of the world. He recognized the purpose: Hu could reclaim the three thousand at any time. It also hinted at keeping money safe for him. Ever since confiscation had entered Chinese criminal law, people had placed property with close relatives and friends to preserve it. But accepting such property was itself an offence, so those asked often hesitated. Fewer than one in a hundred volunteered. Wu believed Zheng was acting courageously out of friendship, with no intention of profiting from Hu’s distress. To Hu, however, the sum would be too small to discuss. Wu therefore pretended not to understand and remained silent. Zheng’s concern was in fact far-reaching. Imagining the worst, he feared that dismissal would be followed by confiscation, leaving Hu’s family without a livelihood. Then, besides the three thousand taels, there would be a troupe established with Hu’s capital, whose earnings could support them. Zheng himself would be content to draw a salary as an employee. None of this occurred to Hu. He readily agreed. “Good. I shall lend you five thousand. If people say, ‘For tanhuang, you must hear Zheng Junsheng’s troupe,’ my five thousand will have been well spent.” Turning to Wu, he added, “Come to my house tomorrow. Besides Junsheng’s business, there is something else I want to discuss.”

Zhou returned and resumed his seat without speaking, merely raising his cup to his guests. He seemed distracted: his cup reached his lips and was set down again; his chopsticks hovered in the air as though he could not decide where to begin. Hu and Wu exchanged knowing smiles. Zheng was puzzled. “What has happened? You seem quite bewildered, as though something were on your mind.” “There is—something quite unprecedented.” Zhou looked at Hu. “Mr Hu, what am I to say?” Mrs Zhou had summoned him to discuss Hu’s gift of a concubine. She was truly a considerate wife and delighted at the prospect. Although Wu, following Hu’s instructions, had asked her not to tell Zhou beforehand, she feared that her husband, uncertain of her feelings, would answer any proposal with, “I must consult my wife first.” That would only create needless complications. Better to make her position clear at once. Zhou was naturally overjoyed by this unexpected marriage, but friendship required a show of reluctance. Now someone else must encourage him. At a glance from Hu, Wu told Zheng, “We shall be drinking at Old Zhou’s wedding.” “Oh, excellent!” Experienced in the world, Zheng had noticed the subtle expressions passing between Zhou and Hu and guessed something of the truth. “One of the young ladies in Mr Hu’s household is to become a concubine in the Zhou family, I suppose?” By “young ladies” he meant maids. “You are half right,” Wu answered. “It is a concubine being given, not a maid. Hangzhou had General Nian in the past; now it has Mr Hu.” He explained the circumstances and praised Mistress Zhu warmly. “Congratulations! Another fine West Lake story,” Zheng said. “But General Nian had a purpose when he gave away concubines. Mr Hu wants both to continue a friend’s family line and to find a good home for the woman. It is an open, honourable act of extraordinary generosity. To keep this a good thing to the end, however, I think there is a precaution we should take.”

“Please explain. What should we do?” Hu asked attentively. “First let me tell you that General Nian gave away more than one concubine, and not only in Hangzhou. Every woman he gave away was pregnant…” In Zheng’s account, Nian Gengyao’s ancestors had originally borne the surname Yan and came from Huaiyuan in Anhui. The founder of the line was Yan Fu, who passed the examinations and became a jinshi. When the list of successful candidates was written, his surname Yan was mistakenly written as Nian. Under the regulations he could have requested a correction from the Ministry of Rites, but all the formalities would have had to be completed before he could receive an appointment. Rather than delay his career, he accepted the error and used the name Nian Fu. On entering office he was sent to Liaodong as inspecting censor, and his descendants settled there. When the Qing founder Taizu took up arms, the Han Chinese of Liaodong were captured and made bondservants, called booi. They were divided between the Upper Three and the Lower Five Banners. Those of the Upper Three came under the Imperial Household Department; those of the Lower Five served the princes. During the Kangxi reign, Nian Gengyao’s father Nian Xialing, his elder brother Nian Xiyao and Nian Gengyao himself all belonged to the household of Prince Yong, the future Yongzheng Emperor. Nian Gengyao’s younger sister had been the prince’s secondary consort and later became a Noble Consort. After following their rulers through the passes into China, bondservants could also sit the examinations. With powerful imperial relatives to support them, advancement could come in the twinkling of an eye.

Nian Xialing rose to be governor of Huguang; Nian Xiyao too became a senior official of the second rank. Nian Gengyao entered the Hanlin Academy in the thirty-ninth year of Kangxi. Recommended by Prince Yong, he became governor-general of Sichuan. This was actually a move in the prince’s struggle for the succession. In his later years, Kangxi had already chosen an heir: Prince Yong’s full brother, the fourteenth imperial son Yinzhen, Prince Xun. When ordered to campaign in Qinghai as commander-in-chief, he was specially permitted to use the Plain Yellow Banner, implying that he represented the emperor on campaign and that Heaven’s mandate would pass to him. His future accession was an open secret. Nian Gengyao was his chief support in the Qinghai campaign. In the winter of Kangxi’s sixty-first year, the emperor fell gravely ill and urgently summoned Prince Xun to the capital, but Nian, commanding powerful forces, held him back. Prince Yong was thus able to collude with Kangxi’s confidant Longkodo, whom he later honoured as “Uncle,” and skilfully seize the throne. Yongzheng concealed his intentions deeply. Soon after taking power, he decided to kill both Longkodo and Nian to silence them. At first he flattered Nian and showed him every favour, encouraging his arrogance. Already overbearing, Nian believed he held the emperor’s secret and was therefore untouchable. His disloyal ambitions grew until his conduct resembled that of a second Wu Sangui. But circumstances had changed. Even Wu Sangui had failed; what hope had Nian? Yongzheng simultaneously stripped away his supporters and emptied his military authority of substance. Only when his pleas for mercy were refused did Nian realize that his whole family faced destruction. He therefore gave away pregnant concubines in the hope of preserving his own bloodline.

None of those present had heard this account before. “Then did Nian leave any descendants?” Wu asked. “Yes. There is an unusual surname, Sheng—the last character of my own name, Zheng Junsheng. Everyone surnamed Sheng is descended from Nian Gengyao.” “Why choose such an odd surname?” “There is a story behind that too. Turn the character Nian upside down and move its first slanting stroke to the top. Does it not look like Sheng?” Zheng continued, “But enough of the digression. Back to our business. Suppose Mistress Zhu is already pregnant. If the bloodlines of the two families become confused, that will be unfortunate.” “Ah!” Wu looked at Hu. “We must ask Mr Hu about that.” “It is hard to be certain.” Hu thought for a while. “Old Zheng has a point. What begins as a good thing should not lead to misunderstanding. To be safe, I have a plan. Let us settle the match now. Shaotang can first find a place where she can live alone for two months. Once it is clear that everything is normal, they can consummate the marriage. What do you think?” “Yes, yes!” Zheng and Wu agreed together. “Then you two shall be the matchmakers. I must trouble you to see to the arrangements.”

So that was why Hu had insisted that Wu and Zheng take the places of honour. Matters had gone this far; Zhou now put aside embarrassment and empty protestations. He offered toasts to each in turn, beginning with Hu. “Mr Hu, there is nothing I need say. Distance tests a horse’s strength; time reveals a person’s heart. If my family line can be preserved, all our Zhou ancestors in the other world will kowtow to you.” “A slip of the tongue!” Hu said. “How can you say such a thing? Drink a penalty.” “Yes, a penalty.” After finishing a second cup, Zhou raised another to Wu. “You should toast him first.” Wu pointed at Zheng. “Without his foresight, there might have been a misunderstanding that turned a fine affair into a dreadful mess. We should have been beyond tears.” “Quite right,” Hu said. “For that thought, I must drink to Old Zheng myself.” “You are too kind.” All three drained their cups. Finally it was Wu’s turn. “Old Zhou, let me manage the wedding for you.” “Nothing could be better. I leave it in your hands.” The host was the first to become drunk. Hu, who had little capacity for wine, drank cautiously and remained as clearheaded as ever. When the meal ended, he invited Wu home for a long night’s conversation. Wu gladly agreed. They thanked their host, said goodbye to Zheng, and set off on foot for Yuanbao Street with the servant.

Halfway home they saw a sedan chair approaching, preceded and followed by two large, bright lanterns. Hu recognized it at once. He tugged Wu’s sleeve and stopped in the middle of the stone-paved road. As it drew nearer, he saw that he was right: each large lantern bore “Qingyu Hall” on one side and “Hu” on the other, in broad, flattened Song-style lettering. On inquiry, he learned that Madam Luosi, worried about him, had sent the chair to fetch him. But there were two men and only one chair. Since they were close to home, he ordered it carried back empty and continued walking with Wu. Yuanbao Street felt strangely unfamiliar. Ordinarily, when he returned at night, lanterns lit the whole way from the street entrance to his gate. Tonight it was black. In the distance only a single lantern burned at the side gate. Most desolate of all was the garden. Its twelve towers and pavilions stood in dark shadow. Hundred Lions Tower alone was brightly lit, yet its light made the scene seem lonelier, sharpening the sense of a world changed, its people gone and buildings empty. A’yun came to meet them. Noticing the guest, she looked closely. “Why, it is Mr Wu!” “Mr Wu will stay here tonight. Tell Madam Luosi.” She hurried back. By the time they reached Hundred Lions Tower, Madam Luosi herself was holding the door curtain aside. For some reason, the sight of Wu suddenly overwhelmed her with grief. Tears sprang from her eyes. She turned away, wiped them quickly, and turned back to greet him. “Please have some tea.” She attended to him herself. “You are too kind. Thank you.” Seeing how worn she looked, Wu was concerned. “Luo Sijie, your responsibilities are greater than ever now. You must take care of yourself.” “Ah!” she sighed. “It really is like a dream.” Wu raised two fingers to his lips, warning her away from such dispiriting talk.

“I hear you walked back. With that fierce northwesterly wind, your faces are red with cold.” Madam Luosi called, “A’yun, bring hot water for them to wash!” “My face is not too cold, but my feet are frozen.” Glancing back, she saw Hu talking to A’yun and lowered her voice. “Have you heard about today?” “Yes.” “Do you think we did right?” “Yes. If one can take something up, one must be able to put it down. It was the right thing.” “He could take it up, but could not put it down. Today he had to let go because he could no longer carry it. Mr Wu, if it were you, could you accept such a defeat?” “What good would refusing to accept it do?” Hu put in from the other side of the room. Wu said nothing. After a pause, Madam Luosi answered, “I will not accept it. We must discuss it properly later.” She asked Wu, “Are you hungry?” “No, not at all.” “Then wine first, and refreshments afterwards.” Turning to Hu, she asked, “Shall Mr Wu sleep in the downstairs study?” “Yes. Let us go down there to drink and talk.” This meant that their conversation would last well into the night. Madam Luosi agreed and took A’yun downstairs to make arrangements. Watching her, Wu felt more deeply moved and concerned. A question that had long troubled him now demanded an answer.

“Mr Hu, let me repeat an old saying: without office, a man is free of burdens. What are your plans?” “You are only half right. Without office, yes; free of burdens, scarcely.” “Then we must find a way to lighten them. Will Lord Zuo not help?” “His powers are limited now too. Yingchun has gone to Nanjing. We shall see what he has to say when he returns.” Wu pondered for a long time before he could bring himself to ask, “What further action will the court take? Will your property be confiscated?” “Not if the public debts are repaid. The pawnshops taken into official custody can be set against them and repay them gradually. I need not concern myself with those. It is the private deposits: I do not know what proportion I shall eventually be able to repay. Whenever I think of that, it is like a thousand-jin burden on my shoulders, bending me double.” “Then it is your heart that is burdened, rather than your person. Can you not take things more lightly?” “How?” “Stop thinking about them.” Hu smiled without answering and changed the subject. “Mr Wu, do not forget Shaotang’s business. Discuss it properly with Luo Sijie later.” Wu shook his head. “Even now, you think only of other people’s private affairs.” “Only that lets me stop thinking about my own. I cannot manage my affairs, so I occupy myself with other people’s. If I manage theirs well, it gives me some comfort. In that sense I am attending to my own affairs too.” “That is the meaning of finding happiness in doing good. A pity that this year…”

“I understand. That is exactly what I wanted to discuss.” Hu continued, “I walked to Shaotang’s today, and the northwesterly wind stung my face. I thought: here I am in a fox-fur robe and a sable cap. My padded shoes may be old, but their soles show more white than black and are as good as new. If I am cold dressed like this, how will people without even a padded coat survive the winter? Before I went to Shanghai, my mother sent word down from the hills that this year’s distribution of padded clothing and congee should continue as usual. But in my present circumstances, how can I still do charitable work?” “That is what I meant by a pity. You still have the means, but if you do it, people are certain to say very ugly things.” Wu sighed. “Only now do I understand that even doing good requires the right opportunity.” “Exactly. Walking back with you, the cold made me think of it again. This good work must still be done. Can you see a way?” “You cannot appear in it yourself. If you do, people will abuse you, and it will even affect the settlement of your debts.” “Precisely. I was thinking of asking you to appear for me.” Wu shook his head repeatedly. “Nobody would believe it. Who am I? What standing have I to distribute padded clothing and congee?” While they were unable to find an answer, A’yun came upstairs at Madam Luosi’s direction. Everything was ready in the study; host and guest were invited down for supper.

Supper was plentiful, the lamps shone brilliantly, and the fire blazed. Wu knew that few evenings as a guest in such surroundings remained to him. He treasured this one all the more, putting aside his worries to enjoy the moment, sipping slowly and savouring the fine wine and food. Only when the second pot of Huadiao was warmed did he speak. “Mr Hu, I have thought of a way. Why not make an anonymous donation and specify its purpose? It would achieve the same result.” As he spoke, Madam Luosi asked, “What are you discussing?” “That matter my mother spoke of.” Hu briefly explained. “Then how much do you intend to give?” “What do you think?” “In former winters, the padded clothing and congee cost thirty thousand taels. We cannot manage that now. I think ten thousand at the most.” “Good. A fitting middle course. Ten thousand it is.” “Mr Wu can take the money and make the donation. But even an anonymous donor’s real name becomes known to someone. I think we should say it is the old lady’s private savings, given without your knowledge. That is the only position you can defend.” Both men agreed wholeheartedly. The depths of winter had arrived; there could be no delay. Madam Luosi left the table and went upstairs to see to it. In her dressing table lay an account of the private possessions discovered in the concubines’ rooms that day—jewels, gold and silver. She would see whether ten thousand taels could be assembled from them.

“Mr Hu, you cannot think only of charity,” Wu said. “You must consider yourself too and keep something in reserve.” Hu remained silent. After a while he suddenly asked, “You enjoy calligraphy and painting, Mr Wu. Before I hand mine over, would you like to choose a few pieces?” He expected polite refusals and thought he would have to press hard before Wu accepted. Instead, Wu answered immediately with one word: “Yes.” Hu pulled a red silk cord. A clear bell rang: a Western-style servant bell, connected to the corridor and the upper floor. Two maids soon appeared. Madam Luosi also sent A’yun down to ask what was needed. “Bring the picture chests over. There is not enough light.” Candles could not be used for examining paintings. A’yun called for two more servants and additional oil lamps, then fetched the keys and opened the chests. When buying paintings and antiques, Hu had made little distinction between genuine and false, fine and crude. A high price was what mattered to him. Antique dealers all knew a story about this. A man once offered him a Song painting. It was genuine, and the price reasonable. The sale was all but settled when the man said, “Mr Hu, I am making no profit on this. The price is a bargain.” “If you cannot make a profit from me, where can you? Take it away. I do not want to take advantage of you.” The sale fell through. Hence every dealer who came to Hu asked a high price and, after concluding the sale, thanked him profusely. Wu knew this field well and had bought several fine works for him. He was broadly familiar with the Qingyu Hall collection. Before the Meifu kerosene lamps arrived, he said, “Let me look at the calligraphy rubbings first.”

Rubbings from inscribed stelae were commonly called “black tigers.” Hu said excitedly, “I have a black tiger that really is tiger meat. It cost me three thousand taels. To tell the truth, what caught my eye was the gold lettering in the Qianlong Emperor’s own hand.” “I have heard that you own a Tang rubbing of the Huadu Temple Stele. A Song rubbing would already be immensely valuable. I should like to see a Tang one.” “A’yun, where is that album?” “I think it is with Mistress Zhu.” “Oh. Is Mistress Zhu still in her own rooms?” “She has moved into a guest room. Her old rooms are locked.” “Then we cannot get the album just now?” “Let me ask her first.” As soon as A’yun left, four maids filed in, each carrying a Meifu kerosene lamp with a white-copper base and a glass shade. The study became as bright as day. Hu handed Wu the bunch of keys to the picture chests. “Please help yourself.”

Wu went about it as though handling his own collection. First he surveyed the chests: about seven chi long, four wide and three high, made of camphorwood. There were eight, arranged side by side against the north wall and labelled with the first eight characters of the Thousand Character Classic: Heaven, Earth, Dark, Yellow, Universe, Cosmos, Vast, Primeval. There were also eight keys, engraved with numbers. Naturally the key numbered one opened the Heaven chest. Inside, on top, lay a booklet entitled Catalogue of Calligraphy, Paintings and Rubbings in the Hu Family Collection of Qingyu Hall. “That makes things easy,” Wu said happily. “I shall read the catalogue first.” It had three main divisions—fine calligraphy, famous paintings and ink rubbings—each subdivided by dynasty. The first calligraphy entry was “Western Jin: Lu Ji, The Pingfu Letter, handscroll on paper.” Wu was so startled that for a moment he could not speak. “What is it?” Hu asked. “At least fifteen hundred years have passed since the Western Jin, and a work on paper has survived! This is more precious than Wang Xizhi’s Preface to the Orchid Pavilion. The original of that went into the coffin with Emperor Taizong of Tang. To find genuine writing earlier still—what extraordinary good fortune for the eyes!” Hu smiled. “Look at it before you say more.”

With great care Wu took out the scroll. Its mounting was exquisite: a blue kesi silk wrapper patterned with birds and flowers, a mutton-fat white jade roller, and a coral fastening pin. He withdrew the pin and unrolled it. At the beginning, over a yellow silk mounting strip, lay a pale-blue silk label inscribed by Emperor Huizong of Song with six Chinese characters: “Jin: Lu Ji’s Pingfu Letter.” Beneath it was a double-dragon seal. Another very old silk label read, “Written by Lu Shiheng of Wu Commandery, Administrator of Pingyuan under the Jin.” The paper was ivory-coloured, the characters about half a cun high. There were nine lines of zhangcao, an early cursive script. Studying them closely, Wu could recognize only a tenth. But every word of Dong Qichang’s later inscription was clear: “Before the General of the Right, after Yuanchang, only these few lines remain: a treasure rare through the ages.”

Wu had seen several examples of Dong Qichang’s hand. Careful comparison convinced him that this inscription was not genuine. Reluctant to say so outright, he merely rolled the scroll up. “Well?” Hu asked. “There seem to be some grounds for doubt.” “Your eye is good. It is a fake.” Hu pointed at the catalogue. “Look at a few genuine things.” Experts had already examined the collection for him and marked the catalogue. There were three kinds of mark. A single circle meant a forgery; a double circle indicated uncertain authenticity, or a copy valuable in its own right, such as a Song copy of a Tang painting. Works accepted without question as genuine bore a little plum-blossom stamp. These made up about a fifth of the catalogue. Wu next selected Twelve Letters of the Su Family, containing letters by Su Laoquan, Dongpo, Ziyou and Dongpo’s youngest son Shudang. One glance showed they were genuine. “I need not inspect every original. I can choose from the catalogue. How many pieces do you intend to give me?” “You decide.” “Then I should like everything bearing a plum-blossom stamp.” He immediately added, “To keep safe for you. Mr Hu, do you trust me?” Hu had not anticipated his real purpose. But this would mean secretly concealing assets and would not be honourable. After considering, he answered firmly, “There is no need, Mr Wu. I shall still give you a few pieces. Choose carefully.” Wu had meant well, but could say no more when Hu refused. Still hoping to preserve as much as possible for him, he selected, besides the Su letters, Emperor Huizong’s Thousand Character Classic in Slender Gold Script, Dong Yuan’s Dragon Emerging from Hibernation in Wind and Rain, and a handscroll of The Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove by Zhao Mengfu. Together, these four works were estimated to be worth more than ten thousand taels.

Under Hu’s direction, the maids opened three chests, found all the works intended for Wu and tied them into a bundle. Madam Luosi and A’yun returned one after the other. The Tang rubbing of the Huadu Temple Stele could not be located for the moment, so they let it pass. The ten thousand taels for the charitable hall had been assembled, all in banknotes. They were counted out and handed to Wu at once. Then the table was laid for wine and supper. “Three cups and sets of chopsticks,” Hu told A’yun. “Sit with us.” He meant Madam Luosi. She regarded Wu as an elder of her own family, so there was no need for the formal separation of men and women. Seated with them, she sipped green Western mint liqueur from a little glass while Wu drank aged Huadiao. Hu took his usual medicinal wine before bed. “Has Old Seven moved to a guest room?” he asked. He sometimes called Mistress Zhu Old Seven. “At her own request. She said that after all the bustle and company, she would not be able to sleep in such sudden emptiness.”

Hu nodded and looked at Wu, signalling him to speak. Wu described the day’s events at Zhou’s house and ended with Zheng’s concern. “That will not be the case,” Madam Luosi said. “Every night the master spends in each room is marked in the almanac. I checked. His last night with Mistress Zhu was more than two months ago. As for…” She had been about to joke that she could not know whether Hu and Mistress Zhu had been intimate in private since. But nobody was in a joking mood, so she swallowed the words. “Better to be careful. There is no harm in waiting another month and sending her to Zhou’s only when she shows no sign of pregnancy.” “Very well. Where will she stay for this month or more?” “At my house,” Wu said. “Then we can be still easier in our minds.” Hu made a decisive gesture. “That settles it.” “I understand,” Madam Luosi said. “I shall arrange everything.”

Now they were to speak frankly of their deepest concerns and fundamental plans. Wu began. “Mr Hu, do you believe this fall is final?” “What can I do but accept it?” “I do not accept it!” Madam Luosi put in. “A road is made by those who walk it.” Hu replied calmly, “Age does not spare a man. After a fall like this, getting back on my feet would be difficult enough. How could I talk of making a new road?” “Not so. If you can stand upright, you can walk,” Wu said. “Do not lose heart. A comeback depends on human effort.” “I appreciate your encouragement. But honestly, everything is easier said than done. You speak of rising again from the Eastern Mountain, but I do not even know where that mountain is.” “All this dispirited talk!” Madam Luosi resented his lack of resolve. “You were never like this before.” “Before was before. Now is now.” Hu too grew agitated. “I am a commoner of the first rank, dismissed from office. I do not even know whether confiscation will follow. There is no point discussing anything else.” Mention of confiscation brought another earnest appeal from Wu. “You must keep some capital in reserve.” Hu said nothing. Madam Luosi, however, was prompted to think. She considered for some time and was about to speak when Hu spoke first.

“If you refuse to accept defeat, I have an idea for you. There is a business you could try.” “You want me to return to my old trade?” She thought he meant embroidery. “No. If you are interested, you could join Yingchun in Shanghai to speculate in land and build houses in lanes. Or you could form a partnership with foreigners and open a firm specializing in foreign jewellery, dress materials and furniture.” “Exactly. Either business would make use of Luo Sijie’s strengths,” Wu said approvingly. “If Mr Hu has fallen, she can make a career of her own. What is lost at sunrise may be regained at sunset.” “I shall have to depend on you from now on.” Hu joked at his own expense. “Who would have thought that in my old age I should live on ‘slipper rice’—supported by a woman?” “What a dreadful thing to say!” She gave him a look. Both men laughed. Wu continued, “But neither business can be started with a small sum. While you still have some control, you should make arrangements early.” Again he was urging Hu to disperse his assets and lodge them elsewhere. Hu would not do that, but thought Madam Luosi should be reminded of something. “See to your own private savings. Surely you have long had enough capital for either venture?” “No ready money. My cash is at Fukang. Who knows how much I shall recover? I have some jewellery, but that is difficult to sell too.” “Take it out soon and ask Mr Wu to carry it to Shanghai. Yingchun can find a way of disposing of it.”

“I do not have it with me.” “Where is it? Have you left it in someone’s keeping?” “With the Zhu family at Jindong Bridge.” Hu fell silent, his expression darkening. At the sight, Madam Luosi’s heart sank. Something must be wrong. “What is it? Are the Zhus not old family connections? Their eldest son’s wife is an excellent woman.” “She is. But the old lady in that household would devour a person without leaving a bone.” “Oh!” Madam Luosi was alarmed. “Old Madam Zhu is a devout vegetarian, always chanting sutras. And the family is famously well off. Surely…” “Surely she would not swallow up your property?” Hu asked, with a trace of mockery. “Yes. I cannot believe she would be so wicked.” “Wickedness was how her family made its fortune in the first place…” Wu interrupted. “There must be a story behind that. Is there not, Mr Hu?” “Indeed. Let me tell it to you.”
