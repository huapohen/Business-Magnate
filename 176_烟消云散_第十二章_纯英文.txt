CHAPTER TWELVE: FOXES IN THE CITY WALL, RATS IN THE SHRINE

The story Hu Xueyan told concerned the digging up of buried treasure. After a great upheaval, when refugees were being brought home and order was gradually restored, people would often become rich overnight. Nine times out of ten, they had found a hidden hoard. There were two sorts of people who buried gold, silver, and valuables. The first were wealthy families fleeing the ravages of war. They could take only readily saleable things such as gold and pearls; silver and antiques were packed into durable containers and buried deep in some inconspicuous place, to be recovered when they returned after the war. If the whole family was killed, or the head of the household or old servant who knew the secret died without an opportunity to pass it on, the fortune remained underground until, years later, someone destined for a windfall came upon it. The other sort had already acquired a fortune but could not enjoy it because the situation had suddenly changed. They buried it for the time being and waited for a better opportunity. In the early Tongzhi years, the Taiping troops buried untold quantities of treasure. Whenever they occupied an important city, they took over the houses of wealthy families as their quarters, a practice known as “setting up a mansion.” When the owners returned, visitors would often arrive claiming that the “Longhairs” had buried valuables somewhere on the premises. If the owner believed them, the next question was how to divide the proceeds: half each, perhaps, or sixty percent for the owner and forty for the informant, whose share would be at least thirty percent. Some searches did turn up treasure, but most such visitors were opportunists. They lost nothing if nothing was found and might as well try their luck with rumors and deception.

Hangzhou was no different after the Taiping troops were driven out: everyone seemed to be digging for treasure. Hu Xueyan had a cousin of one of his parents, whom he called Uncle, named Zhu Baoru, who was particularly keen on it. His wife was the “Old Madam Zhu” of whom Madam Luosi had spoken. Honest-looking but deeply calculating, she told her husband, “You need a way in if you want to find treasure. There is one now. Keep your eyes open, and your luck may turn.” “What way?” “The Reconstruction Bureau. Xueyan is your cousin’s son. Ask him for a post there, and he is bound to agree. But you must be willing to take trouble. Mix with the refugees and listen quietly while they talk about anything and everything. You are certain to hear something useful.” Zhu Baoru had great respect for his wife’s judgment. He went to Hu Xueyan as instructed and volunteered to look after the refugees. Some jobs at the bureau were better than others. The most lucrative was handling claims for women who had lost their fathers and brothers in the fighting and flight. If a young, attractive woman was alone in the world, someone might come to claim her under the pretense of being a relative. Once an understanding had been reached with her, the gratuity could run to a hundred taels or more. Next came food supplies and purchasing, both rich sources of illicit profit. Even copying documents and compiling registers were easy work. Only caring for the refugees was a tangle of troublesome details with nothing to be gained. No one wanted it. Hu Xueyan was delighted that Zhu Baoru had volunteered and appointed him at once.

Following his wife’s advice, Zhu Baoru patiently dealt with the ragged, foul-smelling refugees, resolved their various difficulties, and listened carefully to the secrets that slipped out in casual conversation. He established excellent relations with them. One day a refugee in his thirties, who spoke with a Jiangxi accent, quietly said to him, “Mr Zhu, I have been here for more than half a month, and I can see that you are an honest, kindly elder. I should like to come to your home for a talk.” “Oh?” Zhu Baoru remembered him as a man of few words who had never caused any trouble. He did not even know his surname. “What is your name?” “Cheng.” “Brother Cheng, there is nobody here. Whatever you have to say, go ahead.” “No. There is a great deal to say. It would be more convenient at your home.” Remembering his wife’s words, Zhu Baoru felt a stir of anticipation and took him home. Cheng put down his bundle, untied his belt, and produced more than a dozen gold rings hidden inside it.

“Mr and Mrs Zhu,” he said, “to tell you the truth, I was a Longhair. I have now left the darkness for the light, and I should like to take you as my adoptive parents. Would you be willing to accept me?” The request was rather sudden. Zhu Baoru was still hesitating, but his wife suspected that the bundle held more surprises and readily replied, “We have a son about your age, but he is away. Fate has brought us together. We need not discuss becoming your adoptive parents just yet. Stay with us first.” “No. Only if you accept me as a son will I dare tell you certain things. And once I become your son, I can take the surname Zhu. That will make everything easier.” The couple conferred in private and agreed to adopt him. His surname was changed to Zhu, and since he had been born in the year of the Horse, they gave him the name Jiaju, “the family’s colt.” The gold rings became his introductory gift to his new parents. With money, everything was easy. Zhu Baoru sold two of the rings and fitted Zhu Jiaju out in new clothes. He also bought wine and meat, and they enjoyed a convivial celebration of their new family ties.

Zhu Jiaju seemed never to have known such comfortable days. Delighted, he ate great chunks of meat, drank bowlfuls of wine, and described his life among the Longhairs. An orphan, he had carried baggage for the Taiping troops in his native Jiangxi. When they reached Quzhou in Zhejiang, they released him to go home. He said that he had no home and wished to become a “little Longhair.” Thus he went with them from Quzhou to Hangzhou, though they soon moved on again. This was in the spring of the tenth year of Xianfeng. To relieve the siege of the Heavenly Capital, the Taiping Loyal King, Li Xiucheng, used the strategy of besieging Zhao to rescue Yan. Early in the second month he marched from southern Anhui into Zhejiang, intending to draw back the Zhejiang troops under Brigade Commander Zhang Yuliang, then besieging Jinling, and so ease the pressure. Li entered Hangzhou on the twenty-seventh day of that month. When Zhang’s reinforcements arrived on the third day of the third month, Li withdrew westward: his purpose had been achieved, and he feared that Zhang would cut off his retreat. The occupation had lasted only five days.

Zhu Jiaju served under Li and fought in various places until a defeat separated him from his unit and another Taiping force took him in. His new commander, Wu Tiande, came from the same village and valued such ties. Zhu Jiaju and another fellow villager, Wang Peili, became his trusted personal guards. Later, mortally wounded in battle, Wu told them, “When the Loyal King took Hangzhou the second time, I was stationed there for half a year. My quarters were at Jindong Bridge in the eastern city. When I was transferred, his orders were so strict that I could not take my things. I buried them there and have never had a chance to return. Now that I am dying, I have something to give you.” From a pocket next to his skin he took an oilpaper packet containing a treasure map. He told them to find an opportunity to dig up the hoard and divide it into three shares, sending one to his old home in Jiangxi. He also made them swear brotherhood and vow before Heaven never to betray their trust, on pain of divine punishment.

“Afterward, my sworn elder brother and I agreed to cut the map in two and keep half each. We stayed together all along. Now that Lord Zuo has recovered Hangzhou, our opportunity has come. Since I had been here before, I came ahead disguised as a refugee to find the place. Once I had located it, I meant to send for Wang Peili and discuss how to proceed.” “Where is this sworn brother of yours?” Zhu Baoru asked. “In Shanghai. One letter from me will bring him at once.” “Your sworn brother is one of our own too,” Mrs Zhu said. “Send for him! Tell him to come!” “Wait, wait!” Zhu Baoru waved his hand. “Let us discuss this first. Where is your map?” “I have only half.” Zhu Jiaju, too, took an oilpaper packet from a pocket next to his skin. The half-map was well preserved. Spread flat on the table, it proved to be the upper half, with its bottom edge cut in a zigzag. Presumably this was intended to work like matching halves of a tally: the top edge of the other half was similarly cut, and only when the two fitted perfectly together could they be identified as Wu Tiande’s original map.

“This half gives the location,” Zhu Jiaju said. “The lower half shows exactly where the treasure is buried.” That made sense. Having spent five days in Hangzhou, he had brought the half with the address to find Wu Tiande’s former quarters. Zhu Baoru examined it closely. Two places were named, Jindong Bridge and Wan’an Bridge. There were also two small squares, one labeled “Temple of Lord Guan,” with an arrow and the note: “About thirty paces south; faces west.” The unlabeled square was plainly the house containing the treasure. “That should not be hard to find. And once we find it?” “We rent it or buy it.” “Buy it?” Zhu Baoru hesitated. “A house used by your Longhairs as a mansion can hardly be small. It may cost a great deal.” “That does not matter. Wang Peili will bring money.” “Good!” Zhu Baoru said happily. “Leave this to me.” His wife asked, “Jiaju, what exactly is buried there?” “A great many things…” There were said to be four or five hundred taels’ weight of gold leaf, together with quantities of jewels and ornaments. The method of burial had been elaborate. The jewelry was wrapped in soft paper and placed in porcelain jars sealed with putty. These went into an iron chest, surrounded by lime to keep out damp. Finally the iron chest was placed inside a large wooden chest and buried.

The couple listened with delight. That night they conferred secretly. A sudden adoption of a son whose background was unknown might arouse the neighbors’ suspicions, so they decided to move the next day, to the eastern city, where they could more conveniently search around Jindong Bridge. Once settled, Zhu Baoru told his adopted son to write to Shanghai for Wang Peili. Then he prepared to inspect the neighborhood. “Jiaju, with your out-of-town accent, you would attract suspicion if you went asking questions. Stay here. I shall go alone.” Zhu Jiaju readily agreed. Wan’an Bridge was the foremost bridge within Hangzhou, with a high arch under which grain boats passed. There was a temple to Lord Guan on either side. From their positions relative to Jindong Bridge, the one on the map must be the eastern temple. Beside it was a teahouse. Zhu Baoru ordered a pot and sat from morning until noon, quietly listening to the patrons’ expansive talk. After three days he finally heard what he wanted: accounts of the Taiping troops’ activities during their two occupations. Between Wan’an and Jindong bridges, they had occupied five houses. The one whose position matched the map belonged to a jinshi named Yan.

Now it was easy. Leaving the teahouse, Zhu Baoru found two lanes running south from in front of the temple, one broad and one narrow. He took the broad lane, walked thirty ordinary paces, and came to a stone inscribed with a Mount Tai protective formula. Using it as his reference point, he examined the west-facing houses and soon saw a plaque with bold black characters on a pale ground: “Residence of a Jinshi.” This must be Yan’s home. He did not act rashly but walked past twice, studying the surroundings. The house was not exceptionally fine, but it seemed large. On the second pass someone came out, giving him a view through the sedan-chair hall to the inner gate and a very imposing hall beyond. Afraid of attracting attention, he did not linger. At home he kept his discovery to himself until bedtime, when he and his wife secretly discussed how to make inquiries.

“I cannot simply knock and ask whether they want to sell. At most I can ask whether they have spare rooms to let. If they say no, I must leave, and it will be awkward to go back. That would close the way.” His resourceful wife thought for a moment. “Is not Mr Hu planning another gruel kitchen in the eastern city? Why not use that as your opening?” “Gruel kitchens are not my responsibility.” “What does that matter? Public charities need everyone’s support. Besides, you work for the Reconstruction Bureau.” “Quite right. If Jiaju asks tomorrow, tell him we have not found the place.” “I know. I shall keep him satisfied.” Mrs Zhu was remarkably capable. She attended to every detail of Zhu Jiaju’s food and comfort, so the delay in finding the treasure site did not make him unduly impatient. In fact, Zhu Baoru was already making considerable progress.

Zhu Baoru also proceeded methodically. His wife was right that everyone should support a public charity, and he could approach the household on that basis. Still, a word from Hu Xueyan would give him a stronger pretext. Hu approved of another gruel kitchen but imposed a condition: Zhu Baoru must organize it and then take charge of its management. This was an unexpected opportunity. Even if the treasure came to nothing, running such a kitchen offered the chance of a modest illicit fortune. He readily agreed and went eagerly to call on the jinshi. An old servant named Yan Sheng received him. On hearing his purpose, Yan Sheng explained that the entire family had taken refuge in Shanghai and that he had no authority to decide. He copied out his master’s address and told Zhu to approach him directly. “Very well. But first there are some things I should like to understand. If your master agrees, would he rent the house or sell it?” “I do not know. Since it is for a good cause, he might lend it without charging a single coin.” “That would hardly do. With so many people coming and going every day, a gruel kitchen would leave the house in a dreadful state. I should like to know whether your master would consider selling, and at what price.” “You would have to ask him. Someone did inquire before, but he would only pledge it, not sell. It is the old family home, after all. He might pledge it for a few years and, once things are peaceful, rebuild it and live here again.”

Zhu Baoru asked to look around, and Yan Sheng readily agreed. The west-facing residence had three successive ranges of buildings, with an additional courtyard south of the second hall. Three elegant rooms stood on each of its eastern and western sides. The adjoining galleries were linked by a raised pavilion three zhang square. In the center of the courtyard was a stone flower bed five chi high, its blue-stone retaining walls exquisitely carved. Yan Sheng said that the elderly master had excelled at growing peonies, including such famous varieties as Wei Purple and Yao Yellow. Every spring, when they bloomed, he would hold a banquet in the square pavilion, drinking wine, admiring the flowers, and composing poems to assigned rhymes. Several stones engraved with these poems were set into the gallery walls. The choice peonies, of course, had long since been destroyed. The flower bed was now overgrown with weeds. As he looked, Zhu Baoru calculated. With such a passion for peonies, and a specially designed bed that must have been costly and required particular attention to soil and drainage, the Yan family would never willingly sell unless its fortunes had declined. A pledge for a limited term, however, might be negotiable. Hu Xueyan knew almost every Hangzhou gentleman who had taken refuge in Shanghai and was friendly with many. A letter from him ought to settle the matter. His wife rejected the idea at once. “Do not involve Mr Hu. He must know Mr Yan. One letter saying that everyone should help a good cause, and Yan will let you use the house as it stands. Then you will have no control over it. Even if you do, you cannot shut the gates and attend to our own business. Think!” Zhu Baoru woke as if from a dream. “True, true! How do you think we should begin?” “There is no hurry. Think three moves ahead before taking one. If we proceed steadily and safely, the gold and jewels underground cannot fly away.”

Counting on her fingers, Mrs Zhu laid out the steps. First, they must keep Hu Xueyan satisfied and prevent the eastern-city gruel kitchen from passing into anyone else’s hands. Second, when Wang Peili arrived, they must find out how much money he had brought, and whether it was silver or valuables. If valuables, how could they be sold? They must raise enough to take the house in pledge before proceeding to the third step. That was for Zhu Baoru to go to Shanghai himself, secure an introduction to the jinshi, and negotiate. What he said would depend on the circumstances. “In a word, everything must be kept secret. Do not use Mr Hu lightly. He is too powerful a piece on the board: once brought into play, he cannot be recalled, and the whole business will be ruined.” Zhu Baoru agreed repeatedly. Whenever Hu asked about the kitchen, he replied that he was still looking for suitable premises. Since the proposal had come from Zhu himself, Hu did not press him when he seemed unenthusiastic.

A few days later Wang Peili wrote to say when he would take the passenger boat. On the appointed day, Zhu Baoru and his adopted son met him and brought him home, where Jiaju introduced his adoptive mother. The couple deliberately withdrew to let the two men talk privately. Jiaju described how he had met Zhu Baoru and warmly praised his adoptive mother’s attentiveness. Wang Peili was more discerning. “Your adoptive father seems a decent man,” he said. “But that mother of yours looks a formidable character.” “She is certainly shrewd, but I have seen nothing formidable about her.” “Tell people only three-tenths of what is in your heart; never lay it all bare. Have you found the place?” “Father says he has seen one that looks very like it. He is making inquiries. We should have an answer in a few days.” “What do you mean, ‘Father says’? Have you not looked yourself?” “It is inconvenient for me to appear. How much money have you brought?” “Ten thousand taels.” “Where is it?” Wang patted his money pouch. “Fukang Bank notes.” “And the map?” “Of course I brought that too. But do not tell your parents yet. Wait until the place is found.” Jiaju was startled. “What am I to say if they ask?” “That I left it in Shanghai.” “Would that not be dishonest? We are depending on them. If we are not sincere ourselves, how can we expect sincerity in return?” Wang considered. “I have an idea.”

But he did not explain it. Unable to contain himself, Jiaju urged, “What idea? Tell me, so we can discuss it.” “We must guard against others. We are strangers here, with no local connections. They could easily take advantage of us, so we must find a way to protect ourselves. I think I had better stay at an inn. It will be easier to do what is necessary.” “We must guard against others. We are strangers here, with no local connections. They could easily take advantage of us, so we must find a way to protect ourselves. I think I had better stay at an inn. It will be easier to do what is necessary.” “Do what?” “Never mind. Just find some excuse that will let me stay at an inn.” “That is easy.” Jiaju called his adoptive parents back and said that two friends were coming from Shanghai to see Wang. It would be inconvenient to meet them in the house, so he wished to spend a few days at an inn, then return. It never occurred to the couple that a guest who had only just arrived already suspected them. They agreed at once and found him a small inn called Maoxing on East Street. Once he was settled, they welcomed him with a meal at their home that evening. They talked of their backgrounds and experiences, and nothing else.

When the meal ended in the second watch, Mrs Zhu brought out a set of bedding, neither new nor shabby, but washed perfectly clean. “Jiaju, the bedding at inns is dirty. Take this and see your friend there.” Beaming with pride, the honest, simple Jiaju said to Wang, “You see how thoughtful my mother is!” In fact, the gesture only increased Wang’s wariness. At Maoxing Inn he told Jiaju, “Sit for a while, then go home. Your mother is very calculating. Do not give her cause for suspicion.” “She would never suspect anything. She is even arranging a marriage for me, to a niece from her own family.” Wang smiled faintly. “Let us get rich first.” He left unspoken another thought: Do not fall for a beauty trap. “Now, to business,” Jiaju said. “What was it you wanted to do?” Wang hesitated. He was not entirely confident of Jiaju either and wondered whether to conceal his secret plan from him as well. “Well? Why do you say nothing?” “It is not that I will not speak. We come from the same village and have shared hardship. You might say that life, death, good fortune, and disaster bind us together as brothers. But now you seem closer to your adoptive parents than to me.” “You are wrong. My parents are yours too. We shall all make our fortunes together. Do not be suspicious.” Wang could not immediately refute him, but one thing was clear. Further persuasion might make Jiaju think he was trying to drive a wedge between him and his parents. If their friendship cracked before the undertaking began, and Jiaju sided with the couple, Wang would be isolated in unfamiliar surroundings and certain to lose.

So he put on a look of understanding. “You are right. Your parents are mine too. I shall talk to your father tomorrow. Have you brought your half of the map?” “No. It is too important. Now that I have a home, naturally I keep it there. Did you want to see it now?” “No, no. My original plan was to make a false map. To cut the zigzag edge so that it fitted perfectly, without the least flaw, I would have had to lay your half over it and trim very carefully. But there is no need now.” “What an ingenious idea!” Believing that Wang had accepted his advice and regained confidence in the couple, Jiaju was delighted. “Stay a while, and you will see. My parents really are good people.” “I know.” “I must go. I shall come for you tomorrow morning and take you home for lunch.” “Good. Until tomorrow.” Wang caught his arm. “I did misunderstand the old couple, but no longer. Whatever we have said between ourselves, do not tell them, or I shall feel too embarrassed to stay.” “I understand, I understand.” Jiaju nodded repeatedly. “I am not a three-year-old with no sense of what matters.”

After Jiaju left, Wang Peili went to the office, borrowed a writing brush and inkstone from the clerk, and shut himself in to “do what was necessary.” From his luggage he took a Directory of Officials and removed a sheet tucked between its pages. Spread on the table, it was his half of the map. He trimmed the oil-lamp wick and bent over it. Three elongated rectangles stood side by side, with another, more nearly square block lying crosswise above them. At the center of that block was a little square the size of a die, with a round dot marking the treasure. After studying it for some time, he began to grind ink. He tested the shade on scrap paper until it matched the original, then added four more die-sized squares at arbitrary points, placing a dot in each. Looking again, he could still distinguish the new ink from the old. A close inspection would give him away. He thought for a moment, laid the map face down on the floor, and placed an overturned stool on top to press it down. Then he bolted the door and went to sleep. First thing next morning he examined the half-map. It was covered in dust. He carefully blew and brushed away the loose particles, leaving the difference between old and new ink completely obscured. Pleased with himself, he felt that these decoys would fool even Jiaju. He could now show the map openly.

He put it away and, when Jiaju arrived, went with him to a nearby teahouse to wash and have breakfast. “Let us discuss things,” Jiaju said. “When I got back last night, Father asked whether you had brought money. I said yes. He said he had found a place that looked right. Without money there was no point in approaching the owner; with it, he could begin. Whether we bought it or took it in pledge, we could conclude the deal at once if the price was right.” “Did he ask how much I had?” “No.” Wang nodded, paused, and said, “We must not begrudge small expenses. I should like to give him two hundred taels as an introductory gift. Does that seem about right?” “Yes.” “Where is Fukang Bank? My notes are all for a thousand taels. I must change one into smaller notes.” “I shall ask.” They learned from a tea waiter that the bank was on Qinghefang Street, paid their bill, and set off on foot. It was a considerable distance from East Street, and finding it took some effort. After changing the note, they bought four kinds of food as gifts and hired sedan chairs back to the inn. “Would it not be easier to go straight to Father’s?” Jiaju asked. “Did you not say he might ask about the map? I had better bring it and see how things develop.” “Yes, that is better.” Back at the inn, Wang took the Directory of Officials, and they went together to the Zhus’.

It was just time for the noon gun. Mrs Zhu had stewed a plump chicken and was waiting for them. “Uncle Zhu, Auntie Zhu,” Wang said, setting the gifts on the table and offering a red envelope from Fukang with both hands, “I came in such a hurry that I brought nothing for you. I can only offer money instead.” “That will never do!” Zhu Baoru pushed it away. “You have bought these foods, and we will say no more about them. But no money. I have done nothing to deserve it.” “Please do not stand on ceremony. I shall be troubling you often. Jiaju’s parents are my elders too. If you refuse this small token from a junior, I shall feel uneasy.” Jiaju added his persuasion. Zhu Baoru finally accepted and, opening it at a discreet moment, found a note for two hundred taels. He was delighted. Wang evidently had plenty of money. Even if they found no treasure, there would be ways to squeeze more out of him.

Over lunch they turned to business. “I have found a place that looks very like it. After the meal I shall take you to see it. Have you brought your half of the map?” “Yes. Would you like to look?” “No hurry. We shall look after lunch.” When they had eaten and drunk their fill, Mrs Zhu brought strong Longjing tea to clear their heads and aid digestion. As they returned to the matter, Wang asked Jiaju to fetch his half of the map and took his own from the Directory of Officials. They laid the pieces on the square table and slowly fitted the jagged edges together. The fit was exact; the ink matched perfectly. They were unmistakably two halves of the same sheet. Wang had deliberately staged this display, using the genuine to conceal the false. It was not merely to deceive Zhu Baoru. Above all, he wanted to test Jiaju’s memory. They had divided the map hurriedly, without giving Jiaju a close look. Yet even a glance might have left him with the memory of a single die-sized square. If he noticed the additions, he would realize that Wang had tampered with the map to protect them against Zhu Baoru. Surely he would keep that to himself, leaving them to discuss it afterward. If he remembered nothing, matters would be easier still. Wang watched him closely. To his slight surprise, Jiaju looked as bewildered as his adoptive father: neither understood what he saw.

“The little squares must mark buried treasure,” Zhu Baoru said. “Why are there so many? Was there too much to bury in one place?” “That is possible,” Wang replied. “No,” Jiaju interrupted. “I know there was only one large wooden chest.” Wang’s heart gave a jump. His deception was close to exposure, but he was quick-witted and adapted without changing expression. “Then only one of the sites can be genuine. The others must be decoys.” “Exactly!” Zhu Baoru agreed completely. “I once heard a storyteller recount the Romance of the Three Kingdoms. Cao Cao had seventy-three false tombs. Perhaps they copied that idea in case the map was lost.” Wang nodded and glanced at Jiaju, who still looked puzzled and seemed to be thinking. Wang began to fear that his cleverness might backfire. He was also deeply annoyed with Jiaju, who was as stupid as a block of wood and had no notion of cooperation. “Sometimes in Shanghai I would take the map out and wonder about it too. I regretted not asking at the time. Still, as long as the location is right, it does not matter whether only one spot is genuine or the treasure was divided. At worst it means a little extra work. The things cannot get away.” Jiaju seemed reassured. “Father, let us see the house.” “Very well. Come along.”

As they put the map away and prepared to leave, Mrs Zhu appeared in the hall. “It is windy today,” she told her husband. “Come and put on another layer.” “I am quite warm enough.” He plainly had not understood her intention. “Put on a riding jacket. I have taken it out already.” At this second insistence he realized that she wanted a word and followed her. In the bedroom, while looking down to fasten his jacket, she whispered, “Their story does not quite fit. Could Wang really be unaware that only one chest was buried?” Her words woke him as if from a dream. He suddenly understood the map’s trickery and changed his plan. On reaching the lane where the jinshi lived, he pointed to the neighboring house. “There. The Longhairs used that house as their quarters. That may be the one.” “What is the owner’s surname?” “Wang, I hear,” Zhu Baoru invented. Wang Peili said nothing. He looked back toward the Temple of Lord Guan, gave Jiaju a meaningful glance, and walked slowly over at his ordinary pace, plainly measuring the distance. “Let us talk at home,” Zhu Baoru whispered. “People are beginning to notice us.” The two men dared not even look up and followed him away. Since the capture of Jinling, the court had announced that those forced to join the Taiping ranks would not be prosecuted. Nevertheless, local militia organizations responsible for investigating suspicious persons often harassed them. A single disagreement could lead to their being taken to the militia office for punishment. It was therefore natural that talk of attracting attention alarmed Jiaju and Wang.

After supper at home, Jiaju escorted Wang back to the inn. Zhu Baoru told his wife, “Luckily you warned me. I did not point out Mr Yan’s house, so they cannot approach him behind our backs.” “Wang is dishonest. We really must guard against him. I did not see the map. What was on it?” Zhu traced it on the table with a finger. “Three oblongs in a row, with another lying crosswise above them. It is Yan’s house, beyond doubt.” “Was that written on it?” “Of course not. If it were, why would we have had to search?” “Then how can you be sure?” “I have seen the place. Its three successive ranges of buildings are the three oblongs. The one above is where Old Mr Yan grew his peonies.” “Ah, yes. That does sound right. But I heard you talking as though there were several treasure sites. Why did Jiaju say there was only one chest?” “That proves what you said: Wang is dishonest. There is only one hiding place, and I know where it is.” “Where?” “The peony bed. Why? Because all the other squares are inside buildings. The main hall has polished blue-brick paving; the second and third halls have wooden floors. Digging those up would be troublesome and could not possibly leave no trace. It makes no sense. That leaves only the open-air flower bed.” “Then why mark several sites?” “Decoys.” “Who added them?” “Possibly Wang himself.” She considered. “Say nothing for now. I shall ask Jiaju.” “Ask him? He would tell Wang, and everything would be ruined.” “I understand that perfectly. Leave it to me. I have my own way.”

Meanwhile, Jiaju and Wang were discussing the map at the inn. Jiaju remembered the lower half as relatively plain, without all those little squares. Wang admitted altering it and reproached him for his lack of alertness. “I told you that we must guard against people. You should have understood. If something seemed wrong, you could have kept it to yourself until we could talk. Why speak out at once and make it look as though we were at odds?” Jiaju himself felt that he had spoken and acted without enough thought, so he accepted the rebuke in silence. But he had to learn the truth. “Then which is the real spot?” This experience had convinced Wang that Jiaju was too honest. Believing the saying that honesty was another name for uselessness, he decided to hold something back. He casually pointed to a small square at the upper end of the first oblong. “There.” “There?” Jiaju frowned. “What sort of place is that?” “You ask me; whom am I to ask? The house we saw today seems about right. I measured the distance by pacing, and it faces west. Once we get inside, we shall understand. What your father must now do is find a way to let me inspect it. When we are certain it is right, we can discuss the next step.” “Very well. I shall tell him when I get back.”

Next morning Zhu Baoru went out early, leaving Jiaju no chance to speak. His adoptive mother, however, raised the matter herself. “Jiaju, I heard you discussing the map yesterday. Some of it did not seem reasonable.” “Yes,” he answered cautiously. “Which part did you mean?” “If someone entrusted such an important matter to you both, he should have explained it clearly and shown you exactly where the treasure was. Now it seems that having the map is no better than having none. Would you not agree?” “That… that was because everything happened so hurriedly.” “And another thing…” She broke off, then shook her head. “Never mind.” Uneasy, he urged, “Mother, please say whatever is on your mind.” “It would put you in a difficult position. Better leave it unsaid.” “What could put me in a difficult position? I really cannot imagine.” “Can you not?” “Truly.” “Very well. I shall tell you. If you find it difficult, you need not answer.” “Of course I shall answer truthfully if you ask me.” “I know you are honest.” The implication was that Wang was not. Jiaju understood but pretended not to. Fortunately, it was not a question, so he could remain silent. “Was it one chest that was buried?” “Yes.” “How can one chest be buried in several places?” Now he did feel the difficulty. Yet he knew he must not hesitate, or even the truth would fail to inspire confidence. He answered quickly, “Of course it cannot. Wang and I talked for a long time last night. I think there is only one hiding place, but we must inspect the house to learn which. What Peili wants Father to arrange is a chance for us to look inside.”

“That may be difficult unless we buy the house first.” “How much would it cost?” “We must inquire. I imagine two or three thousand taels.” “We have that much. I shall ask Peili to set it aside and give it to Father.” “There is no need yet.” Suddenly she asked, “Jiaju, do you really want to marry and establish a home?” “Of course. I must ask you to help me.” “Leave it to me, provided you have no objection to Aizhu.” Aizhu was her niece, now twenty-five. She had married at twenty, lost her husband to illness the following year, and remained a widow. By “no objection,” Mrs Zhu meant no objection to marrying a woman who had been married before. Jiaju misunderstood. “If I objected to a woman like Miss Aizhu, I should truly have eyes and no sight.” Aizhu was better than ordinarily good-looking. From their first meeting he had kept stealing glances at her, and afterward he could not praise her enough. Mrs Zhu could be certain of success if she used her niece to win him over. But her widowhood must be made clear. She remembered telling him, though perhaps so casually that he had missed the point. Now she must explain again. “I do not mean her looks. I mean that she has been married.” “I know, I know. You told me. Do not worry about that. I do not mind. But does she have any children?” “You can be easy on that score too. She will not bring a child from the earlier marriage. She has never had one.”

“All the better. Mother, do you know anyone suitable for Peili as well?” “Oh? Is he unmarried too?” “He was married to a girl brought up in his family as his future wife, but they do not get on. That is why he will not return to Jiangxi.” “Since he already has a wife at home, how could I arrange another marriage? I would not do anything so damaging to my store of virtue.” She had disposed of the request with a single easy sentence, but Jiaju would not give up. “What if he spent some money to divorce that wife and send her back to her parents?” “We can discuss it when that happens. If you want to marry, you ought to buy a house. Your father will ask someone to approach the owner, Wang, today. If he is willing to sell, do you have the money ready?” “Yes. Wang and I have a fund that we agreed not to touch, and he keeps it. We can use it now to buy the house.”

“Will you buy it jointly, then, or on your own?” “Jointly, of course.” “That may not be wise,” she reminded him. “You are buying a home for yourself. Is he to live with you?” Jiaju thought. “Perhaps I shall buy another place separately. The treasure house must be bought by both of us. Otherwise it would hardly be fair.” “That is true.” She paused. “But if you buy one together and another on your own, will he not become suspicious?” “Suspicious of what?” “That the one you buy alone is the real treasure house.” “So long as my house is nowhere near Jindong Bridge or Wan’an Bridge, and the two are far apart, there will be no reason for suspicion.” Only then did Mrs Zhu realize that greed had clouded her judgment and nearly betrayed her. Her plan was for the two men to buy the supposed Wang house that her husband had falsely identified, while Jiaju alone bought or took in pledge the jinshi’s house. The two properties would then be close together, one genuine and one false treasure site, without arousing suspicion. Fortunately, Jiaju had never imagined that she already had Yan’s house in mind, so he failed to penetrate the secret. Even so, it had been a narrow escape. The more one spoke, the more likely one was to blunder. She dropped the subject. That night the couple whispered in bed for much of the night and devised a linked series of stratagems: first, conjure something out of nothing; second, kill with a borrowed knife; third, destroy the bridge after crossing it. They hurried to make their preparations and put the schemes into effect one after another.

The next afternoon Zhu Baoru came home just as Wang arrived for supper. “I have found a way in,” he announced cheerfully. “The owner is not called Wang after all, but Liu. A property-broker friend of mine is a distant relative of the family. I have asked him to inquire.” Hangzhou people called a house broker a “tile-shaker.” This man, surnamed Sun and fourth among his brothers, was fluent, persuasive, and very amiable. Zhu introduced them, and they got on well. But when buying the Liu house came up, Sun Four shook his head vigorously. “No, no. A bad idea.” “Why?” Jiaju asked. “Old Zhu and I are old friends. I could never harm you. The Liu house is unclean.” “Unclean? Is there a fox spirit?” “A fox spirit would be harmless enough. Offer four boiled eggs and two liang of spirits on the second and sixteenth days of the month, and all would be well.” He lowered his voice. “Many people died there when the Longhairs used it as their quarters. It is often haunted.” This only strengthened Wang’s confidence. “Fourth Master Sun, I have never believed in ghosts.” “Why trouble yourselves? There are plenty of good houses now. No one wants the Liu property, but the moment you ask, the owner will treat it as a rare treasure. The price is not low either. It is not worth it.”

With the conversation at an impasse, Wang looked to Zhu Baoru for help. Zhu spoke calmly. “Here is the situation. My adopted son and his good friend want to settle in Hangzhou. They thought of buying the Liu house together because it is near mine. They are strangers and do not know the local circumstances. I do. I told them the place was unclean, but they said they could pull it down and rebuild. That would settle the matter.” Turning to the two men, he added, “When you rebuild, you can obtain a house-protecting talisman from Celestial Master Zhang at Mount Longhu. That will make it doubly safe.” “Yes, yes,” Jiaju said. “I know a ritual master at Shangqing Palace on Mount Longhu. We can invite him to perform the rites.” “Brother Sun, you have heard them. Please go ahead.” “With Celestial Master Zhang guaranteeing it, there should be no trouble. Very well. I shall bring an answer in three days.”

On the third day the answer arrived. The situation was rather complicated. The house had been rented separately to three households whose leases had not expired. To make them leave would require moving allowances, so the owner offered two alternatives. “The house is four thousand taels. If you pay two hundred to each household for moving, making forty-six hundred in all, the deed can be signed and possession transferred at once. If you do not pay moving allowances, you must wait three months, until the leases expire and the house can be recovered.” Zhu Baoru continued, “Naturally, you would not pay the whole price at once. First an earnest payment; the rest would be deposited at Fukang and released after possession was transferred. What do you think?” “What do you think, Father?” Jiaju asked. “Could the price be reduced?” “Certainly that can be discussed. Let us settle the method of payment first. I favor the second arrangement. Waiting three months saves six hundred taels, no small sum.” “What if the tenants refuse to leave?” Wang asked. “I asked Sun that too. He says they will leave, because the owner will let them stay rent-free for those three months, which amounts to a moving allowance. We can also put penalties in the contract: if possession is delayed, the earnest money must be returned, with an additional penalty. That will make it completely safe.” “In that case, let us pay the earnest money first and the balance when possession is delivered. Why must we deposit the balance at the bank?”

“There is a reason…” The owner, Liu, was said to be in the rice trade and expanding his business. He intended to borrow from Fukang against the house, which would no longer be possible if he sold it. But matters would be different if the bank knew that he had a source of repayment. “Our deposit at Fukang would in effect guarantee him,” Zhu Baoru explained. “The bank would know the loan would not turn into a bad debt, and naturally it would lend. I have been thinking: the interest on the deposit would belong to you, so you lose nothing. It would also give Sun, as intermediary, a better chance to bargain the price down. Since you have entrusted this to me, I must consider every side and see that you do not lose in any way.” “Yes, yes.” Wang found the reasoning sound and turned to Jiaju. “Shall we do that?” “Yes. Father, please try to get the price down further.” “Very well. I shall speak to Sun now and invite him to supper tonight. You can discuss it face to face.”

Zhu Baoru went out at once. His wife took a basket to the market to prepare for their guests, leaving Jiaju to watch the house. This gave him the chance to say what had been on his mind for days. He began with his proposed marriage and honestly recounted his request that his adoptive mother find a wife for Wang as well. “We are brothers who have shared hardship. I have always wanted us to stay together. Having served with the Longhairs, we would find it hard to live back home. Hangzhou is a fine place. Nothing could be better than making our fortunes and settling here. Leave finding you another wife to me. I shall see it done.” His words were persuasive, and Mrs Zhu’s solicitous care over recent days had already softened Wang’s opinion. He therefore joined enthusiastically in a serious discussion of settling in Hangzhou. “So many people died in the Liu house, and it is haunted. It is an unlucky place; we cannot live there,” Jiaju said. “Once we have dug up the treasure, we shall not care. We could sell it cheaply. What do you think?” “Exactly. Better buy another house to live in than rebuild that one.” “That is just what I mean!” Jiaju abruptly brought the conversation to his point. “I must establish my home first, then I can help you establish yours. I want to buy a house now, or take one in pledge. What do you think?” “It is a good thing. Why should I object?” “Good!” Jiaju was delighted. “That is what I call a brother through hardship.” Wang nodded, thought a while, and asked how much he needed. “For now I must make do with somewhere just large enough for two. Here is my idea: we set aside a sum solely for our ‘proper business,’ then divide the rest and deposit it separately for our own use. Of course, if either is short, he can borrow from the other. That can always be discussed.”

After consideration, Wang agreed. Of the ten thousand taels he had brought, ninety-five hundred remained. They set aside forty-five hundred as their common fund, opening an account that required two seals. The remaining five thousand was divided equally for their individual use. With this settled, both felt that they could now wait at ease. Over wine with Sun Four that evening, Wang bargained calmly. As intermediary, Sun courteously promised to do his utmost with the owner and secure the lowest possible price. In such an atmosphere they naturally got on well and parted in high spirits. After Sun left and Wang returned to the inn, Jiaju told his adoptive parents the whole arrangement. Armed with that information, Zhu Baoru privately pursued his own business. Claiming an official errand to Suzhou, he actually went to Shanghai and used Hu Xueyan’s name to obtain a meeting with the jinshi and discuss taking his house in pledge. Yan readily agreed and gave him a letter directing a nephew in Hangzhou to settle the details. The round trip took half a month. Meanwhile, the purchase of the Liu house had also been agreed at thirty-four hundred taels. The four hundred was to be paid as earnest money; the remaining three thousand was put in a Fukang passbook account named Zhupei. A new seal was cut for Wang to keep, while Jiaju held the passbook. A draft agreement was ready: possession within three months; a penalty of ten taels for each day of delay; and, if the delay exceeded a month, cancellation of the contract and repayment of twice the earnest money.

“Father,” Jiaju said, “we have only been waiting for you to return before signing the contract. The other side is pressing us. Shall we do it tomorrow?” “No hurry. The contract must be examined carefully, and we must choose an auspicious day to sign.” In fact, it was time for the second of the three linked stratagems. Zhu Baoru had only just returned and needed to make his arrangements. After four more days of delay, the contract was finally signed on a day deemed favorable for deeds and property purchases. Wang Peili had also decided to move into the Zhu home. But on the day just before he was to move, a patrol from the militia bureau arrested him. Under questioning at their headquarters, he named Jiaju and Zhu Baoru. The adoptive father and son were consequently arrested as well.
